## AI Deep Dive: Michael Saylor

From the Lex archive, episode two seventy-six, April of twenty twenty-two. Lex Fridman sits down with Michael Saylor, CEO of MicroStrategy, MIT graduate, and one of the loudest Bitcoin believers on the planet. The setting is charming: a handmade nineteenth century model of a seventeenth century Dutch sailing ship, sitting right there on the table.

Saylor opens with grades. Humanity gets a B minus in engineering, and, in his words, a D minus on economics. His complaint is that economists reduce a messy world to a single number. Inflation, he says, isn't a scalar. It's a vector with many dimensions. Rockets and airfoils aren't designed with one number, so why economies?

That leads to his big idea: asset inflation. Official inflation tracks a government basket of consumer goods. But in twenty twenty, Saylor says, interest rates fell to near zero, and the price of bonds, stocks, and houses jumped. He notes a typical house was up about nineteen percent in a year, while the official figure was seven point nine percent. If you're twenty-two and saving for a first home, he says, your inflation rate is nineteen percent. He also points to a house deed from nineteen thirty at a hundred thousand dollars, now estimated at thirty point five million, which works out to about six and a half percent a year for ninety-two years. His claim is that money supply has grown around seven percent a year, far above the official story.

Second idea: digital energy. Saylor sees Bitcoin as a way to launch a billion dollar block of pure value into cyberspace and move it at the speed of light. He argues Bitcoin is ethical because it's property, like corn, land, or gold, rather than a security controlled by a small team. No board, no CEO, no one to cheat you.

Third, his view of risk. After thirteen years, he says, the engineering risk, the risk of a ban, and the risk of a better copy have all faded. He calls August twenty twenty, when MicroStrategy bought two hundred fifty million dollars of Bitcoin, year one of institutional adoption.

A line that sticks. Saylor says, quote, you're literally bleeding the free market to death, end quote, comparing inflation to the well-meaning doctors who bled George Washington.

How has it aged? Mixed, and you should judge. Inflation did surge past seven percent soon after this conversation, which fits his warning. But he said it in April of twenty twenty-two, just before a long crypto slump, so the idea that the risk had never been lower got stress-tested hard. Why does it matter for an AI briefing? Because the sharpest part of the talk is the method: refuse the single number, and model the whole system. That's good advice for evaluating anything, including AI benchmarks.

## Acquired: Lucasfilm

A note up front. This early Acquired episode, number six, from January of twenty sixteen, has no published transcript. So we'll tell the story of the deal itself, from well-established public facts, rather than putting words in Ben and David's mouths. The show notes tell us only that they covered Disney's twenty twelve acquisition of Lucasfilm, riding the wave of The Force Awakens, and that they discussed Walt Disney's original flywheel diagram. That flywheel is a great place to start, and we'll come back to it.

Let's begin with a young filmmaker named George Lucas. He grew up in Modesto, California, loved cars and racing, and nearly died in a crash before graduating high school. That changed his path. He went to film school at the University of Southern California, where he met future collaborators. His first feature, THX eleven thirty-eight, came out in nineteen seventy-one. Then in nineteen seventy-three came American Graffiti, made on a small budget and a huge hit. Lucas earned real money from it, and, more important, leverage.

He used that leverage on a space fantasy that almost nobody believed in. Twentieth Century Fox backed it, with executive Alan Ladd Junior taking the risk. Here's the twist that matters for our story. In his deal, Lucas took a modest director's fee and, in exchange, kept the sequel rights and the merchandising rights. Fox gave away the toys because toys from movies weren't thought to be worth much. Star Wars arrived in May of nineteen seventy-seven with a budget of around eleven million dollars. It went on to earn hundreds of millions at the box office. The toys, made by Kenner, became one of the most successful merchandising lines in history. Kenner famously sold empty boxes with a certificate in the first Christmas, because the figures weren't ready. Kids took the IOU. That's demand.

Meanwhile Lucas built a company. Lucasfilm was based in Northern California, far from Hollywood, which was the point. He wanted independence. In nineteen seventy-five, he founded Industrial Light and Magic, the visual effects house, because the effects he needed didn't exist. He founded Skywalker Sound, and the THX audio standard, which you, Scott, know very well from a career in audio and video. He built Skywalker Ranch. For the sequel, The Empire Strikes Back in nineteen eighty, Lucas financed the film himself, and it paid off. Return of the Jedi followed in nineteen eighty-three. Alongside came Indiana Jones, which began with Raiders of the Lost Ark in nineteen eighty-one, a collaboration with Steven Spielberg.

There's another famous branch of Lucasfilm. The company had a computer graphics division. In nineteen eighty-six, Lucas, who was going through an expensive divorce, sold it to Steve Jobs for ten million dollars. It became Pixar. Hold that thought, because the buyer of Lucasfilm will end up owning both halves.

After Return of the Jedi, Lucas stepped back from Star Wars for years. Then he returned with the prequel trilogy in nineteen ninety-nine, two thousand two, and two thousand five. Reception was mixed, to put it gently. Still, those films made money, along with a vast universe of games, books, comics, and the animated series The Clone Wars. By the late two thousands Lucas was in his sixties, and he'd said publicly that he was ready to retire. He also had no obvious successor in the family business.

Now to the buyer. In two thousand five, Bob Iger became CEO of Disney. Iger's strategy was simple and bold. Buy great franchises, and let Disney's machinery amplify them. First, in two thousand six, Disney bought Pixar for about seven point four billion dollars, bringing Steve Jobs onto the board as its largest shareholder. Then in two thousand nine, Marvel, for about four billion dollars. Marvel was the proof that the strategy worked, because the Marvel films, tied together as one universe, became a juggernaut.

And that brings us to the flywheel. Walt Disney drew a famous diagram showing how his businesses fed each other. A film creates characters. The characters become toys and books and music. They become rides in a park. The park creates new fans who see the next film. Everything feeds everything else. Disney had scale in every spoke: studios, television, consumer products, theme parks, cruise ships. What Disney lacked was another franchise as big as the ones it had. Star Wars was the biggest unclaimed one on earth.

According to Iger's later account, he raised the idea with Lucas in twenty eleven, when Lucas was thinking about what would happen to his creation. Lucas wanted a steward. He chose Kathleen Kennedy, the longtime producer, who had worked with Spielberg. Kennedy became president of Lucasfilm and was named Lucas's successor, with Lucas staying on as a creative consultant. On October thirtieth, twenty twelve, Disney announced it was buying Lucasfilm for about four point oh five billion dollars, roughly half in cash and half in Disney stock. Lucas ended up owning a bit over two percent of Disney, and he said he would give much of the proceeds to education. Disney also announced it planned a new Star Wars film every few years, starting with Episode Seven in twenty fifteen.

Notice the price. Four billion dollars, close to what Marvel cost. Skeptics asked if Disney was overpaying. The company's argument was that it wasn't just buying film. It was buying characters, worlds, a library, ILM, Skywalker Sound, a licensing business, and decades of fan devotion. Disney said it expected the deal to pay off within a few years. And the best part is that Disney already knew the territory, since Star Wars had been part of its parks through licensing for years.

There were risks, and let's name them. First, the fans. Star Wars fans are among the most passionate and the most critical anywhere, and the prequels had shown what happens when expectations aren't met. Second, the creative risk of continuing a story whose author has walked away. Third, the danger of oversaturating the market. Disney needed to treat Star Wars as a treasured asset, not as a vending machine.

And the early results were spectacular. Remember, the episode came out in January of twenty sixteen, only weeks after The Force Awakens opened in December of twenty fifteen. That film grossed more than two billion dollars worldwide, becoming one of the biggest of all time. It put Disney's acquisition on a path to repayment almost immediately, before counting toys, games, and television.

What came next, with the benefit of hindsight? Rogue One, in twenty sixteen, grossed over a billion dollars. The Last Jedi, in twenty seventeen, did very well but divided fans. Then Solo, in twenty eighteen, became the first Star Wars movie to lose money at the box office, a sign of that oversaturation risk. The Rise of Skywalker, in twenty nineteen, closed the main saga with over a billion dollars but also mixed reviews.

But the real payoffs came through other spokes of the flywheel. The Mandalorian debuted in twenty nineteen as a flagship series for the new Disney Plus streaming service. It gave Star Wars a fresh home in television, followed by more series. And in twenty nineteen, Star Wars Galaxy's Edge opened at Disneyland and at Walt Disney World, immersive lands that the franchise's fans had wanted for forty years. Add the merchandising, the video games, and the consumer products, and you can see the diagram at work.

Of course, the story isn't all success. In recent years the franchise has had a slower film pipeline, repeatedly delayed projects, and leadership changes at Lucasfilm. Critics argue that Disney leaned too hard on nostalgia, and that fans grew tired of quantity over quality. That's the cost of a flywheel: when it spins fast, it needs constant fuel.

Let's slow down on the Star Wars origin, because the near-misses are the best part. Lucas had a hard time selling the idea. United Artists passed. Universal passed. Fox said yes mostly because of Alan Ladd Junior's faith in Lucas after American Graffiti. The shoot in Tunisia and at Elstree Studios in England was troubled. The crew didn't understand what they were making. Equipment broke, the weather turned, and the budget got tight. Lucas worked himself to exhaustion and was hospitalized with chest pains after the shoot. Fox executives weren't sure they had anything, and Lucas himself expected a modest result. Then the first screenings, and the lines around the block, told a different story. Legend has it that Lucas spent opening weekend in Hawaii to avoid the stress, building a sandcastle on the beach with Steven Spielberg while the calls came in about record crowds. That's also where they sketched out the idea that became Indiana Jones.

Now think about what that merchandising decision meant. For decades, Hollywood treated licensing as a side hustle. Lucas treated it as a second business. The profits funded his independence. He used them to build Skywalker Ranch, to finance The Empire Strikes Back, and to give Lucasfilm the freedom to take risks without asking a studio for permission. Independence was the founding idea of the company, and the merchandising rights were what paid for it. In the language of the Disney flywheel, Lucas had found the spoke that turned the wheel faster than the film itself.

There's another detail that matters to the purchase. Lucasfilm wasn't only Star Wars and Indiana Jones. It also housed LucasArts, the games business, which made classics in the nineteen nineties. And it housed ILM, which had won Academy Awards for visual effects and worked on films far beyond Lucas's own. ILM was the company the rest of Hollywood called when it wanted the impossible. Skywalker Sound did the same for audio. When Disney bought the company, it bought a toolbox as well as a library. For a company that already owned Pixar's technology and Marvel's franchises, adding the world's most famous effects house was another way to keep the whole machine in-house.

What about the deal mechanics? The stock component mattered. By taking Disney shares, Lucas tied himself to the success of the combined company, rather than walking away with cash. That's good alignment. It also meant Disney could preserve cash and still do the deal at a headline price of about four billion dollars. Disney said it would repurchase shares to offset the dilution from issuing stock. That's a standard move, but it signals confidence.

There was also the matter of timing. In twenty twelve, Disney's stock was healthy, the Marvel acquisition had been a clear win, and The Avengers had come out earlier that year and grossed over one and a half billion dollars. Iger had momentum, board support, and the credibility to say, we've done this before. Lucas, for his part, had watched Marvel and Pixar flourish under Disney without losing their identity. That mattered to him. He didn't want to sell to a buyer who would bury the brand. Disney's record of letting Pixar stay Pixar, and Marvel stay Marvel, was part of the pitch.

Let's talk about the question that hung over the sale: what would Disney actually do with the stories? Lucas had written treatments for more Star Wars films, and it's been widely reported that Disney chose not to follow them. That was a sensitive point between creator and buyer, and Lucas later spoke about feeling that the new films were taken in a different direction. It's the unavoidable tradeoff of selling a beloved world. Once the keys change hands, so does the story. Yet the fan reaction to The Force Awakens, with its mix of the old cast and new faces, showed that audiences were ready to go along.

The economics tell the story too. Before the deal, Star Wars merchandise had already generated billions of dollars in retail sales over its lifetime. In the years after, the toy business roared back. A single movie can drive a whole holiday season of sales, and the figures, costumes, and Lego kits keep moving between films. The Force Awakens broke records for merchandise sales. Then came the games, from mobile titles to big console releases, and a stream of books and comics. Disney had spent about four billion dollars. Within a handful of years, the franchise had generated many times that in revenue across every line of business.

Finally, a note about brand management, since you'll appreciate the operator's angle. Disney took the whole Star Wars universe and decided what was canon and what wasn't. In twenty fourteen, it reclassified the old Expanded Universe novels and comics as Legends, and started fresh with a new official timeline. That was a controversial call, but it gave the writers freedom and gave newcomers a clean entry point. Operations people know this one well: sometimes you retire a legacy system to move faster, even if some users love it.

So, what lessons can an operator take from this deal? Here are three.

One. Buy the thing you can multiply. Disney didn't buy Lucasfilm to run it as it was. It bought a franchise it could run through every channel it already owned. The value was in the combination, not the standalone asset.

Two. Rights are the whole game. Lucas won because he held onto the sequel and merchandising rights when nobody thought they mattered. Decades later, the same rights were worth billions to him and then to Disney. When you negotiate, ask what everyone else is undervaluing.

Three. Succession is a product decision. Lucas didn't just sell. He chose a steward, set a plan, and handed over creative direction. Transfers go wrong when no one owns the story. Iger's own habit of building relationships with founders first, before making an offer, was the soft skill that closed the deal.

And a bonus, for the audio-video crowd. Look at how much of this story is about technology serving a story. ILM built effects to tell a tale. Skywalker Sound and THX made the theatre experience feel premium. Lucas understood that audiences pay for how it feels. You know a thing or two about that.

One last point on the price. Four billion dollars for the keys to a galaxy sounds like a lot until you divide it out. Spread across the franchise's films, shows, parks, and toy aisles, it looks like one of the smartest purchases of Iger's tenure. Disney also kept buying, taking over much of Fox's film and television assets in twenty nineteen, which, by a lovely irony, included the very studio that gave away Star Wars toys in nineteen seventy-seven.

That's Lucasfilm: a filmmaker with a stubborn streak, a company that built its own tools, and a buyer with a flywheel. The best sequel rights Fox ever gave away, and the best deal Disney ever signed.
