## Acquired: Acompli, Sunrise, and Wunderlist

A quick note up front. This early Acquired episode, number eight, has no published transcript. So I'm telling you the story of the deal itself, from well-established public facts, and I won't put words in Ben and David's mouths.

What we do know from the show notes is the setup. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal sat down with Kurt DelBene, then Microsoft's executive vice president of corporate strategy and planning. The topic was three acquisitions by Microsoft: Acompli, Sunrise and Wunderlist. They covered how Outlook Mobile came to be, how to decide whether to build or buy, how to preserve a culture, and how to judge an acquisition when the answer isn't revenue at all. They also touched on the origin of Outlook on the PC, which was an internal project called Ren, led by Brian MacDonald.

To understand why those three deals mattered, you have to go back a few years. In the early twenty-tens, Microsoft was the king of the desktop and had a mobile problem. The iPhone and Android had changed how people worked. Office, the crown jewel, lived mostly on Windows PCs. Meanwhile, the best work tools on phones came from startups. And Microsoft's own phone platform was a distant third.

Then, in February of twenty fourteen, Satya Nadella became chief executive. His message was a mantra. Microsoft would be mobile-first and cloud-first. And that meant something radical. Office had to work well on devices Microsoft did not own, including the iPhone and Android. Within weeks, Microsoft released Office for iPad. It was a signal that the company would go where its customers already were.

That's the strategy.

Let's set the stage with the man at the center of the episode. Kurt DelBene spent years inside Microsoft's Office organization and ran the Office division in the late two thousands. In twenty thirteen, he was pulled away to help rescue the troubled launch of the federal healthcare website. Then he came back to Microsoft as the head of corporate strategy. So he's an operator and a strategist, which makes him a good guide for a conversation about buying companies.

Now a bit of history on Outlook itself, because it matters. The notes mention that Outlook on the PC began as an internal project called Ren, led by Brian MacDonald. That was the era when email was becoming the nervous system of the office. Outlook paired with Exchange, Microsoft's mail server, and for two decades that combination ran the corporate world. If you've ever worked in a company with an IT department, you've lived inside it. Scott, you probably have, many times. Which is exactly the problem. Outlook was a desktop giant, and the world was moving to a small glass rectangle in your pocket.

Microsoft had tried to answer that with its own phone platform, and the results were rough. In twenty fourteen, it bought Nokia's phone business for about seven billion dollars. A year later, it wrote most of that value off. Windows Phone never got past a small share of the market. And that taught Microsoft a hard lesson. If people won't buy your phone, you can't count on them using your apps on it. You have to build for the phones they already have.

That's the backdrop. Now the three deals.

Start with Acompli. It was a mobile email app, founded by Javier Soltero and Kevin Henrikson, among others. It was built for people who lived in their email on a phone. It sorted mail into focused and other, it had a smart calendar, and it let you attach files from services like Dropbox. Crucially, it worked with accounts from many providers, including Gmail and Exchange. It was fast, and it felt designed for the small screen rather than shrunk down from a desktop app.

Microsoft bought Acompli in December of twenty fourteen. Press reports at the time put the price at around two hundred million dollars. A few weeks later, Microsoft relaunched it as Outlook for iOS and Android. That was a big decision. Rather than build a mobile Outlook from scratch, Microsoft bought a team that had already solved the problem, and gave it the Outlook name.

Here's the twist worth noticing. The Acompli team had built its product on a cloud service that Microsoft then had to rebuild and move over to its own infrastructure. That kind of integration work is where acquisitions often go wrong. Yet the Outlook mobile apps went on to be downloaded in huge numbers and became a core part of Microsoft's mobile story.

And the human side matters too. Javier Soltero didn't just get acquired. He went on to run all of Outlook at Microsoft. That's part of what Ben and David asked Kurt about. How do you keep a startup's culture alive inside a giant? One answer is to put the founder in charge of the product people already know. Soltero later moved on to other roles, and eventually to Google, where he led its Workspace products. But the Outlook decision made him a symbol of what a good acqui-hire inside a big company can look like.

Next, Sunrise. Sunrise was a calendar app, founded by Pierre Valade and Jeremy Le Van. It was beautiful, and it connected to the calendars people already had, from Google, iCloud and Exchange. It also pulled in your other tools, like Evernote, Trello and Facebook events, right into the calendar view. Calendar is a classic problem. Everybody needs one, and almost nobody loves theirs. Sunrise made people love theirs.

Microsoft bought Sunrise in February of twenty fifteen. Again, reports at the time put the price at around one hundred million dollars. And here's a different story. Where Acompli became Outlook on phones, Sunrise was folded into Outlook's calendar features. The standalone Sunrise app was shut down in August of twenty sixteen, and its ideas and team carried on inside Outlook.

Why does that matter? Because it shows a second model of acquisition. Sometimes you buy a product. Sometimes you buy a team and a set of ideas, and you let the product retire. Both can be successes, but you can't measure them the same way. If you only look at the app's downloads, Sunrise looks like a loss. If you look at what Outlook calendar became, the picture changes.

Third, Wunderlist. Wunderlist was a to-do list app from a Berlin startup called six Wunderkinder, led by Christian Reber. It was clean, cross-platform and loved. It had millions of users across phones, tablets and the web, and it was famous for being simple. Microsoft bought it in June of twenty fifteen. Reports at the time put the price in the range of one hundred to two hundred million dollars, though the figure was never officially disclosed.

And once again, the plan was not to keep the brand forever. Microsoft later built a new app called To Do, using Wunderlist's ideas and team, and eventually retired Wunderlist itself in twenty twenty. For users, that was bittersweet. For Microsoft, it was the plan. Buy the people and the product sense, and fold them into a broader suite.

Here's a sidebar on how this kind of integration actually works, because it's the part of acquisitions nobody posts about. When a big company buys a small app, three things have to happen at once. The product has to keep running, because real users depend on it every morning. The engineering has to be moved onto the buyer's systems, which means security reviews, legal reviews and new infrastructure. And the team has to be kept happy while all that boring work happens. In the Acompli case, the security and infrastructure questions were serious enough that Microsoft spent real effort addressing them in public. Anyone who's ever migrated a venue's control system or a campus network will recognize the feeling. The visible change is small. The invisible work is enormous.

Think too about the timing. Three deals in about six months, from December twenty fourteen to June twenty fifteen. That wasn't an accident. It was a strategy being executed in sequence. First email, the most important daily habit. Then calendar, the thing that connects email to your actual day. Then tasks, the thing that turns meetings into action. If you had to design a minimal set of apps to own a knowledge worker's morning, you'd probably pick the same three.

And there's an uncomfortable question underneath. If these startups were so good, why didn't they just stay independent? The honest answer is that building a business on someone else's platform is hard. A calendar app depends on other companies' calendars. An email app depends on other companies' mail servers. A to-do app competes with free tools from the phone makers. Great products, tough economics. Selling to a company with distribution can be the smart move for the founders, too.

Now let's talk about what happened afterward. The Outlook mobile apps became the success story. They grew quickly and later added features like integrated calendars and add-ins. Microsoft shut down Sunrise in the summer of twenty sixteen and moved its ideas into Outlook. Wunderlist hung on until twenty twenty, when Microsoft retired it in favor of To Do. Microsoft's mobile reputation also changed. A company that had once looked lost on phones became one of the most visible makers of apps for them, on other companies' platforms.

There's a cultural lesson in that, too. Nadella often talked about moving from a know-it-all culture to a learn-it-all culture. These acquisitions are an early, concrete example. Microsoft admitted, in effect, that the best mobile email app had been built by someone else. It paid for it, listened to the people who built it, and shipped it under its own name.

Now let's pull it all together.

Before we leave the deals, a word on why founders say yes. When a giant company calls, you get three things a startup can't easily buy. You get distribution, meaning your product lands in front of hundreds of millions of people. You get resources, meaning engineers, servers and security teams. And you get time, because you no longer have to raise the next round. Against that, you give up control. That trade is the heart of every deal like this, and it's why the founders' stories after the acquisition matter as much as the price.

Also notice how the prices compare with the size of the buyer. Even adding all three reported figures together, you get a few hundred million dollars. For a company with well over a hundred billion dollars in annual revenue, that's small money for a very big option on the future. Smart acquirers often buy options like that rather than finished businesses.

One last detail on Wunderlist, because it shows the human side. The app came from Berlin, which was becoming a real startup hub at the time, and it had a devoted fan base that cared about small touches, like the sounds it made when you checked off a task. That sort of affection is hard to measure on a spreadsheet, and it's exactly what a buyer is paying for. It's also why shutting a beloved app down, even for good reasons, stings. Users remember how a product made them feel long after the company has moved on.

Put the three together and you can see the pattern. Email, calendar and tasks. These are the three daily tools of knowledge work. Microsoft had the old-school versions. It bought the new-school ones. And it did it for a combined price that was a rounding error in a company that size. Compare that with Microsoft's other big moves around then. It bought Mojang, the maker of Minecraft, for two and a half billion dollars in twenty fourteen. Those were headline deals. These three were small, quiet and strategic.

Now let's talk about the big question from the episode's notes. How do you decide whether to build or buy? There's no formula, but a few principles show up in this story.

First, speed. Mobile was moving faster than Microsoft's release cycles. Buying Acompli gave Microsoft a working mobile mail product within weeks, not years.

Second, taste. Great product feel is hard to hire into a big company. Startups like these three had a clear point of view about what a good calendar or to-do list should feel like. You can buy that.

Third, culture and talent. If you buy a startup and the team leaves, you've paid for a shell. That's why the Soltero story matters. Microsoft gave the founder real responsibility, and the team had a reason to stay.

Fourth, measure the right thing. The show notes mention this directly. They say that sometimes an acquisition's success isn't measured by revenue at all. Think about it. Outlook mobile is free. Sunrise was free. Wunderlist had a small premium tier. None of these were meant to be big profit centers. They were meant to keep Office relevant, and keep people inside the Microsoft ecosystem on the devices they actually carried.

That's a framework many operators can borrow. Before you do a deal, or a big project, write down what success looks like, and whether it's revenue, strategic position or capability. If you don't, you'll measure the wrong thing and judge the wrong result.

Let's also acknowledge the other side. Not every outcome here was a clean win. Users of Sunrise and Wunderlist lost products they loved. Some of the integration took longer than people hoped. And it's fair to ask whether Microsoft could have kept the independent brands alive. That's the cost of consolidation.

And how does it look today? With hindsight, the three deals look smart. Outlook on phones is a daily tool for a huge number of people. Microsoft To Do grew out of the Wunderlist idea. And Microsoft went on to bigger moves, including LinkedIn in twenty sixteen for about twenty-six billion dollars, a deal that came after this episode was recorded. But the logic was similar. Buy a network, a team or a product that points where the world is going.

For an audio-video professional, there's a familiar echo. Think about how many times you've seen a large vendor buy a small, nimble company, then fold its best features into a platform. The product line changes, the name disappears, the engineers stay, and the customers have to adapt. It happens in conferencing, in control systems, in streaming. The lesson holds. Ask what the acquirer is really buying, then plan around that.

Here are three lessons an operator can take. One, go where your customers already are, even if that's on a competitor's device. Nadella's move to put Office on the iPad and the iPhone was uncomfortable, and it was right. Two, buy a team, not just a product, and give its leader real ownership. Three, define success before you sign, because the scoreboard you pick decides the story you tell.

So that's episode eight. A small trio of apps, a big strategic shift, and a reminder that the cheapest acquisitions can sometimes carry the most strategy. And Ben and David's guest, Kurt DelBene, was in the best seat to explain it, from inside the strategy office of the company that did it.
