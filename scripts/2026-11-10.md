## AI Deep Dive: Tom Brands, Iowa Wrestling

This one's from the Lex Fridman archive. Episode two forty-five, released in late twenty twenty-one, and a bit of a departure from his usual AI conversations.

His guest is Tom Brands, Olympic champion and world champion in freestyle wrestling, three-time NCAA champion at Iowa, and head coach of the Iowa Hawkeyes. Lex recorded it at Tom's home after an Iowa versus Iowa State meet, with family and friends around, and he admits he wasn't sure he'd ever publish it. Why is it on a morning briefing? Because it's a masterclass in how high performers think, and you run a team.

Idea one. What drives Tom is hatred of losing, more than love of winning. He told a story about winning gold in Atlanta in nineteen ninety-six, then driving home in a rented three-cylinder Geo and mowing the lawn, because it hadn't been mowed in a month. He says the lessons came from losses. He lost to John Smith in nineteen ninety-one, fourteen to four, yet scored four takedowns, and he left knowing he could go with the best in the world.

Idea two is repetition, and doing what you hate. In Tom's words, quote, the things that you hate to do in this sport are the things that make you the very best, end quote. He thinks that's rare. The best athletes, he says, do the extra work on their own. The ones who plateau did everything the coach asked, and nothing more. He doesn't want people watching the clock. His rule, there's no clock in the wrestling room.

Idea three is how he builds mental toughness. You put people in situations they're willing to go through but don't think they can survive, and once they do, the barriers come down. It also depends on accountability and communication, which for Tom means direct conversations. His line was, I don't have time to waste, and neither do you.

Tom also had a thoughtful take on recovery. Looking back, he said he'd have trained in a more relaxed way. He pointed to Terry, who learned a less-is-more approach going into the two thousand Games. Recovery, he said, is work too. Massage, stretching, and rest all count, as long as you do them with the same discipline as the hard sessions.

And he was honest about mistakes. In ninety-six, he assumed he'd be the next coach in line when Dan Gable retired, and he admitted that was immature of him. He said that experience made him a better coach, because he could talk to young athletes from real perspective.

There's humor too. Tom told of a swim race against his identical twin Terry, in which he insisted Terry had false-started. Terry couldn't hear him, swam a full lap, and got tired. Tom won the rematch. Then Tom admitted that when they timed it fairly, Terry had actually beaten him. Lex called that pretty honest.

Why does it matter? It's a reminder that culture is built on small, unglamorous habits. And it has aged fine, because none of it depends on the moment. And remember who's saying it. This is a man who won Olympic gold, then coached Iowa to national titles.

## Acquired: Bungie, with Xbox Co-Founder Ed Fries

One quick note. This early Acquired episode has no published transcript, so I'm telling the story of the deal itself, using well-established public facts, and not putting words in Ben and David's mouths. The episode features Ed Fries, a former Microsoft vice president and a co-founder of Xbox, who was right in the middle of this.

Here's the story. A small Chicago game studio, a Mac-only developer, became the creator of one of the biggest franchises in video game history. And it got there because Microsoft bought it, then lost it, then watched it sell to Sony. Let's start at the beginning.

Bungie was founded in nineteen ninety-one by Alex Seropian, a young entrepreneur who was then a student at the University of Chicago. He started out by making a simple game and selling copies out of plastic bags. Soon he teamed up with Jason Jones, a programmer who'd built a game of his own, and the pair started building games for the Apple Macintosh. Remember the era. The Mac was a small market, but it was a loyal one, and games for it faced less competition.

Their breakout was Marathon, in nineteen ninety-four. It was a science fiction shooter that ran on the Mac, with a story told through computer terminals, and it earned Bungie real standing. Then came Myth, a real-time strategy game that did well on both Mac and PC. By the late nineties, Bungie wasn't just a Mac studio anymore. It was a studio with a reputation for craft, and for ambition.

Let's rewind a little further, because the Bungie origin story is better than most. Seropian's very first title was a Pong-style game called Gnop, which is Pong spelled backward. Yes, really. Then came Minotaur, a game of labyrinths and mythology. Jason Jones joined, and Bungie's next game, Pathways into Darkness, pushed the Mac into serious three-dimensional territory. These were small hits, but they built a fan base that would follow the studio for decades.

Here's a detail operators will appreciate. Bungie in those years was tiny. A handful of people, some of them friends from school, shipping games on a shoestring. Its edge was that the Mac audience was passionate, and the competition was thin. When your market is small, you can be the best in it. That's a strategy, not an accident.

Meanwhile, a new game was taking shape. It was called Halo. It began as a real-time strategy game on a distant, ring-shaped world. Over time it changed shape, first into a third-person action game, and eventually into the first-person shooter we know. In July of nineteen ninety-nine, Halo was shown on stage at the Macworld keynote, with Steve Jobs presenting. Imagine the scene. Jobs, the Mac, and a small Chicago studio unveiling a new game. That's where Ed Fries comes into the story.

Ed Fries ran Microsoft's games publishing group. And in those days Microsoft was building something secret, a game console. It would become the Xbox, and Ed was one of its co-founders. The console needed games, and it needed a killer app, something that would make people buy the box. Ed saw Halo, and he saw potential.

Here's the twist. Bungie, at the time, was not a company that was easy to buy. It had a publishing arrangement through Take-Two Interactive, which held rights to some of Bungie's existing work, including its game Oni. So there were several parties in the room, and each one wanted something different.

This is the part Ed Fries talks about in the episode. How do you find something that every party wants, so you can get a deal done? In this case, the answer was a split. Microsoft would get Bungie Studios, with Halo. Take-Two would keep Oni and the Myth series. According to the episode notes, a Mac gaming studio was spun out under Peter Tamte, to carry those assets forward, and Ryan Brandt led the effort to divide up Bungie's current projects and assets with Take-Two. It was a tidy solution, but it was far from simple.

The deal was announced in June of two thousand. The price was never officially disclosed. Press reports at the time suggested around thirty million dollars, and I'll flag that as a reported figure. For Microsoft, which was about to spend billions on a console launch, it was almost a rounding error.

Now, the episode highlights one more piece. Ed's call with Steve Jobs, after the acquisition. Think about it from Jobs's side. I'm reading between the lines here, but it's a fair guess. A Mac game, announced from his stage, was suddenly going to be an Xbox exclusive. That meant it would not come to the Mac. Ed describes that call in the episode, along with sharing the Macworld stage with Steve and Alex Seropian, and it shows how personal deals can be.

Let's pause on the Xbox side, because Ed's position explains the whole deal. In the late nineties, Sony's PlayStation was crushing the industry. Microsoft, the PC giant, worried that a living-room box would eat the PC's lunch. A small team inside the company, including people like Seamus Blackley, Kevin Bachus and Ted Hase, pitched Bill Gates on building a console of its own, based on PC technology and DirectX. Gates was convinced. And that created a hard problem. A console is nothing without games, and Microsoft had no console games to speak of.

Ed Fries had spent years running Microsoft's game publishing, which meant titles like Flight Simulator and Age of Empires, mostly on the PC. He knew what a great game team looked like. He also knew Microsoft couldn't build a console hit from a standing start. It needed to buy one, and quickly. Bungie, which had a stunning demo and a team that could ship, was an almost perfect fit. Halo's first public trailers at the time stopped people in their tracks. It looked like nothing else.

The timing mattered as well. Microsoft officially unveiled the Xbox on stage in March of two thousand one, and launched it in November. That left Bungie barely a year to turn an ambitious Mac and PC game into a console launch title. It's a miracle it shipped as polished as it did. And, yes, the team moved to Redmond, near Microsoft's campus, to do it.

Halo changed form one last time, becoming Halo: Combat Evolved, an Xbox launch title in November of two thousand one. And it worked. It became the reason to own an Xbox. Players loved the vehicles, the design of the weapons, and a rule that you could only carry two weapons at a time. Online play was still young, but the split-screen multiplayer, with four friends in a room, became legendary.

Why did it work? A few reasons. First, the game was excellent, and it was ready on day one. Second, Microsoft gave Bungie resources, and room to make the game it wanted. Third, Halo had a clear identity. Master Chief, the Covenant, the ring itself. It felt like a world, not just a level. Fourth, timing. A new console needs a flagship, and Halo was that flagship.

Then came the sequel, and the near-miss. Halo 2 was one of the most anticipated games ever made. But the project fell badly behind schedule, and parts of it weren't hitting the mark. The episode highlights how Jason Jones led the effort to save it, a story about rescuing a project that's off schedule and not yet good enough. The team cut features and reworked the game. And it launched in November of two thousand four.

How did it do? Halo 2 earned about one hundred twenty-five million dollars in its first twenty-four hours. That was bigger than most Hollywood openings, which made headlines at the time. Online play on Xbox Live turned the game into a social habit for millions. That's the sort of franchise that defines a platform.

But under the surface, tension was building. Bungie was a studio with its own culture, living inside a giant company. Microsoft wanted more Halo, and wanted it faster. And Bungie wanted independence. Halo 3 arrived in September of two thousand seven, and was another huge hit. And in that same year, Bungie and Microsoft split. Bungie became independent again. Microsoft kept the Halo franchise, and formed a new studio, called 343 Industries, to carry it forward.

Which brings us to the episode's other thread, Bungie today, and the unlikely path to get there, led by Harold Ryan. After leaving Microsoft, Bungie signed a ten-year publishing deal with Activision in twenty ten, to make a new shared-world shooter. That became Destiny, in twenty fourteen. Bungie later bought back its independence from Activision. And in early twenty twenty-two, Sony announced it would acquire Bungie for about three point six billion dollars. So the studio that Microsoft bought for a reported thirty million had now sold for more than a hundred times that.

Let's be fair about the arithmetic, though. Microsoft wasn't just buying a studio. It was buying Halo, and Halo helped make Xbox a real competitor. Then, years later, Bungie created another franchise, Destiny, that Microsoft didn't own. So who won? In some ways, everyone, and in some ways, no one.

What's the hindsight? Look at the pieces. Microsoft got the killer app it needed at the exact moment it needed it. Bungie got the money and the platform to make its best work, and then got its independence back. Seropian walked away earlier, with a good outcome. Sony, much later, paid a premium for a studio that had already proved itself twice. And Apple's Mac lost a landmark game.

Let me say more about why those early Mac years mattered, because Halo didn't appear from nowhere. Marathon taught the team how to tell a story inside a shooter, with a lonely hero, a mysterious ship and an artificial intelligence or two. If that sounds familiar, it should. Halo has a lonely soldier, a mysterious ring, and an artificial intelligence named Cortana. Myth taught the team about tactics, terrain and the thrill of a battle that unfolds on its own terms. The skills compounded. By the time Microsoft came calling, Bungie had spent nearly a decade learning how to build worlds, tell stories and finish games.

There's also a human side to the acquisition. For the founders, selling meant a choice. Stay independent and small, or join the giant with a console and a massive budget. Jason Jones and the core team chose the giant, because the budget and the platform could let them make the game they had in their heads. And the Mac fans? Many felt betrayed, and some never forgave it. That's the cost of winning a bigger audience. You can't always bring the old one along.

For Microsoft, the integration was its own project. Bungie was famously a studio that did things its own way, with its own humor and its own late nights. Microsoft was a corporation with schedules, reviews and a launch date it could not miss. Making those two cultures coexist is, frankly, the hidden story of every acquisition. The best ones don't try to blend the cultures. They protect what makes the acquired team special, and give it what it lacks, which is money, reach and patience.

Let me give you some numbers to frame the legacy. The Halo series has sold tens of millions of copies. Halo 3 made about one hundred seventy million dollars in the United States on its first day. Halo became a franchise with novels, comics, merchandise, and eventually a television series. Few game brands have matched that reach. And Bungie, after leaving, delivered Halo Reach in twenty ten as its final Halo game, a fitting farewell that fans still love.

Destiny deserves its own moment. It was an attempt to do something unusual, a shooter that felt like an online role-playing game, in a persistent world, with a story that kept evolving. It wasn't an easy launch. But it found its audience, and over the years it grew into a live service that supported hundreds of employees. In twenty nineteen, Bungie and Activision parted ways, and Bungie took full ownership of Destiny. That independence is what made Bungie so valuable to Sony, who announced its roughly three point six billion dollar purchase in January of twenty twenty-two, and finished it that summer.

Look at the symmetry. Microsoft bought the studio, built an empire on one franchise, and lost the creators to independence. Sony bought the same studio years later for a second franchise. Both buyers wanted what Bungie does best, which is building worlds that people don't want to leave.

And here's what's worth sitting with. The Halo story is also a story about how fragile a creative team is. Bungie's most productive years happened when the team had room, resources and a shared mission. When the people who made it left, they didn't stop being creative. They just took their creativity somewhere else. Companies that buy talent need to remember that the talent isn't owned. It's rented, and it renews each year, on the strength of the culture around it.

Now, three lessons an operator can take from this one.

Lesson one. Great deals find what each party wants. The Bungie deal worked because Microsoft, Take-Two and the founders each got something specific, and the assets were divided cleanly. If you're negotiating a complicated arrangement, look for the thing each side values most and costs the other side least.

Lesson two. A flagship product beats a broad catalog. A new platform lives or dies on a few must-have experiences. Halo was that for Xbox. It's the same in audio-video. One reference installation, one signature product, can carry a brand further than a hundred average ones.

Lesson three. Rescuing a late project takes ruthless focus. Halo 2 was in trouble, and what saved it was cutting scope, committing to the core, and having a clear leader. Jason Jones took ownership. If a project at your shop is slipping, decide what truly defines it, and protect that.

One more thing worth saying. Acquisitions don't always end where they start. Microsoft bought Bungie to launch a console. It ended up building a franchise that outlived the relationship. Talent can leave, and that's okay, as long as the thing you built stays.

So, how would Ben and David grade it? I can't put a grade in their mouths, because I don't have the transcript. But looking at the facts, Microsoft got a franchise that helped define Xbox for years, for a reported price that was tiny in corporate terms. That's a very good outcome for a company that was about to bet billions on a console. And it shows why Acquired keeps returning to the same idea. The best acquisitions put the right talent next to the right resources, at exactly the right time.
