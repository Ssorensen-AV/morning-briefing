## Acquired: Zillow plus Trulia, with Zillow Group CFO Kathleen Philips

Now our main course. This is Acquired episode twenty-two, from October of twenty sixteen. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal talk through Zillow's purchase of Trulia, with a guest who lived it, Kathleen Philips, then the CFO of Zillow Group. Ben warns up front that the audio is a little rough. We'll do the storytelling for you.

Let's start with two companies that were basically twins.

Zillow was founded in two thousand five in Seattle by Rich Barton and Lloyd Frink. They'd worked together at Microsoft, and they founded Expedia, which began inside Microsoft and was spun out as a public company in two thousand one. Zillow's big idea, launched in two thousand six, was the Zestimate. It was a data-driven estimate of what every home in the database would sell for. David says it was probably the first time American homeowners had any sense of their home's value without listing it. Zillow raised about eighty million dollars from investors like Benchmark and T C V, and went public in July of twenty eleven.

Trulia was founded a year earlier, in two thousand four, in the Bay Area, by Pete Flint and Sami Inkinen. They were Stanford business school students, and the legend is that they started it between their first and second years, because they couldn't find housing for year two. Trulia raised about thirty-three million dollars from Accel, Sequoia and others, far less than Zillow, and went public in September of twenty twelve.

Here's the twist. Zillow's first approach to Trulia came in late twenty eleven, right after Zillow's own I P O. Kathleen explains why. Zillow had spent a year, from July twenty ten, completely focused on that I P O, and the point was liquid public stock to use for acquisitions. Trulia was the natural first target. David notes how hard it is for two private companies to agree on value when nobody knows what either stock is worth. A public Zillow could just offer shares.

Trulia hired an investment bank, Catalyst. Talks broke down in early twenty twelve. Zillow tried again in August twenty twelve, while Trulia was preparing to go public. Talks broke down again. Kathleen says they were fierce competitors who'd each admired the other, played in the same sandbox, and each concluded for a long time they should just go it alone. Shifting from that, she says, is hard when you've grown a company from the ground up.

Meanwhile, both stocks soared. Kathleen remembers Zillow's revenue at the I P O was around forty million dollars, with a market cap of around six hundred to seven hundred million. She jokes that Zillow's emerging businesses were now bigger than the whole company at I P O.

Fast forward to spring twenty fourteen. Zillow does something unusual. It quietly sounds out major shareholders of both companies, under N D A, about strategic opportunities, including buying Trulia. Kathleen clarifies that these were largely the same shareholders, who held both stocks, and part of their thesis was that a combination would eventually happen. It was, in her words, natural industrial logic.

And the logic was big. Kathleen says Zillow brand sites had about two thirds of web traffic and three quarters of mobile traffic in real estate, but touched only about four percent of transactions in the United States. Lots of green field, she says, which is why they never worried about going international. She adds that for the first time, about half of home transactions now involved millennials. Ben, who was twenty-seven and renting, says his whole peer group lives on Zillow for entertainment value.

Then the drama. In early June twenty fourteen, Zillow hires Goldman Sachs. On June third, Rich Barton, then chairman, reaches out to Pete Flint to schedule a dinner. Pete says his near-term schedule can't accommodate dinner. Kathleen laughs, and says Pete knew very well what was coming. Two days later, Rich is more direct. The Zillow board fully supports a merger, and shareholders in common are supportive. A few days after that, a letter goes to Pete and to Greg Waldorf, Trulia's lead director. This was the first time Zillow had put anything directly in front of the Trulia board. Kathleen says it turned up the urgency.

Now, how did they settle on price? The offers were close. Zillow started with about thirty percent of the combined company for Trulia holders, and Pete came back at around thirty-seven. Kathleen says there was no fancy model. They'd had nearly ten years of side-by-side operating history, and it was always about two thirds Zillow, one third Trulia. Even a year after closing, she says, lead volume was still about two thirds to one third. So they were really quibbling at the margins. On July fifth, Rich and Pete agreed that thirty-three percent made sense. Trulia's board did counter at thirty-four and a half percent, plus a go-shop clause, which would let Trulia keep entertaining other bidders, and a hefty breakup fee. The breakup fee ended up at one hundred fifty million dollars. Zillow's reply, within a day, was basically no. Thirty-three percent, final offer, no go-shop. Kathleen says there was never any chance of a go-shop with a direct competitor. It would only create a frenzy in the market.

The speed is the part Kathleen is proud of. Diligence started July first. The merger was announced on July twenty-eighth. Twenty-seven days, with about fifty people at Zillow working on the agreement. Her rule is that deals go fast, usually under twenty days, to avoid distraction, to avoid risk of losing the deal, and to get back to day jobs. And remember, she was still chief operating officer, running the people organization, plus legal. She says it consumed her for about eight months.

Then there's what David calls the most amusing part. Trulia's co-founder Sami Inkinen was rowing across the Pacific Ocean, with his wife, in a rowboat, from California to Hawaii, for the entire negotiation. Kathleen assumes he'd given someone his proxy before leaving. Ben points out it's a first for Acquired, and honestly, it's still the best excuse for missing a board call.

Okay, but announcing was the easy part. Next came the F T C, the Federal Trade Commission. They issued two requests for information, instead of the usual one, which markets take as a bad sign, and share prices dipped. The question was whether online real estate portals counted as their own market, and whether the combination would give pricing power. Kathleen's argument was that the real market is much bigger. Zillow touched about four percent of transactions, and a small share of agents' advertising spend. The F T C's concern was the price of advertising for agents, since the sites are free to consumers. She says they got all the company email, hours of depositions, and economic experts on both sides, and the F T C talked to other players and to agents. Kathleen herself spent about four months full time in Washington D C, and says she aged a few extra years in six months. The lesson David takes for startup founders is to be careful how you characterize your market in writing, because innocent phrases can look very different out of context.

The F T C finally approved the deal in February of twenty fifteen. And then, right before closing, another twist. ListHub, the data provider that supplied a large share of both companies' listings, cut them off. ListHub was owned by Move dot com, which had just been bought by News Corp. Zillow and Trulia would now have to sign direct deals with hundreds, maybe thousands, of local multiple listing services. Kathleen has a nice answer on this. It was a natural contract termination, they'd been preparing for it, and she used it with the F T C. How could they be a monopoly if their lifeblood, listings, was controlled by a competitor? She also notes the relationship was symbiotic, since ListHub sold agents reports like, your listing on one two three Main Street was viewed fifty times on Zillow. And one unforeseen benefit of the merger was that as number one and number two in the market, it was much easier to get listings directly.

So what did the combined company actually do? A big decision was to keep both brands. Kathleen says there was a lot of brand equity in Trulia, and consumers who strongly preferred one or the other. What they merged was the plumbing. Listings now come in from a central source. Instead of cutting developers, they redeployed them. A big team of former Trulia people in San Francisco built the Premier Agent app, one of Zillow's most successful launches that year. Premier Agent is a subscription advertising product where agents pay to be featured next to for-sale listings, and after the deal, agents buy across both sites. Kathleen says more entrepreneurial agents were forming teams and buying ads in large quantities, building big businesses.

Retention was delicate. Trulia's management got about thirty-three million dollars in equity retention. Kathleen says they put themselves in the shoes of the Trulia team, thinking about who they needed to keep and for how long. And they never lost sight of the psychology, which is being acquired by the competitor you've fought ferociously for ten years. The goal was to make everyone feel like this was a winning deal.

Let's step back and look at why this deal worked, because the hosts spend real time on it.

The first reason is that the numbers were already on the table. Both companies were public, so both sets of financials were in the open. Kathleen's point is that when you can see ten years of side-by-side results, valuation stops being a fight about the future. It becomes arithmetic about the past. Two thirds, one third. That's why the gap between thirty and thirty-seven percent closed in a matter of days instead of months.

The second reason is that the deal was an accelerant, not a bet on something new. Zillow wasn't buying a technology it didn't understand. It was buying more of the thing it already did well, more audience, more listings, more agents, in a market where the audience attracts the advertisers. David keeps coming back to that. You don't have to invent a new business. You have to add scale to the one you have, and let the flywheel do the rest.

The third reason is cultural. Trulia was acquired by the rival it had fought for a decade. The easy mistake would have been to absorb it, erase the brand, and watch the best people leave. Instead, Zillow kept the brand, kept the founders engaged with retention packages, and gave the engineers more interesting work. Kathleen says the headline benefit wasn't cost cutting. It was freeing up talent.

And with hindsight, from where we sit today, the picture is mixed in an interesting way. Zillow Group became the dominant real estate portal in the country, and Trulia stayed alive as a separate brand. Pete Flint went on to become an investor. But Zillow also had a later, expensive detour into buying and flipping homes itself, which it shut down in twenty twenty-one. That's a reminder that the Trulia deal worked because it was the same business at larger scale. The detour wasn't.

Now the hosts' framework. Ben files this under a business line acquisition, but of the same business line. More supply, more demand, same marketplace. David links it to Ben Thompson's aggregation theory. In the information economy, you win by aggregating customers with the best experience, and listings and advertisers follow. Kathleen's own phrase is time machine acquisition. It simply accelerated what each company was already doing. They expected traffic benefits, and the benefits of scale, like listings, exceeded expectations.

Kathleen also lays out Zillow's broader M&A approach. They'd looked at about one hundred twenty-five potential deals the previous year. First question, will this accelerate something we're already doing, or is it something we haven't figured out yet. But, she says, in every deal they start with the people. Dotloop helps agents close transactions online instead of on paper. HotPads, StreetEasy and Naked Apartments build out a portfolio of brands, with HotPads for younger urban renters and StreetEasy for New York. And teams get redeployed. A HotPads group now handles all rentals syndication across the platform. She also tells the story of Susan Daimler, who came in through the acquisition of her small company, bifolio, and ended up running StreetEasy.

David's tech theme comes from Rich Barton. His secret, as he told the New York Times, is to ask what piece of marketplace information people crave and don't have. Zillow gave people their home value, and they came back every week, like a mouse in a lab turning the wheel for cheese. Ben's theme is that real-world objects are media. People browse listings as entertainment. And Kathleen drops a surprise. For its first three years, Zillow had no real estate listings at all, just Zestimates. The bet was to build audience first and advertisers will follow.

Now the grades. Ben gives it a solid B plus. It's an accelerant, but in Acquired's view, the A grades are for ten-x outcomes, like Instagram and Android. David goes with B for the deal and a plus for the execution. They knew traffic would grow by about a third, and Trulia holders got about a third of the company. Fair price, fair value. He calls the execution A plus, across the valuation, the negotiation, the F T C, and ListHub. Kathleen accepts, humbly, and says she felt like she was living in a textbook, and that B plus is fair because they were still early in reaping the benefits.

The episode wraps with some period-piece hot takes. Snap had just renamed itself Snap Inc. and launched Spectacles. Ben was excited. Kathleen said she'd buy a pair, and liked the blue lipstick. Twitter was rumored to be talking to Disney, and David predicted there'd be an episode. Hindsight is generous here. Disney never bought Twitter. And AppLovin, the profitable, mostly bootstrapped mobile marketing firm that had raised only about four million dollars, was bought by a Chinese private equity firm for one point four billion dollars. It went on to become a very big public company.

Carve-outs. Ben recommends a video called The Marvel Symphonic Universe, about why none of us can hum a Marvel theme. David recommends Shoe Dog, Phil Knight's memoir, and its line about the run he took in nineteen sixty-two, I'm not going to stop. And Kathleen recommends the glam rock band The Struts.

So what should an operator take from this? One, move fast on deals. Twenty-seven days from diligence to announcement is a lesson in focus. Two, protect your supply. The ListHub cutoff showed why you never want your lifeblood controlled by a competitor. Three, when you buy a rival, keep what customers love and merge only the plumbing. And four, put the people first. That's what Zillow did, and that's why a once bitter rivalry became a very good B plus.
