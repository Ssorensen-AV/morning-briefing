## AI Deep Dive: George Hotz

This one's from the Lex archive, back in June of twenty twenty-three, episode three eighty-seven. The guest is George Hotz, and it's his third time on the show.

If you don't know him, here's the short version. At seventeen, he was the first person to unlock the iPhone. He went on to found comma dot ai, which sells a driver-assist system called openpilot that you can add to an ordinary car. And at the time of this conversation, he'd just started a second company, tiny corp, which makes a neural network framework called tinygrad. He'd also done a brief stint at Twitter, where he described himself as an intern. Lex calls him one of his favorite people to talk to, and you can hear why. He's blunt, funny, and allergic to hype.

Let's start with the idea at the center of tiny corp. Hotz's argument is that nearly all of a neural network is simple. It's the same adds and multiplies, run on different data, every single time. No surprises, no branching. So why, he asks, do we run it on a stack where every layer, from Python down to the chip, can compute anything at all? That generality costs you. It's why a processor has to spend a pile of transistors guessing which way a branch will go.

So tinygrad goes the other direction. It has no built-in matrix multiplication at all. Hotz says there are roughly twenty-five basic operations, in four families: operations on one tensor, operations on two, reductions like sum and max, and movement operations that just change how you look at memory. Everything else gets built from those. For comparison, he says PyTorch's lower-level set has about two hundred fifty operations, and the older kernel library has around two thousand. His analogy is RISC versus CISC, the simple instruction set that won the chip wars. He even points out that a movie got there first. In the nineteen ninety-five film Hackers, a character announces that RISC architecture is going to change everything.

Here's the part that should interest an operations person. Why does any of this matter? Hotz says the real bottleneck for AI chip startups isn't the silicon, it's the software. A new accelerator might be great hardware, but someone has to write and tune hundreds of kernels before it can run a mainstream framework. He says the chips were good and the software was terrible, and he names several well-funded companies as examples. His prediction is that if you can't build a top-notch software stack on Nvidia GPUs, all the way down to the driver, you've got no chance on your own chip.

That's why tinygrad started by targeting AMD graphics cards. And here's a story with a very human ending. Hotz says that when he tried AMD's top consumer card for machine learning, the kernel driver crashed just from running their demo apps in a loop. He gave up on camera one week. Then he emailed AMD's chief executive, Lisa Su, and she replied. His message back was that dumping code on GitHub isn't open source. Open source, he said, is a culture, with developing in public and issues that don't sit stale for a year.

His motive isn't really chips, though. It's power. His central thesis, in his words, is that there are things that centralize power, and they're bad, and things that decentralize power, and they're good. He worries that if one company's chips become ten times better than everything else, whoever controls that company controls a huge advantage. And he doesn't love the cloud, for one simple reason. Quote, the fundamental limitation of cloud is who owns the off switch.

Which brings us to the product, the tinybox. In the episode, it's a fifteen thousand dollar box with six graphics cards, nearly a petaflop of compute, and over a hundred gigabytes of graphics memory. The design constraint is delightful. It has to run off a normal wall outlet, a one hundred twenty volt, fifteen amp circuit. He wants it quiet enough to sit in your room, around forty to forty-five decibels, and his trick is, ironically, to make the Tinybox big, so it can use big slow fans instead of jet engines. The goal is to run a sixty-five billion parameter language model at a few words per second, in your house. Pre-orders were a hundred dollars, fully refundable, a trick he says he borrowed from Elon Musk.

Now to self-driving. Hotz says almost nobody talks about it anymore, and that his team solved the core problem years ago. He means learning a human driving policy from data. The new push at comma is what he calls DriveGPT, a simulator trained to predict the next video frame, so you can train the car inside it. The reward isn't, did you copy a human. It's, would a human have taken over? That's a clever metric, because a driver grabbing the wheel is the clearest sign they're unhappy. How close is it? He says they're, quote, a couple bug fixes away. It might take a year, might take ten. He also says Tesla will always be one to two years ahead of comma, because, in his words, they're not doing anything wrong.

And then there's the AI safety section, where he surprises you. Asked whether he agrees with Eliezer Yudkowsky that AI will almost surely kill everyone, he says yes, but for a different reason. Not a rogue machine, a human with a machine. Think of the little red button, not the big one. The scariest outcome in his mind is humans amusing themselves to death, with an infinite, perfectly generated TikTok. He does think AI could end society as we know it. But he doesn't think it ends the species, because, as he puts it, life reproduces without a repair shop, and robots can't. He thinks we'll build superintelligence long before we build robustness.

So, how has it aged? Mixed, and it's worth saying so. His remark that the language models of the day were roughly junior engineers and bargain-bin copywriters has been overtaken by events. Better models have since moved well past that bar. His call that cross-entropy training alone won't produce general intelligence is still debated, with reinforcement learning now playing a much bigger role than it did then. On the other side, his point about software being the real moat for AI hardware, and about Nvidia's lead being a software story, looks very well established. And tinygrad is a real, active open source project.

He ends where he always does, with the big question. What's the meaning of life? To win. Win what? He hasn't figured out the game yet. But he says, quote, I think the game is to stand eye to eye with God. And his reasoning is a tidy bit of engineering logic. He wants his own creations to stand eye to eye with him, so why wouldn't a creator want the same?

Why does this matter for you? Because it's a clear look at the argument that the AI era will be decided by software stacks and who owns the off switch, not just by who builds the fastest chip. Whether or not you buy his predictions, it's worth hearing the reasoning.

## Acquired: Virgin America

This one comes from episode ten of Acquired, from April of twenty sixteen, and it's a good one for a man who has spent his career around airports, airplanes, and anything that has to run on a schedule. It's an acquisition that isn't really about technology at all. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal admit it up front. They just have a romantic fascination with airplanes. And the deal was only a couple of weeks old when they recorded, so, as Ben says, this one is highly speculative.

The deal is Alaska Airlines buying Virgin America. Both are West Coast airlines, and David and Ben are Seattle guys, so this is their backyard.

Let's start with how Virgin America came to exist. Richard Branson founded it in two thousand four. But there's a catch. U.S. law caps foreign ownership of a domestic airline at twenty-five percent. So Branson and the Virgin team had to sell off seventy-five percent of the company to a couple of hedge funds before they could even try to fly. The name was originally Virgin USA, and it got rebranded later. And here's a detail Ben loves. Virgin America doesn't even own the Virgin name painted on its planes. It licenses it.

It took until two thousand seven to clear the regulatory hurdles, buy aircraft, and start flying, with San Francisco International as the hub. Things went fairly well, which, as the hosts note, is more than you can say for most startup airlines. And Virgin did some things that felt like technology. In two thousand nine, it became the first airline to offer in-flight Wi-Fi, through Gogo. It also put interactive touch screens in the seat back of every seat on the plane. And then there was the lighting. Purple mood lighting, nightclub style. David says it was the favorite airline of his business school classmates, and then, with a straight face, adds, we can leave it at that.

Virgin went public in November of twenty fourteen. About eighteen months later, a bidding war broke out between Alaska and JetBlue. On Monday, April fourth, twenty sixteen, Alaska announced it would buy Virgin for two point six billion dollars, in cash. That was a forty-seven percent premium to the previous closing price, and roughly an eighty percent premium to where the stock traded before the bidding war rumors started. Add Virgin's debt and aircraft leases, about one point four billion dollars more, and the total comes to roughly four billion dollars. David's reaction, delivered deadpan, is that this is four Instagrams. Ben's reaction is a bit more sober. He says what a ridiculous, capital-intensive, high fixed cost industry this is.

Here's the first twist. Alaska didn't have two point six billion dollars lying around. At the end of twenty fifteen it had eighty-eight million in cash and about one point one billion in marketable securities. So how did it win? Its balance sheet. Alaska was one of the very few airlines with investment grade debt, which means lenders believed it was unlikely to go bankrupt. JetBlue, the hosts figure, simply couldn't borrow enough to reach that price. Ben's read is that JetBlue had to cry uncle.

How did the hosts categorize the deal? On their usual scorecard of people, technology, product, business line or other, Ben says the real answer is capacity. Alaska was strong in Seattle and nearly absent from San Francisco and Los Angeles. Virgin was the mirror image, a spider web of routes from one hub. David called it industry consolidation. And there's context. Delta had been steadily taking gates in Seattle, partly to compete with Alaska, and partly to use Seattle as a gateway to Asia. Alaska was a domestic carrier with a nice niche and nowhere obvious to grow. Buying Virgin was a way to roughly double in size.

Let me slow down on Alaska's pitch to its own investors, because the hosts clearly enjoyed picking it apart. Ben found the investor deck on Alaska's website. It lists five reasons to be bullish. One, a fundamentally changed industry structure, meaning consolidation, with four near-identical giants. Two, returns-focused leadership teams, which Ben calls tooting your own horn and claiming competency. Three, constrained airport real estate. Nobody's building many new airports, there are only so many gates, and so buying capacity makes more sense than building it. Four, growth in leisure travel. And five, new revenue sources, which, as David says, is a polite way of saying charging for bags, plus the nicer stuff, like Wi-Fi, movies, and snacks that are actually edible. Ben adds that Alaska co-brands its snacks with the Seattle restaurateur Tom Douglas, and that he now opens a snack menu on another airline and finds Tom smiling at him.

Now the hosts spend a good while on why airlines are a terrible business, and it's worth hearing. Alaska told its own investors that from nineteen seventy-seven to two thousand nine, the industry lost fifty-two billion dollars. From twenty ten to twenty fifteen, it made forty-five billion. David points to a paper by the investor Michael Mauboussin called Measuring the Moat, which uses airlines as an example of an industry with no moat at all. And Ben ran the numbers on market caps that day. Delta around thirty-six billion, Southwest thirty, American twenty-five, United twenty-one, Alaska about ten, JetBlue seven, Virgin two and a half. Add them up, and the whole industry is worth about one hundred thirty-two billion dollars. Apple alone was worth about five hundred ninety billion. That's the point. Airlines don't capture much of the value they create.

By the way, the four big carriers by then handled about eighty percent of domestic traffic after the United and Continental merger and the American and US Airways merger. The supply side is just as lopsided. Boeing and Airbus are a duopoly, and as David jokes, an airline can't pull a Google and just decide to build its own airplanes. Ben's line is, getting good at servers is different than getting good at airplanes.

Now, here's where the hosts start poking at the price. Ben lists the size difference. Alaska carried thirty-two million passengers a year, Virgin seven. Alaska flew about a thousand departures a day, Virgin two hundred. Alaska served one hundred twelve destinations, Virgin twenty-four. Alaska's pre-tax profit was about one point three billion dollars, Virgin's about two hundred million. So it's an expensive purchase of a much smaller operation.

Then the payback math. Alaska told investors it expected two hundred twenty-five million dollars in annual net synergies once everything was integrated, around twenty eighteen. Divide four billion by two hundred twenty-five million and you get a seventeen-year payback, on synergies alone. David notes Virgin's own earnings help, but either way, it's going to take a while.

Then David raises two head-scratchers. The first is fuel. The airline industry's recent renaissance was driven largely by falling fuel prices, which roughly halved what airlines spent on fuel. If that's temporary, then Alaska is buying near the top of the market, when profits are artificially inflated.

The second is the fleet, and this is the one an operations person will appreciate. Alaska flew only Boeing. Virgin flew only Airbus. You might think a plane is a plane. It isn't. They have different control systems. A pilot who flies Boeings can't fly Airbus planes, and the reverse. Maintenance and parts are completely different too. Most major airlines do run mixed fleets, but Southwest, as Ben notes, is entirely Boeing seven thirty-sevens, because staying lean and interchangeable is the heart of its model. Alaska had the same playbook, and now it was suddenly a fifty-fifty shop. Ben adds that, from a heartstrings perspective, a Seattle company buying an all-Airbus airline is just not patriotic.

So what would have happened otherwise? Virgin was going to be sold anyway, and JetBlue, an Airbus operator, would have had an easier time capturing cost savings. Ben also points out that Virgin was only recently profitable. It took three years to post a profitable quarter. And it was trying to be both a low-cost airline and a premium one. Ben's line is you can't be both a Volvo and a Cadillac. David connects it to the Edsel, the Ford car that tried to be everything to everyone, and failed spectacularly. Still, David gives Alaska some credit. Standing still wasn't safe either, with Delta pushing in and consolidation everywhere.

One more point about the price. Notice what happened to Alaska's own stock when the deal was announced. It went down. Ben confirms the market value of Alaska was still around ten billion dollars. The market, in other words, wasn't cheering. And the hosts return to the brand question, because it's the heart of the matter. Alaska's brand was professional and business-commuter friendly, with some of the best on-time performance on the West Coast. Virgin's brand was a nightclub with wings. Ben says people who love Virgin really love Virgin, and people who love Alaska love Alaska too. Both are among his favorite airlines. But they're different animals, and the whole premium, in his view, was paid for something Alaska wasn't going to keep.

The hosts also discuss why this deal may not be as odd as it looks. Alaska, David says, had built something genuinely good in Seattle, with the best routes and customer service for people flying in and out of Sea-Tac. But it had nowhere to go. Expand internationally? Move into other cities? Both hard to do organically. So the pitch was simple. We have a great balance sheet, and for an airline, a lot of cash. We're run pretty well compared to the industry. Here's a chance to buy Virgin, basically double in size, and run the same playbook again. The alternative was staying put and hoping Delta's pressure and the wave of consolidation didn't squeeze them. That's the case for the defense, and the hosts, to their credit, say they've been taking potshots at the deal and want to give Alaska its due.

There's a brand question too. Alaska said it planned to retire the Virgin name by twenty eighteen. Ben asks the obvious thing. If you obliterate that brand, what was the point of paying a markup on a markup for capacity? Virgin felt premium, and customers loved it, and that affection was baked into the price. And a quick pro tip from David, for anyone flying between Seattle and the Bay Area. Skip San Francisco and use Oakland or San Jose, because fog delays at SFO ripple through the schedule. As a Bay Area traveler, you may have opinions.

For the tech theme, David picks niche marketing. Both airlines won by serving a niche passionately. Alaska owned Seattle business travelers, and Virgin owned style-minded San Francisco customers. His lesson for startups is not to go after Google on day one. Start with a small group that loves you, and knock down one bowling pin at a time. The hosts also riff on loyalty programs, and on how travel sites like Kayak and Priceline commoditized airlines and squeezed their margins, which pushes survivors to consolidate. David ties that to aggregation theory, and notes that Southwest refused to be listed on the aggregators and still has some of the most loyal customers. Its stock ticker, after all, is LUV.

There's a lovely detour near the end about network effects, and it's a good one for anyone who's ever had to standardize a team on one tool. David describes two years earlier, when some of his portfolio companies used HipChat and others used Slack. He'd tell the HipChat people to try Slack, and they'd say HipChat was good enough. Then more of their friends and industry groups moved to Slack, and the pull became irresistible, even if Slack's product hadn't been any better. Ben's version is that people started creating shared Slack channels between companies, so nobody wanted to run two applications. Then he asks whether the same logic applies to airline loyalty. Nobody wants to keep points in two programs, which was always annoying. He remembers startups that tried to aggregate loyalty programs for him, and says that was a total pain.

Ben takes the aggregation idea one step further, and it's the cleanest bit of reasoning in the episode. Travel sites like Kayak, Hipmunk, Travelocity, and Priceline appear fifteen or twenty years earlier. They make airline seats easy to compare, which weakens loyalty to any one airline and compresses margins. Thinner margins mean you need a cheaper back office and economies of scale. And if you're a small airline, the inefficiency of being small could kill you. So, follow it all the way back, and the online travel aggregators help create the environment in which two airlines will fight a bidding war to get bigger. David says it's reminiscent of Ben Thompson's aggregation theory, in which the player who owns the customer relationship on the front end commoditizes everything behind it. It had completely happened to airlines. And Southwest, which refused to be listed on the aggregators, kept some of the most loyal customers around.

Time for the grades. Ben goes with a B minus. His case is that the price was inflated by the bidding war and by a brand Alaska didn't plan to use, but Alaska had few options, and it had put itself in the one position, with investment grade credit, where it could actually make the purchase. David goes lower. A C minus. Quote, they paid so much money. And he adds, that's a large price for something that your pilots can't fly. Ben's question afterward is the funniest of the episode. Can an airline make a good purchase?

For the carve-out, Ben picks a Michael Mauboussin talk at Google, based on the book The Success Equation, about untangling skill and luck. David highlights the paradox of skill. In a field like basketball, as the players get better and more evenly matched, luck plays a bigger role in who wins. And David says the same holds in startups and investing.

Now, how does it look with hindsight? This is worth saying plainly, since the hosts were speculating. The deal did close, and Alaska folded Virgin America into its own operation. The Virgin brand was retired from the skies, which is exactly the fear Ben flagged. For years, Alaska worked through the Boeing and Airbus puzzle, and in the long run it moved toward simplifying its fleet around Boeing. The fuel worry was also well founded in general terms, because airline profits have always swung with oil prices. Take that as background, not as something Ben and David said.

If you want lessons an operator can use, there are three. First, standardization has real value, which is the Southwest lesson. Every extra type of aircraft, or any other unlike system, multiplies training, spares, and scheduling complexity. Second, a strong balance sheet is a strategic weapon. Alaska won a bidding war because it could borrow when others couldn't. Third, synergy math deserves suspicion. When the savings take seventeen years to pay back the price, the deal has to work for other reasons, and you'd better know what they are.

That's Virgin America, a deal that was exciting for a lot of reasons, and expensive for all of them.
