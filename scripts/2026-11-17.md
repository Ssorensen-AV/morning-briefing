## AI Deep Dive: Benjamin Netanyahu

From the Lex Fridman archive now, back in July of twenty twenty-three. Lex sat down with Benjamin Netanyahu, then in his sixth term as Israel's prime minister. It's a political conversation, so I'll attribute what's said to the people who said it. But the AI section is worth your time, and it's a window into how a head of government thought about the technology.

The setting matters. Large protests were underway across Israel over proposed judicial reform. Lex said the planned three-hour talk was cut to one hour. Netanyahu defended the reform as restoring balance among the branches of government. His critics said it concentrated power. That argument is still unresolved.

Now the ideas. First, AI and speed. Netanyahu said people don't have an intuitive grasp of geometric growth. We think in straight lines, and AI is climbing like a ski slope. His conclusions, after talking with Sam Altman and Elon Musk, were simple. There's no option of staying out. Regulation is needed. But global regulation isn't assured. He even offered a dry twist: the only thing that might push countries to cooperate is the threat that AI could come to control us.

Second, he compared AI to cyber. A decade earlier, he said, he read a novel about a Chinese-American cyber war, called in experts, and set a goal of making Israel one of the world's five cyber powers. He wants the same for AI. He pointed to medical records covering ninety-eight percent of Israelis, and floated adding a genetic database on top.

Netanyahu also said Israel will do more with less. He called it a relatively small but highly gifted population, and argued that Nvidia building a supercomputer there, and Intel investing twenty-five billion dollars in a new plant, showed where the talent was. That's his pitch, and you can weigh it as a pitch.

Third, and most interesting, jobs. Netanyahu said he's a free-market guy, and he always believed new technology produced more jobs than it destroyed. Now he isn't sure. Here's the quote. "If I have to bet, it will annihilate many more jobs than it will create and it'll force a structural change in our economics, in our economic models, and in our politics." Lex pushed back. He said he believed, maybe naively, that AI would create more jobs than it takes. Netanyahu's reply: "Write that down and we'll check it." Lex said it's on record.

Lex also raised monopoly power. His hope was that open source and the little guy can still challenge the giants. Netanyahu said that's been his policy, that the market is the best regulator, but he wasn't sure the nimble mammal always beats the dinosaur.

There's a nice moment in the middle of that exchange. Netanyahu asked where you draw the line on monopoly power, and said it isn't as simple as breaking up AT and T. Economic power, he said, becomes information power, and that becomes political power. Lex answered, "I wouldn't count out the little guy." Netanyahu's reply was a dry, "Well, I hope you're right." Fair enough. That one's been aging in real time, with open models and tiny startups regularly embarrassing the giants, and the giants regularly buying them.

There was also a lighter beat. Lex asked who he'd bet on in a fight between Elon Musk and Mark Zuckerberg. Netanyahu said he refused to answer, then added that he was openly telling you he was dodging. A politician to the end.

Why does it matter? That bet is still live. Today's TLDR issue literally includes a study predicting AI will cut junior desk hiring. Netanyahu's worry about jobs and Lex's optimism are both still unsettled, and nobody has collected on either side.

A word on how the rest has aged. The peace, power and corruption sections reflect a very different moment, before the events of October of twenty twenty-three. Netanyahu argued then for expanding the Abraham Accords toward Saudi Arabia. That's a debate you'll want other sources for.

## Acquired: Snapchat

A quick note on this one. Episode twelve of Acquired is a strange one, because it's about a deal that never happened. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal cover Facebook's attempted acquisition of Snapchat for three billion dollars, in November of twenty thirteen. This episode dates from May of twenty sixteen, so take every number as of then. The nice part is that they have the benefit of history to play out what would have happened.

Let's start with the origin. In twenty ten, Evan Spiegel is a sophomore at Stanford, living in the Kappa Sigma fraternity house. Across the hall is a senior named Bobby Murphy. They become friends, and Bobby recruits Evan for a social app inspired by Google Plus. It goes nowhere.

Evan, meanwhile, is doing great. He majors in product design, and David Kelley, founder of the Stanford d.school and of IDEO, becomes his advisor. Undergrads usually can't take d.school classes, but Evan finds a way. He also gets into the business school class on entrepreneurship and venture capital, co-taught by Eric Schmidt. Guest speakers include Meg Whitman and Biz Stone. Scott Cook of Intuit meets Evan there, and is so taken with him that he hires him as an intern. David says you could tell this kid had talent, years before Snapchat.

That's the pattern in this whole story. David points out the Entrepreneurship and Venture Capital class was his favorite course in business school. Evan wasn't even enrolled. He audited it, and by sophomore year he had a design advisor from the d.school and an internship offer from Intuit. For a kid who hadn't shipped anything yet, that's a good network.

The summer after sophomore year, Evan and Bobby try again with Future Freshman, a site for high schoolers navigating college admissions. It goes nowhere too. Ben jokes that the story feels familiar, a failed idea before the big idea, college friends, somebody claiming it was really their idea. David says the similarities to The Social Network are eerie.

Hold that thought, because the legal drama is coming. Ben notes the founders even might have used a very similar law firm.

Then, April of twenty eleven. Evan's junior year. A fraternity brother, Reggie Brown, runs up with an idea. He'd sent a picture to a girl the night before, and thought: what if pictures could disappear? Evan, according to court filings, gets very excited and calls it a million dollar idea. Evan denies saying the million dollar part. Bobby, now graduated and working, comes back as chief technology officer.

Notice how much of this story we know from court filings and interviews. David says it's all there in the record, which is why the episode spends so long on the history. You couldn't script it better.

Here's a fun detail. Evan submits the app as his project in a Stanford product design class. They call it Peek-a-Boo. At that point it isn't even a phone app. It's a website where you click a choose-file button. On the final day, a panel of venture capitalists judges the projects, and the app goes over, in David's words, like a lead balloon. According to an interview with Evan, one judge suggested they talk to Best Buy about a partnership.

They keep going anyway. By the end of that summer, they have a hundred and twenty-seven users. Ben, who's built consumer apps himself, says those numbers are terrifying when everything you read about has insane engagement. And then comes a big argument about equity splits, and Evan and Bobby push Reggie out. Ben's lesson to founders is the same as David's: have the equity discussion early.

David adds a dry caveat: unless your strategy is truly Machiavellian and you want to push your co-founders out. Otherwise, pro tip, have the equity talk early, before anyone feels they've already earned more than the rest of the group thinks.

Then the app basically goes on ice. Evan goes back to Stanford, Bobby takes a job at another startup, and a cease and desist arrives from another company called Peek-a-Boo. They rename it Snapchat. Reggie Brown reportedly came up with the ghost logo, before he was out.

Now comes the lucky break, and it's wonderful. Evan's mom tells her niece, a high schooler in Orange County, about the app. The niece downloads it and starts using it with friends. Her school had given students locked-down iPads with no iMessage. Ben adds that Facebook was reportedly blocked too. Which is ironic, given what's coming.

Think about that. The reason Snapchat got installed on those iPads is that Facebook was blocked. The kids needed another way to communicate, and Evan's cousin's app was it. The kids needed some way to text each other during the day, and Snapchat worked on an iPad. It sweeps through the school, then Orange County, then up the state into Northern California and Silicon Valley.

The numbers. December of twenty eleven, just over two thousand users. January, twenty thousand. April, a hundred thousand. David says it's the proverbial hockey stick, and the product barely changed. Ben says it's almost like Snapchat didn't find the market, the market found it. David says, literally, Evan's mom found the right market. Ben warns founders not to learn too much from this. It's such an outlier that copying it is like chasing a unicorn.

David also notes that Snapchat was by then the biggest tenant on Google's cloud, and at a hundred thousand users they suddenly had real hosting costs. A hockey stick is wonderful, until the invoice arrives.

Time to pay the server bills. Lightspeed puts in four hundred and eighty-five thousand dollars, at a four point two five million dollar valuation, about ten percent of the company. Evan, the story goes, refreshes his Wells Fargo app in class, sees the money land, says goodbye to his professor, and drops out of Stanford months before graduating. He moves to Los Angeles, Bobby follows, they stay at Evan's dad's house, and friends drop out to join them. In October of twenty twelve they launch on Android, serving twenty million Snaps a day. In December they add video.

And that same December, Mark Zuckerberg emails Evan. Facebook has already been building a clone called Poke. Zuck flies down to meet Evan at Snapchat's headquarters, and walks in with a plan. He lays out how he'd build Snapchat and how he sees Evan's role in the world. Then he ends with: we're launching Poke next week. Ben says this is not an offer to buy them. It's "we are going to crush you."

Ben says Facebook's Poke was actually a very good clone, from a user experience perspective. It revived the name of an old Facebook feature that nobody uses anymore. David jokes this is not the acquisition offer you've been looking for.

Evan, as the story goes, hands his whole team copies of The Art of War. Ben thinks that might be overdramatized. They watch Poke launch. And here's the twist. The buzz for Snapchat from Poke's launch is enormous. Evan reportedly called it Merry Christmas for Snapchat. Ben's lesson: when a big company launches your feature, it can raise awareness of your product rather than kill it. He saw it with his own to-do app when Apple launched Reminders, and with Instapaper.

Ben calls the fear Sherlocking, after an old Apple search app that put third-party search tools out of business. His to-do list app was sure it was dead when Reminders launched. Instead, the launch taught people they needed a to-do list, and some didn't like Apple's. Instapaper saw sales spike when Apple added its read-later feature. Don't be afraid of the big guy launching your feature.

The Poke launch also legitimizes Snapchat. Until then, David says, if anyone talked about it, it was just for sexting. Ben remembers getting it and realizing his friends were sharing candid, casual pictures they'd never put on Facebook.

Then things move fast. February of twenty thirteen: sixty million Snaps a day. A thirteen and a half million dollar Series A from Benchmark at about seventy million dollars. Matt Cohler at Benchmark spotted it first. He was an early Facebook employee, and his first deal at Benchmark was Instagram. David calls this Shakespearean. And the same month, Reggie Brown, now back home in South Carolina, sues and claims a third of the company. It settles out of court, reportedly for hundreds of millions, though nobody knows.

Ben says it's one court case that didn't leak. David says thank you to that court case anyway, because we got this great story out of it.

April: a hundred and fifty million Snaps a day. June: an eighty million dollar Series B led by IVP at eight hundred million dollars. That's more than ten times the Series A valuation, four months later.

People thought it was crazy. Plenty called it a fad. In August, Facebook got desperate and added a feature letting you post to Instagram from Messenger, as if that would take Snapchat down. It didn't. Ben admits he thought the product was a fad, easy to clone over a weekend. David says: young Ben, you hadn't yet learned the power of network effects.

In September, three hundred and fifty million Snaps a day. In October, Stories launch, which both hosts call game-changing. And in November twenty thirteen, the offer. It leaks to the Wall Street Journal that Mark Zuckerberg offered three billion dollars in cash. Nobody knows who leaked it. Zuckerberg reportedly alleged it was Evan's tactic. Evan said Zuckerberg might have done it to hurt Snapchat's fundraising. Ben points out that three billion dollars is three Instagrams, and WhatsApp hasn't happened yet. So it looked preposterous at the time.

Think about Zuckerberg's position. Less than a year earlier, David says, he flew to Los Angeles and told Evan how Snapchat should be built, and that Facebook was going to build it anyway. Then he's back offering three billion dollars in cash. Ben says the question isn't whether it's crazy. It's why Facebook needs it. Facebook's holy grail metric is engagement, and Snapchat is the perfect storm of more engaging. But Facebook's core business is owning your attention, and Snapchat is more engaging.

Fast forward to May of twenty sixteen. About a hundred million daily active users, roughly a tenth of Facebook. Nearly a billion Snaps a day, about ten per user. And sixty percent of daily users create a Snap every day. That blows up the old rule that ninety percent of users lurk. David says it's because Snapchat made creating stupid easy.

Ben compares that with a Reddit-style site, where ninety percent just read, nine percent comment, and one percent create. Snapchat flipped that. Ben also admits he used to think the look, that dark bar behind the text, was hacky and amateur. Now, he says, they should put it on billboards, because it's their brand.

On product, both hosts say Evan is an incredibly talented product visionary. The app opens in camera mode. Snapcodes made adding friends simple. Vertical video ads fit phones. David cites a profile reporting that in Evan's company, saying "let's see what Google or Facebook did" can get you fired. The same profile says it's notoriously hard to work for Evan, with lots of turnover.

David admires the refusal to follow the playbook. Ben adds that the app opens straight into the camera, which is a very different design from the usual table view and navigation bar. Once you learn it, it's like its own language. The first time, though, people say, I need my kids to teach me.

Now the category. Ben says that at the time of the offer it would have been a product acquisition. But today Snapchat is many products, with its own ad sales needs. David says it'd be a new category: a wholly independent company inside another company.

What would have happened otherwise? Ben thinks Facebook would have tied Snapchat to Facebook login and run it on the same ad dashboard, with the same targeting. But Snapchat went the other way. Evan has promised no creepy targeting, no tracking users, and no data collection. David says it's become the anti-Facebook. Ben notes that advertisers can target only location, age, and content channels, and get reporting only on how many people opened the Snap. There are no links. So it's useless for conversion-based advertising, but a killer platform for brands like Coca-Cola. Ben's big argument: advertising stays between one and one point four percent of GDP, brand advertising is bigger than direct response, and most brand money is still on television. So can Snapchat be the television of the future?

David says YouTube is the best place for brand advertising online right now, but people skip pre-rolls after five seconds. Snapchat's ads are ten seconds and immersive. Ben says if you can captivate me for ten seconds, I'm good. He also loves that Snapchat has no feed to finish, so it doesn't make him feel like a completionist who missed something. Ten-second ads you don't want to skip, and an average of thirty minutes in the app. Ben's image: Instagram is a museum, Snapchat is a crowded bar. David asks who spends more time in museums than bars.

A few more details from the hosts are worth keeping. On the product side, David says the whole look and feel is intentional, even when it seems janky. Ben says the interface is like its own language and grammar, and once you learn it, you get incredibly fast. And Ben notes that Snapchat bought the technology behind its QR-style Snapcodes, which he calls the first time he's seen QR codes used effectively in the wild.

There's also the question of how Snapchat will make money. Ben points out that, as of this recording, it's pre-scale on revenue, and the valuation rests on a promise: that it can pull brand dollars away from television. David adds that Snapchat's ad platform is so primitive that an advertiser can't do any real conversion tracking, and Ben says that's exactly the point. It's one-directional, television-style advertising, with no links to click.

Last, a note on tone. These early episodes are loose, and the hosts say so. They apologize for running long, tease each other about grades, and invite listeners to join their brand new Slack community to argue about it. It's a good reminder that one of the best podcasts in tech started as two friends experimenting in public.

Their tech trend: start small, nail one audience, then grow. Ben adds Paul Graham's idea that startups can keep climbing hills without local maxima, and admits he's not sure he agrees, but likes it as inspiration. David adds one more: it's a dirty little secret that there's not much real innovation in Silicon Valley, and copying the playbook can be dangerous.

Time for grades. The question is whether Facebook should have walked away at three billion. Ben gives Facebook an F. They needed to offer more. He says Snapchat is valued at about sixteen billion dollars on paper, and it's projected to earn about three hundred million dollars in revenue this year. David is divisive. He gives Facebook an A. His argument: Snapchat and Facebook can't coexist. Evan would walk in and basically say, I stand for everything you are not. Bid it to fifteen or nineteen billion dollars, WhatsApp territory, and you'd get culture clash and an implosion. Poke, he says, was recreated pixel for pixel and didn't work inside Facebook.

Ben doesn't change his grade, but he admits David has a point. He says that's why we need new startups, to create value incumbents never could. They skip the Carve-Out and send you to their new Slack community. A reminder that this was the early days of Acquired.

And a lesson for an operator like you. Facebook had the money, the users and the clone, and still lost the thing it needed, because culture and product vision can't be bought. Sometimes the right move is walking away.
