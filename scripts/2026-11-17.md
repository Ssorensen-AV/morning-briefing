The first idea is about freedom. Kennedy says freedom doesn't mean chaos. It has to come with self-restraint. He recalls Thomas Jefferson's line about the tree of liberty, and says each generation has to defend rights the founders won, because forces start chipping away at them the moment they're signed.

The second idea is about the Cold War, and it's personal. Kennedy remembers the Cuban Missile Crisis as a boy. He says around day eight, U.S. Marshals came to his house to take the family to an underground bunker in the Blue Ridge Mountains, while his father, Robert Kennedy, slept at the White House with the crisis committee. He also claims that military brass wanted a nuclear exchange with Russia, while the Soviets were still behind. That's his account, and historians debate how close the brass pushed.

The third idea is about the C I A and the press. Kennedy says the Church Committee hearings in nineteen seventy-three revealed a program called Operation Mockingbird, with at least four hundred journalists secretly working for the agency. He argues the practice never really ended, and says it shaped how Americans hear about the war in Ukraine. Those are his claims, and Lex pressed him on the mechanism. Kennedy's answer was, quote, through the press.

Here's the quote I'd keep. On what he wants from Washington, Kennedy said, quote, it's not our business to change the Russian government. He argued that regime change in a country with more nuclear weapons than America is, in his words, irresponsible.

How has it aged? Mixed, and I'd be careful. His prediction that Ukraine couldn't win has been argued over ever since. And his presidential run didn't end as a Democrat. That's well documented. ## Acquired: PayPal

Today's Acquired episode is number eleven of two hundred seventeen, from May of twenty sixteen. The topic: eBay buying PayPal. The hosts, Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal, call it an older deal that set the tone for modern technology acquisitions. Ben opens by apologizing for a month with no episode. He'd had knee surgery. David points out that Ben recorded the last one five days after. Ben says he's now fully off the painkillers. Stay away from the narcotics, kids.

Let's start in December nineteen ninety-eight. Peter Thiel, Max Levchin, and Luke Nosek found a company called Confinity. Their idea: payments and cryptography for Palm Pilots. The first version of what we now call PayPal was money beamed between handheld organizers.

Here's the problem. That market is tiny. So in nineteen ninety-nine, they pivot to a money transfer service they call PayPal.

Meanwhile, another founder is circling. Elon Musk, fresh off founding and selling Zip2, starts a company in nineteen ninety-nine called X dot com. It's an online financial services and email payment company. Ben's comment: domains were cheap back then.

In March of two thousand, the two companies merge. The story is that Michael Moritz at Sequoia, an investor in Confinity, helped orchestrate it. Neither confirmed nor denied, says David. Musk starts as CEO, then Thiel comes back as CEO of the combined company. In October of two thousand, with Musk in the chair, they make a call. PayPal is doing so well that the whole company should focus on it. No more Palm Pilots. In two thousand one the company takes the name PayPal. Ben calls it classic Elon, doing what he does best: focus.

In February of two thousand two, PayPal goes public. This is just after the dot com crash. David notes it's the first consumer internet company to go public in eighteen months. It closes the first day at a market value of about one point two billion dollars. Just a few months later, in July, eBay announces it will buy PayPal in an all-stock deal. Depending on how you count, that's between one point two and one point five billion dollars, about a twenty percent premium over the prior day's close.

Here's the twist the hosts keep returning to. Within four years, over half of PayPal's employees were gone, including all the founders of both Confinity and X dot com. Then, in July of twenty fifteen, under pressure from activist investor Carl Icahn, eBay spins PayPal back out as its own company. When it starts trading, PayPal is worth about one and a half times eBay. Today, they say, it has a market value of about forty-six billion dollars.

So the question for the episode: was it a good deal for eBay, given where PayPal ended up?

David sets the scope up front. This episode is about the first act: the founding, the sale to eBay, and the early years afterward. The spinoff, and PayPal's own life as a buyer of other companies, could each be an episode of their own. There's, in his words, at least one more in the tank.

There's also a fun footnote on who ran PayPal inside eBay. It was something of a hot seat. Scott Thompson was president, then left to become CEO of Yahoo. Very quickly it came out that his résumé had been embellished. David recalls that he claimed a computer science degree he didn't have. Marissa Mayer arrived at Yahoo after him. Next up as president was David Marcus, who had come in through eBay's purchase of his payments company, Zong. He now runs Facebook Messenger.

The hosts frame the analysis this way. Assume an eBay shareholder ends up with one share of PayPal and one share of eBay, as happened at the spinoff. Then ask how PayPal fed the marketplace, how PayPal grew, and whether it would have grown that way without eBay.

Start with why PayPal sold. Ben points out that eBay transactions generated about two-thirds of PayPal's payment volume. PayPal had found its fit, but mostly as a service for eBay sellers. David mentions a line often attributed to Peter Thiel, that PayPal was essentially an app built on top of eBay. That's a precarious spot for a company.

And eBay had options. It was building its own payment product, called Billpoint, in partnership with Wells Fargo. It could have cut PayPal off. But the hosts say that carried regulatory risk. And, as David puts it, what PayPal built was really hard. They'd been classified as a money transfer service, not a bank. They had to make people feel safe sending money over a very young internet. And they had to beat fraud. Ben says there were bodies buried all over the place of payment services that couldn't keep a lid on it. David adds a great detail: PayPal's fraud detection technology became the inspiration for Palantir, which Thiel started with some of the core engineers. Fraud detection is just the sort of job machine learning is built for.

Now, about the eBay market at the time. Only about forty percent of eBay transactions were settled electronically. That means sixty percent weren't. Ben wonders aloud whether people were mailing checks. The hosts aren't sure. Meanwhile, Meg Whitman, then eBay's CEO, said she hoped the electronic share would rise dramatically. David says he was a high school freshman, watching classmates trade internet stocks between classes. Ben was selling Beanie Babies on eBay. He made about five hundred fifty dollars. A Jerry Garcia bear went for three fifty, a fish for a hundred, and an inchworm for another hundred. David remembers that one.

So what category of deal was this? David calls it an accidental business line. He suspects eBay's executives saw a feature. Payments were critical to the marketplace, they'd tried to build it themselves, and these guys did it better, so buy them. Then the feature turned out to be a bigger business than eBay itself. Ben agrees, with a twist. It was a control play, the Apple way: own the key pieces of the experience. But what it became, he says, was a behemoth nobody saw coming.

Then David raises a classic tech saying: you can't build a really big company on somebody else's platform. PayPal both proves and breaks that rule. It grew huge on eBay's back, and had to sell because it was so captive to the platform. He wonders if you can do it when you pull users off the host platform into your own world. He cites Pinterest, which used Facebook invites. Ben offers LinkedIn, which grew by emailing your address book, and later settled a class action for about a hundred million dollars. David adds Airbnb, which in its early days posted to Craigslist to pull supply and demand over. The trick, he says, is to take the users and keep them in your ecosystem. PayPal couldn't easily do that in its early years, since eBay was almost the only use case.

What if eBay hadn't bought it? Ben goes back and forth. PayPal probably never would have left eBay. It was the cash cow. And he can't imagine eBay losing. David offers one negative case. Over the next thirteen years, PayPal grew to account for sixty to seventy percent of the combined value, and all of the growth. Could eBay have recruited and kept top talent without that sexy growth business attached? Would it have stagnated sooner? Ben's view: if eBay doesn't buy PayPal in two thousand two, it buys it later, at a different price. Higher or lower, nobody knows.

Now the part David says he was most excited about: the PayPal Mafia. He argues that this single acquisition was the catalyst for much of Silicon Valley as we know it. Why? The people at PayPal were young and ambitious, and had just been through something hard and exhilarating. Then eBay came along, with a complete culture clash. They hated it, and they left quickly, still young and hungry.

The list is remarkable. Companies founded by PayPal alumni: LinkedIn, Yammer, Yelp, YouTube, Palantir, SpaceX, and Tesla. Reddit isn't founded by them, but Yishan Wong, a PayPal alum, was its CEO for a while. Then there are the companies where Mafia members were early investors or advisors: Facebook, Uber, Airbnb, Square, Pinterest, and Stripe. David's point: practically every major company Silicon Valley produced since can trace its history, directly or indirectly, to that group.

Ben jokes they'd love a guest, but everyone who was there is too rich to bother with a podcast. Then he corrects himself. It wasn't PayPal money that made them rich. PayPal had raised only about one hundred eighty million dollars, including the I P O, across a merger and lots of founders. Some got to the high tens of millions, maybe a hundred. Not hugely rich. It was what they did next.

And PayPal invented moves that now seem routine. Ben says it was the first to do refer a friend, get free money. Today Uber gives you fifteen dollars. PayPal literally gave you a dollar or five dollars, and emailed your friends to say, congratulations, you have free dollars. David says they invented viral acquisition and growth hacking. PayPal was among the first apps you could embed in other web pages. And YouTube, a PayPal offspring, won on embeds. Ben tells his own story. Years ago he built a t-shirt site for a friend with a unique PayPal button on each product page. No storefront, no inventory system. It worked, because they didn't sell many shirts.

Ben then draws a parallel to online advertising. Both are scale businesses with high fixed costs and thin margins, where you need massive volume before anyone takes you seriously. In payments, every account can send or receive, so each new user makes the network more valuable. It's a flywheel. David notes it's a recursive argument. Picture pitching Uber before it existed: drivers everywhere, riders everywhere, a car around the block in a minute. You'd be laughed out of the room. And the same with PayPal. Everyone has it, so everyone feels comfortable using it. How do you get there? The magic of PayPal, says Ben, is that they figured out how.

David closes the theme with a lesson from Jim Goetz, a Sequoia partner, speaking at Stanford. Goetz says what sets PayPal alumni apart is a willingness to put out a product that's far from finished, and iterate like mad on real market feedback, without kidding themselves about the signals. You can't start with the end state. You start with a very small idea and build from there. Ben adds the cost of getting attached. Every line of code you write is a burden you carry. He admires teams who ship, learn in days, rip half of it out, and do it again. David says the skill is holding two things at once. Absolute commitment to the vision, and no bias about how you'll get there.

Time to grade it. David points out the odd thing about this one. Their best grades have gone to acquirers who leave the team alone, as in Pixar, Instagram, and Bungie. This is the total opposite. eBay mucks around and everybody leaves. Ben wonders if it's the era. Back then, acquisitions were all about integration. It's only recently, he thinks, that companies learned to leave it alone, the Facebook style.

Then David reads a story from David Sacks, in an article from Tech Republic. After the deal, eBay held an integration meeting for the key PayPal people. Three hours long. They showed up with a one hundred thirty-seven page PowerPoint deck. The PayPal team said, we've never had a three-hour meeting in our lives. What is PowerPoint? Sacks said that if they stayed, they'd have to create a whole new department just to do PowerPoint. Ben says it's a product they needed and a team totally incompatible with their culture.

So David's grade is a B plus. It breaks all the rules, yet created billions in shareholder value, and spawned the whole ecosystem. Handled differently, eBay could have captured more. But then, he says, we'd all be poorer for it, because the exodus created the Mafia.

Ben grades through two lenses. As a marketplace feature, he doesn't think PayPal added tremendously to eBay's core business. He cites Keith Rabois, who says PayPal's leaders saw eBay's growth plateauing, and knew they needed to sell. eBay's net revenue was three point two billion in two thousand four, and it's seventeen point nine billion today. It's doing great. But Ben doesn't credit PayPal for that. Then the other lens: eBay as a company. Holy crap, absolutely, he says. They bought a second business line when the first wouldn't be enough, and it grew like a weed, ending up larger than eBay's original business. That's an A plus. David agrees the financial outcome is a massive home run. Ben adds that the deal was generally good for the world, and for U.S. G D P. David quotes the old line: what's good for General Motors is good for America. They even float a few comparisons. Maybe Instagram, though David says the jury is still out. Ben says Instagram is predicted to do three billion dollars in revenue this year, on a billion dollar purchase. Not too shabby.

One more hindsight note for you. The deal looks cheap today, but remember the setting. It was two thousand two, the dot com crash was fresh, and PayPal's only proven market was a single auction site. A company that might have been squeezed by its biggest partner got a home, a balance sheet, and thirteen years to compound. The founders got their exits and a head start on everything they built next. Few acquisitions leave that many winners behind, even the ones who walked out the door.

Quick takeaways for an operator. One: dependence is a risk, even when it's your best channel. PayPal's biggest customer was also its ceiling. Two: the hard parts, like fraud and regulation, make a moat. Three: culture beats integration decks. Four: iterate on real signals, and don't fall in love with what you've built. And five: your alumni may be your legacy. The people eBay lost became the story.

Then the Carve Outs. Ben recommends the Bill Simmons podcast episode with investor Chris Sacca. It covers Kobe Bryant investing in startups, the early days of Uber and Twitter, and how Sacca once made four million dollars day trading, then lost it all. David is reading Antifragile by Nassim Taleb, about a third of the way in. His summary: the opposite of fragile isn't robust, it's something that gets stronger under stress. Like a body that exercises, or a vaccine. He adds that Taleb jokes that to spread an idea, you tell everyone it's a secret and make it controversial. David ties it back: the PayPal guys didn't mind being disliked. They embraced it.

What's the hindsight? The episode is a decade old, and its framing holds up. PayPal was worth a fraction of what it later became, the Mafia story only grew, and the thirteen years inside eBay look more and more like an incubator. The hosts called it an accidental business line. Sometimes the best deals are the ones you didn't know you were making.
