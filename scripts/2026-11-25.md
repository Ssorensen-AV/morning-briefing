## AI Deep Dive: Greg Lukianoff

From the Lex Fridman archive, back in September of twenty twenty-three, here's Lex's conversation with Greg Lukianoff. He's a First Amendment lawyer and the president and CEO of FIRE, the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression. At the time, he was about to publish The Canceling of the American Mind, with co-author Rikki Schlott.

It's not an AI episode. But it connects to every conversation about who gets to say what online, and who decides.

Greg's definition of cancel culture is specific. It's the uptick, starting around twenty fourteen, in successful campaigns to get people fired, expelled or deplatformed for speech that would normally be protected. Plus the climate of fear that follows. He says that after twenty-two years in the field, he'd seen nothing like the number of professors being targeted.

Here's idea one. Free speech is about knowledge. He argues you can't know the world as it is without knowing what people really think. Censor people, and you don't change minds. You just push opinions underground, and you get what's called preference falsification: an inaccurate picture of the world.

Idea two is about who does the canceling. Greg says it's not just students. Administrators sometimes help. He tells the story of a Stanford Law event where a federal appeals court judge was shouted down. Ten minutes in, an administrator gave a prepared seven-minute speech suggesting free speech might not be worth the squeeze. Greg's view: after any shout-down, investigate whether administrators helped.

Idea three: he insists this is not one-sided. He says about a third of successful attempts to punish professors come from the right, and FIRE sued over Florida's Stop WOKE Act, and won.

He also describes FIRE's college rankings. Michigan Technological University was first. Harvard came dead last, with a score of negative ten point six nine, rounded up to zero.

Here's his line on the heart of it. Quote: "If the goal is the project of human knowledge, which is to know the world as it is, you cannot know the world as it is without knowing what people really think."

The conversation also gets personal. Greg speaks openly about a suicidal crisis in two thousand seven, and how cognitive behavioral therapy helped him. Lex ends on a note of hope: people are sick of not being able to be authentic.

Why does it matter? Whatever you think of his politics, the question is timeless. Today it's about AI moderation and what models are allowed to say, and the same tension applies.

## Acquired: Inside the M&A Press with Bloomberg's Alex Sherman

Today's Acquired is episode twenty-one of two hundred seventeen, from September twenty-seventh, twenty sixteen. It's a special one. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal drop their usual format and interview Alex Sherman of Bloomberg, who covers technology, media and telecom deals and hosts a podcast called Deal of the Week. So there's no single company to profile. Instead, we get a look at how deal news is made, and why you read about acquisitions when you do.

Start with Bloomberg itself. David calls it maybe the largest technology company nobody thinks about, because people see it as a media company. Michael Bloomberg founded it in nineteen eighty-one. He'd been a partner at Salomon Brothers, which got acquired, and he made ten million dollars in the deal. He used it to start a company called Innovative Market Systems. A couple of years later, mercifully, the name changed to Bloomberg. In nineteen eighty-three, Merrill Lynch invested thirty million dollars for a thirty percent stake. Bloomberg bought it back in two thousand eight, when Merrill was acquired by Bank of America during the financial crisis.

The core business is the terminal. Ben and David call it the WhatsApp for Wall Street, long before WhatsApp. It's branded hardware and software on a trader's desk. You make trades, chat with other traders, and run data and analytics. Each terminal costs about twenty-five thousand dollars a year, and roughly half a million people pay. Alex says the terminal business brings in about nine billion dollars a year. That money subsidizes the news operation, which is why Alex never wanted to leave for a newspaper that lives on advertising. Ads, Ben notes, are a hard business unless you're Google or Facebook.

Why does that matter for news? Because Bloomberg's incentives are different. Most outlets chase page views. Bloomberg's customers pay to trade on information. So the incentive is to be right. David says, your incentives are aligned with the truth.

Here's the first big idea: the fifteen-minute head start. Terminal customers got Bloomberg's news fifteen minutes before the public. Alex calls it a built-in paywall. If you break a deal and six billion dollars of market value appears instantly, that's the sales pitch. But the edge has eroded. Headlines hit Bloomberg TV right away, and anyone with a terminal can tweet them. Still, Alex says, so much trading is algorithmic that you only need to be first by a tiny fraction of a second. Bloomberg kept the delay.

The second idea is my favorite. The red headline. Bloomberg's biggest stories scroll across the terminal in red. Alex says that when a story gets the red treatment, stocks almost always jump, probably because algorithms are coded to react. So editors sometimes decide not to put a story in red when it's nuanced, like a company thinking about a deal but not in talks, or talks that have died. Ben's example is the rumored Apple and McLaren story. Bloomberg didn't redline its own version. But when the Financial Times published, Bloomberg did flag the FT's headline, because it trusts a short list of outlets: the FT, the New York Times, CNBC and the Wall Street Journal. If one of them beats Bloomberg to a story, Bloomberg still alerts clients.

Third, how a deal story actually gets sourced. Alex says Bloomberg's standard is direct sources on both sides of a deal. Not bankers who aren't working on it, not rival executives, not friends of an executive, and not traders with a financial interest. Everything is anonymous, so the job runs on relationships. His example is Synaptics, a California company targeted by a Chinese conglomerate over nine months. Alex wrote four stories tracking the whole cycle: interest, a lower price, and then the deal dying. Nobody else matched them. Traders emailed him saying he was being played. Alex knew his sources were directly involved on both sides, so he knew no one had an incentive to lie.

There are exceptions. If the Wall Street Journal and the Financial Times are chasing the same story, and Alex has a very strong source on one side, he'll give the other side a short window to comment. If they don't wave him off, he'll run it. The phrase "people familiar with the matter" is code. Usually it means both sides weighed in.

Here's some inside baseball. Look at the bylines. The first name is usually the reporter whose source supplied the main information. Take Verizon buying Yahoo. If the first byline is the Yahoo reporter, the information likely came from the Yahoo side. The last name is probably the reporter who called the other company to check. And timing? Alex says about eighty percent of the time, there's no strategy. The story went out when it did because he finally got lunch with the right person, then wrote it up, and had a parent-teacher conference in the morning. Only about twenty percent have a real reason for the timing.

Then there are the public relations firms. In M&A deals, companies hire outside firms, such as Brunswick, Sard Verbinnen, Joele Frank and Abernathy MacGregor. Many of their people are former journalists, hired to work out where leaks come from and to shape the story. Once a company is in a sale process, Alex often stops dealing with the company and deals with the firm. Yahoo already worked with Brunswick, so much of its communication ran through them.

How does Alex pick what to chase? He keeps a chase list built from tips from executives, bankers, lawyers, board members, consultants, private equity people and PR contacts. He writes two to three stories a week. Name recognition and size drive priorities. Bloomberg barely looks at deals under one billion dollars unless the name is well known. He might skip a three or four billion dollar deal if he's close to incremental news on Yahoo. Ben jokes that if you want to bury a small acquisition, announce it on the day Verizon does something big.

Now here's the question Ben and David came to ask. Why doesn't anyone cover deals after they close? Alex gives two reasons. It's hard, because you have to dig into culture and financials, and after a deal many numbers stop being public. And it isn't actionable. Bloomberg tells its reporters to spend roughly eighty percent of their time breaking news on live deals, and twenty percent on their own projects. Alex says the public mostly wants the headline: who bought whom, for how much. Do we need a story to know Facebook buying Instagram worked? No. We just know. That's also why Acquired's retrospective approach is different. David compares it to getting dopamine from news versus eating a plateful of vegetables.

Alex has a teaser for his own show. His team plans oral histories of deals around their anniversaries, and maybe a counterfactual, a deal that didn't happen. Ben says that's what makes Acquired's most popular episodes work. The Snapchat and Facebook acquisition that never happened keeps getting downloads, because it's a story, not a headline. David says the lesson is the difference between news, stories and analysis.

Now the tech trend section, and Alex asks the hosts a question. Social media companies like Twitter are valued on user growth. What happens when growth stalls? Does subscription come next, the way newspapers moved to paywalls? Ben says there's a catch. A newspaper doesn't need network effects, but Twitter does. If your friends leave because they won't pay, it's worth less to you, so you leave too. Alex counters with a thought experiment. If you had to pay a penny to stay on Facebook, would you? His whole life is on Facebook. David notes WhatsApp charged one dollar a year after a free first year. David suggests Twitter could do what Bloomberg does: sell its data fire hose, valuable to financial customers, while the public stays free. Alex warns about annoying power users.

Alex tells a great personal story. When Comcast was going to buy Time Warner Cable, he had the story sourced at nine thirty at night. But it was night. His editors were home, and he had to find the right people, who had to approve his sources, edit four paragraphs, queue headlines and decide on a red headline. While he did that, CNBC's David Faber just tweeted the news. Alex had it at least forty-two minutes earlier. His lesson: with Twitter, a reporter with a good reputation doesn't need the institution to move markets. But he does need the paycheck. Kara Swisher and Walt left to start Recode, and then got acquired by Vox. The Information charges several hundred dollars a year. David's point is that independent reporters don't have the red headline, the direct channel to customers.

He also confesses to a mistake. Years ago, he tweeted that Shutterfly had hired Catalyst to sell itself, thinking the story had been published. It hadn't. The stock jumped five percent. For about nine seconds, he had front-run his own story. Bloomberg then changed its policy: reporters can't tweet a story until it has a link. And then the link was changed so only terminal subscribers could see it.

What's the advice for startups? Build a relationship, but know the reporter's job. Bloomberg covers Uber, not a startup with a small valuation. Ask the reporter what matters and give them that. For Bloomberg, it's exclusive information. For others, it might be a sit-down interview. Alex adds a trick: split your news into pieces and give each outlet its own exclusive. David jokes that in venture capital, that's called a party round.

Let me circle back to a few things I skipped. First, a detour on Snapchat. Snapchat is one of Acquired's most downloaded episodes, the one about the deal with Facebook that never happened. Alex says he's thirty-four and can't figure out how to use it. It's the first product that made him feel aged out. David says that's depressing. But Alex sees a pattern. He'd just met the executive team at Line, the Japanese messaging company. In Japan, Line has become a whole ecosystem. You order a cab through it. You order meals through it. Other apps live inside it. Ben calls it basically an operating system. Alex thinks Snapchat is heading that way, and that Facebook is trying, although Messenger is still separate from the main feed. David points to the new iOS ten, which puts an App Store inside iMessage. So the question for the hosts is whether the Asian messaging model comes to the United States.

Second, Alex talks about covering media at a media company. Three years on the media beat gave him a neat perk. If he needed a source from Time Inc., he'd walk three rows over to someone who used to be the managing editor at Time. When he moved to covering all technology, media and telecom deals, that network came with him.

Third, a few more details on how he works. He says almost all of it is on background. Nothing is on the record. That's why the relationships matter so much: the chairman of a board, a chief executive, a corporate development executive, a lawyer, a consultant. He says Verizon's interest in buying AOL was a scoop he broke with his colleague Scott Moritz, out of the blue, before the Yahoo story. Once it was out, everybody chased it. And Yahoo was different. Yahoo eventually went public with the fact that it would sell itself, and then the whole world was on the story. For Alex, that meant Yahoo became the priority.

Fourth, on the economics, David makes an interesting point. Alex's incentives, he says, are the opposite of a typical publication's. A typical publication wants a story to look splashier than it is. Bloomberg's customers pay to trade, so a splashy but wrong headline costs them real money. If others overhype a rumor, Bloomberg may even choose to downplay it. Ben calls it not being the boy who cried wolf. Alex says it's why Bloomberg is a good place to work. You go home feeling that your only obligation is to be as right as possible.

Last, Ben and David admit something about their own show, and it's a nice human moment in a very inside-baseball hour. Ben says they've checked their numbers about three times since launch, looking at the future of Acquired, which is still a side project for both of them. They looked at other tech podcasts and wondered how big theirs could get. But it's niche: people in M&A, or in startups who hope to be. Alex's phrase for that is a trade publication. Alex has the same problem with Deal of the Week. His latest episode featured Rob Kindler, Morgan Stanley's global head of M&A, and Rob's brother, the stand-up comedian Andy Kindler, hoping comedy fans would tune in. Ben says the stories are what bring people in. He's heard listeners say they weren't interested in M&A, but a friend told them to hear the story.

Alex also admits that after he joined as an intern in January of two thousand eight, he took meetings with nearly every Bloomberg competitor over the years, and never left. His reason is simple: the terminal business pays for everything, so he can focus on work that serves the core customer, not a side project. Ben and David say the episode gave them a new way to read every deal headline, and David adds that the coded language in articles is easy to skim past until someone explains it. That's the real value of this one, and it's why Alex was a perfect guest for a show about deals.

Then the show's regular segments. Follow-up on Instagram, their benchmark A-plus deal. It announced over five hundred thousand active advertisers, up from two hundred thousand in February. Ben credits integrated ad tools: check a box, upload assets, and boom, an Instagram ad. Amazon's share price hit eight hundred dollars. Ben jokes that the way to play it is Seattle real estate.

The hot take is Ford buying Chariot, a San Francisco shuttle subscription service. Ben isn't sure what the play is, compared with General Motors buying Cruise. Alex adds that Bloomberg is obsessed with self-driving cars. Alex then calls Apple like the mafia in how it keeps partners quiet. And he flags the Yahoo breach: five hundred million user accounts exposed in a hack from twenty fourteen, which could lead Verizon to push to change the price. David, a former investment banker, notes merger agreements have material adverse clauses, and this might qualify. Ben cites the Sony hack as a reminder of how costly breaches can be.

Carve Outs. Ben recommends a Burning Man twenty sixteen drone video on Vimeo. David picks Algorithms to Live By, by Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths. And Alex picks a New York Times column by Ross Douthat on late-night hosts swinging left, and the gap between culture and politics.

So what are the takeaways for an operator? One, incentives shape information. Bloomberg's paying customers want accuracy, so the newsroom is built around it. Two, a distribution channel plus trust is worth more than any single story. Three, the headline is not the whole story, which is exactly why Acquired exists.
