## AI Deep Dive: Preston Stewart and Michael Kofman on the War in Ukraine

This week's Lex Fridman episode, number five oh four, is a late-night conversation about the war in Ukraine, now in its fifth year. Lex's guests are Preston Stewart, a West Point graduate and Afghanistan veteran who covers the war on his own channel, and Michael Kofman, a senior fellow at the Carnegie Endowment and a leading analyst of the Russian and Ukrainian militaries.

First idea: it's a drone war, but not the way headlines suggest. Kofman says drones cause about eighty percent of losses. Yet he stresses it's a combination: minefields, artillery, and drones working together. Drones can't fly well in wind and rain, so artillery covers the bad weather. And there's a kill zone, roughly fifteen kilometers deep on each side of the line, where almost anything that moves gets hunted.

The numbers surprised me. Kofman estimates eight to ten thousand small first-person-view drones launched per day, on one side alone. But the viral videos only show successes. In practice, it takes about five drones to hit one person. A tank usually takes thirty to forty. One heavily armored Russian tank took seventy-nine. And Kofman says the war is far less autonomous than people think. Most drones are flown by hand, with teams of two or three people behind each one. He argues the AI hype is ahead of reality.

Here's a detail that'll make an AV person smile. Because jamming works against radio signals, both sides now fly drones on hair-thin fiber optic cables, spooled out behind them like a fishing reel, at distances up to about fifty kilometers. You can't jam a cable. Soldiers carry a detector that looks like an old Game Boy and shows the enemy drone's video feed, so they can watch whether it has spotted them. Kofman says tactics and counter-tactics now turn over about every three to four months.

Second idea: bad information on the Russian side. Both guests say Russian maps claim territory ten to fifteen kilometers beyond what's actually held, sometimes based on a single soldier waving a flag for a drone. Stewart mentions a faked AI video of one such claim, which Russians themselves called out. Kofman's point is that this isn't just propaganda. Fire support and troop rotations get planned around lines nobody holds, and soldiers die because the map is wrong. He adds that control means being able to use terrain, not just stand on it. In his words: "You have presence, but you don't really have control."

Third idea: why Bakhmut mattered less than it seemed. Kofman describes what he calls Bakhmut syndrome: defending a city past the point where it makes sense, because it has become a political symbol. He notes it took Russia another year to reach the next town over, which suggests the city's fall changed little.

Fourth idea, and it's an uncomfortable one for the United States. Stewart notes that the U.S. military is built around air superiority: bombers and precision munitions delivered with little fear of being shot down. In Ukraine, neither side can fly freely over the other, so you get what he calls air denial, and the drones fill the gap. His point is that this war isn't a template for every war. But Kofman adds a warning. Armies tend to draw lessons from the late, positional phase of a long war and assume the next war will open the same way. Still, our opponents are watching and planning around how this one is being fought. Kofman quotes William Gibson: the future is already here, it's just not evenly distributed.

There's also a fascinating stretch on the Wagner mutiny of twenty twenty-three. Kofman argues Wagner was dismantled, but its tactics spread. Russia copied the idea of replenishable assault groups, many staffed by convicts, and it worked for a while, then hit diminishing returns because the army over-specialized in small, grinding attacks.

And on the economics: Kofman says Russia is spending about forty-four percent of its government budget on the war, around nine to ten percent of its economy. He calls it an economy slowly consuming itself, though still able to fight into twenty twenty-seven. Ukraine, for its part, depends heavily on Western money, weapons, and intelligence.

The conversation ends on a lighter note. Both guests tell the story of a Ukrainian unit that used a heavy drone, fitted with a pod, to fly a wounded soldier out from the front, with a crew talking to him the whole way. Stewart's takeaway was simple: it's what gave him hope.

Where do they land? Kofman says this has been Ukraine's best year on the battlefield since twenty twenty-three, and Russia's worst since winter twenty twenty-three. But he's cautious about calling a winner. Russia's strike campaign on Ukrainian infrastructure makes the winter uncertain, and he doesn't see Russia willing to negotiate yet. Even a ceasefire, he warns, could just be a pause before a third round.

Why does this matter? The combination of cheap mass, rapid iteration, and fragile communications is a preview of how technology reshapes any contested environment. Worth the listen, if you have a few hours.

## Acquired: Siri

One note up front. This early episode of Acquired, from December of twenty fifteen, has no published transcript. So I'm telling the story of the deal itself, from well-established public facts, not putting words in Ben and David's mouths.

The episode covers Apple's purchase of Siri. And it's a great story, because Siri was a company, an app, and a feature, in that order. Most people remember only the last one.

Before we go further, let's set the scene, because the timing explains a lot. In two thousand seven, the iPhone had just arrived, and the App Store opened in two thousand eight. Suddenly, hundreds of millions of people were carrying a computer with a microphone, a camera, and an always-on data connection. But the interface was still a grid of icons and a tiny keyboard. Voice technology existed, but it had a bad reputation. Dictation software from companies like Nuance had been around for years, and it needed training, patience, and a quiet room. Phone menus that said "press one for sales" had taught a generation to hate talking to machines.

What changed was the cloud. Speech recognition got dramatically better once the heavy lifting could happen on big servers instead of on the device. Google was pushing voice search on phones. Microsoft was working on voice, too. And the research behind Siri had a different angle: not just hearing the words, but modeling what the person meant, and which service could fulfill the request. That second part, understanding intent, is what DARPA's money had paid for over many years.

Now, let's go back to the beginning. Siri's roots are not in a garage. They're in a research lab. In the early two-thousands, the United States defense research agency, DARPA, funded a huge artificial intelligence project at SRI International, a research institute in Menlo Park, California. It was called CALO, which stood for Cognitive Assistant that Learns and Organizes. The goal was a personal assistant that could learn from you and help you manage your work. It was one of the largest artificial intelligence projects ever funded in the United States, and hundreds of researchers worked on it.

It helps to know what SRI was. SRI International began as the Stanford Research Institute, and it has a remarkable track record as a place where ideas are born and then spun out. Its researchers worked on the early ARPANET, and a team there, led by Doug Engelbart, gave the famous demonstration in nineteen sixty-eight that showed the world the computer mouse, hypertext, and video conferencing. It built Shakey, one of the first mobile robots that could reason about its surroundings. It later spun out the surgical robotics company Intuitive Surgical, and the speech technology that fed Nuance. So when SRI said it had an assistant worth turning into a company, it had a history of being right about the future, a bit early.

Out of that project came the people who would build Siri. Adam Cheyer, an engineer at SRI, was a central figure in the assistant work. Tom Gruber, a well-known artificial intelligence researcher, was part of the story too. Chris Brigham, named in the episode notes, was among the early team. And then came Dag Kittlaus, the man who would become the face of the company.

Kittlaus is Norwegian. He spent years in the telecom industry, including time at Motorola, where he worked on mobile software. He had a sharp instinct about where phones were going. He saw that people were going to use phones for everything, and that tapping through menus was a poor way to get things done. He thought you should be able to simply ask. He joined SRI as an entrepreneur in residence, met the CALO team, and saw that the research could become a product.

So in two thousand seven, SRI spun the technology out as a new company, called Siri. The name, by the way, is said to come from Kittlaus himself. It's a Norwegian name, and he had planned to use it for a daughter. That story is widely repeated, so I'll pass it along as one of those company-name tales.

The big idea of Siri was different from voice search. Voice search turns your words into a list of web links. Siri tried to understand what you wanted, and then do it. Book a table. Find a taxi. Buy tickets. It connected to services like OpenTable, Yelp, and Fandango, and acted as a go-between. The founders called it a virtual personal assistant. Today we'd say it was an early agent.

The company raised money from respected investors. Menlo Ventures and Morgenthaler Ventures backed it early. Later came a round with Horizons Ventures, the firm of Hong Kong billionaire Li Ka-shing. Reported funding totaled in the tens of millions of dollars. That was serious money for a company with no revenue.

Siri launched as a free app in the iPhone App Store in February of twenty ten. It got great reviews. Early users were delighted, and it was clear it could be a big thing. The company had plans for other phone platforms, too, which would turn out to matter.

Now here's the twist. Apple moved fast. Within weeks of launch, in April of twenty ten, Apple bought Siri. The price was never officially announced, but it was widely reported at around two hundred million dollars. Steve Jobs was reportedly personally involved in the deal. Think about that for a moment. A tiny startup launches an app on Apple's own store, and Apple buys it before it can spread to its competitors. That's a classic Acquired lesson: sometimes the most valuable thing you buy is the denial. Siri on Android would have been a gift to Google.

Let me add one more point about the deal itself, because it shows how Apple operates. Apple doesn't usually buy products. It buys teams and technology, then folds them into its own roadmap, and it rarely explains itself. Siri is probably the most famous case where the acquired product became a headline feature of the flagship device. Most of Apple's acquisitions, like the ones listed in the episode, disappear into the operating system with no fanfare. That secrecy has benefits. Rivals can't see what Apple is building. But it has a cost, too. Outsiders, and sometimes insiders, couldn't tell whether Apple was serious about getting the assistant right until years later.

There's also a competitive angle that's easy to forget. Siri's founders had planned to bring the app to other platforms. If the app had spread to Android, Google would have had a ready-made rival to its own search, at a moment when search on phones was becoming the big prize. Apple's purchase turned a possible threat into an exclusive. It's a reminder that in technology, the best acquisitions are sometimes defensive.

After the deal, the standalone app was shut down. The team went inside Apple. And then, for a while, not much seemed to happen. Siri took more than a year to appear in the iPhone.

It finally arrived with the iPhone four S, in October of twenty eleven. Siri was front and center in the keynote. And the timing was haunting: Steve Jobs died the very next day. Siri was one of the last big products he had a hand in.

Now let's talk about the Apple side. The executive in charge was Scott Forstall, senior vice president of iOS software. Forstall was a Jobs favorite, with a reputation as a driven and ambitious leader. Siri sat in his organization. But Forstall's time at Apple ended in October of twenty twelve. He left after the troubled launch of Apple Maps, which was widely seen as a failure. Tim Cook announced that the iOS software would move under other leaders, including Craig Federighi, and Eddy Cue would take over Siri and Maps. So Siri lost its champion just as it was taking its first steps.

Meanwhile, the founders left. Kittlaus, Cheyer, and Gruber all moved on, and they later started a new company called Viv Labs, with a more ambitious vision of an assistant that could write its own code to answer questions. Samsung bought Viv in twenty sixteen. Gruber stayed at Apple for some years before leaving. So the people who built Siri went on to build what was, in effect, Siri two point zero, and sold it to a competitor.

The episode also looks at the cluster of small acquisitions Apple made around Siri. They show where Apple was heading. Apple bought Topsy, a company that analyzed Twitter data and search, in twenty thirteen. It bought Novauris Technologies, a speech recognition company. It bought Cue and Spotsetter, which were about personal assistant features and location-based recommendations. It bought a company called OttoCat. And, in the fall of twenty fifteen, shortly before this episode, it bought VocalIQ, a Cambridge, England startup working on conversational dialogue, and Perceptio, which focused on running image recognition on the device itself.

Look at that list, and a pattern appears. Apple was buying speech, language, search, context, and on-device learning. All of it was meant to make Siri better. These are small deals, often just a few million dollars each, and Apple didn't announce most of them. Apple's standard statement was that it buys smaller technology companies from time to time, and generally doesn't discuss its purpose or plans.

Apple also hired talent. The episode mentions Alex Acero, a leading speech scientist from Microsoft Research, who joined Apple to lead Siri's speech work. That was a big signal. Apple had decided that to fix Siri, it needed to build real speech and machine learning expertise in-house. Over time, Siri's voice recognition did improve a lot, thanks in part to neural networks.

Now, let's step back and ask: was the Siri deal a win? In the short term, yes. Apple got a headline feature that sold iPhones, and it kept the technology from rivals. The hosts, in December of twenty fifteen, were discussing speculation about the future of voice in everyday computing. At that moment, the future looked bright. Amazon had just launched the Echo, in late twenty fourteen, and voice was suddenly a real product category.

But with hindsight, the story is mixed. Siri was first, and then it fell behind. Amazon's Alexa, Google Assistant, and later chatbots built on large language models all passed it in capability. Siri became known for misunderstanding people and for being unable to hold a conversation. Part of the reason was structural. Apple prized privacy and on-device processing, and it was slow to adopt the kinds of data-hungry cloud approaches that rivals used. Part of it was organizational, as the project was passed between leaders and teams. And part of it was that the original Siri vision, an assistant that actually does things across many services, was hard to deliver on a platform that controls how third-party apps behave.

The latest chapter makes that clear. In the last couple of years, Apple has been rebuilding Siri around large language models, and by reports it has leaned on outside AI technology, including a deal with Google, to power parts of it. Whether that finally delivers on what Kittlaus and Cheyer imagined in two thousand seven is still the open question. Apple bought the idea early. Delivering it took far longer than anyone expected.

Let's talk about what happened to Siri as a product, because the numbers tell a story too. After the iPhone four S, Siri spread to every new iPhone and iPad, and then to the Mac in twenty sixteen, the Apple Watch, the Apple TV, and the HomePod speaker in twenty eighteen. Apple has said, over the years, that Siri handles billions of requests a week. That's a staggering scale. But scale and delight are different things. For many users, Siri became the thing you used to set a timer or start music, and not much more.

Apple tried to widen the product. It opened Siri to third-party apps through a developer kit in twenty sixteen, and added Shortcuts later. It added a more natural voice, and moved some processing onto the device to protect privacy. And in twenty twenty-four, Apple announced Apple Intelligence, which promised a far more capable Siri that understood personal context. That plan was delayed, and Apple acknowledged the delay publicly. For a company that prides itself on polish, a delayed flagship assistant was an uncomfortable moment.

So what can an operator take from this story? Here are three lessons.

Lesson one: buying is not the same as building. Acquiring a great team and a great product is the easy part. Integrating it, keeping its champions, and giving it room to evolve is the hard part. Apple lost the founders within about a year or so of the iPhone launch, and the product stalled.

Lesson two: a champion matters. Siri's sponsor at Apple, Forstall, was pushed out. Without a powerful advocate, a feature drifts. Any of us who have launched a new system inside a big organization know that feeling. If the project has no owner with clout, it quietly becomes everyone's second priority.

Lesson three: being first is a head start, not a moat. Siri had a multi-year lead, and a huge installed base. Competitors still caught up and passed it, because the underlying technology kept changing. The moat is the rate at which you keep improving, not the day you launch.

Before we leave the lessons, one more thought for operators. When a technology is almost good enough, there's a trap. Users get a taste of the magic, and then the first failures feel worse than if the product had never promised anything. Siri's early demos set expectations very high. Every wrong answer reminded people of the gap between the promise and the product. If you've ever rolled out a control system or a conferencing setup that worked beautifully in the demo room, you know exactly how that feels. The lesson is to ship what works reliably, and to be honest about what it doesn't do yet.

And here's a bit of humor to close the loop. Every one of us has, at some point, asked Siri to call someone and watched it dial the wrong person. That's the legacy of a two hundred million dollar acquisition: a product so close to the future that we keep forgiving it for not quite being there.

That's the Siri story. In the end, it's a tale about timing and patience. Apple saw the future early, bought it cleanly, and then took more than a decade to catch up with its own idea. For anyone building technology, that's both comforting and cautionary: being early is hard, but staying ahead is harder.

To recap the arc: a DARPA research project, a Norwegian entrepreneur, a quick sale to Apple, a famous product launch, and a long road to getting it right.
