## AI Deep Dive: Mohammed el-Kurd

Today's deep dive is from the Lex Fridman archive, episode three ninety-one, released in July of twenty twenty-three. Lex's guest is Mohammed el-Kurd, a Palestinian poet, writer and journalist. Lex introduces him as offering a very different perspective from his earlier episodes with Benjamin Netanyahu and Yuval Noah Harari. This is a political conversation, not an AI one, and these are Mohammed's views, which Lex pushes on throughout.

Mohammed grew up in Sheikh Jarrah, a neighborhood in East Jerusalem. He tells it this way. His family was expelled from their home in nineteen forty-eight, and his grandmother ended up in Sheikh Jarrah in nineteen fifty-six, in housing set up by the United Nations and the Jordanian government. Starting in the nineteen seventies, settler organizations claimed the homes. Families there fought in court, and he says they received more than fifty expulsion orders. In his telling, the legal fight was never a simple real estate dispute. He calls it a political battle over demographics. He argues that Israeli courts took the settlers' documents at face value, while refusing to look at documents from the Jordanian government, the United Nations and the Ottoman archives. Israel's government has framed these cases as property disputes. That framing is exactly what he rejects.

The second idea is about words. Lex presses him on language like hate and violence, and on whether his rhetoric helps. Mohammed says the label of hate is loaded, and that Palestinians living under occupation are asked to answer for their anger while the conditions behind it go unexamined. Lex asks about Hamas rocket fire and about Israel's future, and Mohammed pushes back on how those questions are framed. They don't settle it, and they don't pretend to.

The third idea is the role of culture. He talks about the writer Ghassan Kanafani, assassinated in the nineteen seventies, as an inspiration. And he talks about his own book of poems, called Rifqa, after his grandmother. He tells a story about his mother, also a poet, who would read her poems to his father each morning, and his father would guess which lines the Israeli military censor would cross out.

Here's one short, clearly attributed line, from the hope chapter. Mohammed says, quote, what gives me hope is believing in the inevitability of justice, end quote. His argument is that no injustice in history has lasted forever, and that views once called extreme often look different in hindsight.

Why does it matter? Whatever you think of his conclusions, it's a long, direct exchange between two people who disagree, and you hear his reasoning in his own words. Lex also admits he's not an expert on all of this, and he asks tough questions from several sides.

## Acquired: ExactTarget, acquired by Salesforce, with Scott Dorsey

This is episode fifteen of Acquired, from July of twenty sixteen. It's a rare one. Ben and David don't just retell a deal, they interview the founder who lived it, Scott Dorsey, the co-founder and CEO of ExactTarget. Scott is also Ben Gilbert's cousin, and Ben actually interned at ExactTarget one summer in college. So this one's family.

Here's the setup. In December of two thousand, the dot-com bubble had just burst. Venture funding had dried up. And three first-time software founders, none of them with a technical background, moved to Indianapolis to start a tech company. Scott Dorsey, Chris Baggott and Peter McCormick. Scott had just finished his MBA at Kellogg and was working at an internet incubator in Chicago called Divine. Chris Baggott was his brother-in-law, they'd married into the same Indianapolis family, and Chris had the big idea. Apply database marketing to the internet, and use permission-based email to help small businesses get to know their customers. Chris was so passionate that Scott sold his house, put two little kids in the car, and drove to Indiana.

Did Silicon Valley fund them? No. Scott says they spun their wheels with venture capitalists in Indianapolis and Minneapolis and got nowhere. A lot of those early venture firms simply couldn't picture this becoming a large business. So the first round was friends and family. About two hundred thousand dollars, from Scott's parents, his brother, his father-in-law, and, Scott says, pretty much all of Chris's neighbors. Chris went door to door and collected five thousand dollar checks. They kept the amounts small on purpose, so a failure wouldn't ruin Thanksgiving dinner.

Here's the twist. The investors who put in five thousand dollars and held on all the way to the Salesforce deal ended up with well over a million. Scott says pools and home renovations started popping up all over Chris's neighborhood.

The three founders worked without salary for the first six months. Then they found Bob Compton, an accomplished venture investor and tech entrepreneur, who became the lead angel investor, Scott's mentor, and chairman for the first seven or eight years. His backing gave the company the credibility to raise more.

Now, the way they built the early business is one of the best parts of the episode. This was before cloud computing, before Amazon Web Services. They had to buy servers and rack them themselves. And they built their first sales team out of friends whose dot-coms had failed. They were independent contractors, commission only, no salary, paid in equity and commissions. They had to find the lead, build the pitch, close the deal, collect the money, and implement the customer, and only then did they get paid. Scott says that when the company had forty-four employees, twenty-six of them were in sales. As he puts it, that's heavy.

One more detail on the founders' habits. Scott says the three of them were all sales and marketing people, and they described themselves as marketers building software for marketers. Everybody sold, and everybody spent time with customers. Version one of the product was driven entirely by the founders, and from there the customers shaped it. That closeness, he says, was one of their real strengths.

The early customers weren't big names. They were restaurants, dry cleaners and pizza shops, paying about a thousand dollars a year. But two moves changed the shape of the company. First, they built a channel program for digital agencies, who built websites and wrote copy but had no email tools. Agencies could rebrand ExactTarget's platform, and that opened the door to Fortune five hundred customers, like General Mills and Home Depot, when ExactTarget was still tiny. Second, they moved up to franchise organizations, where a parent brand controls the content but each franchisee gets autonomy. That parent and child structure became the foundation for serving enterprises.

Scott had been a Salesforce customer from day one, and he studied how they built. One platform, one code base. Every feature had what he calls a switchboard, so a small shop paying a thousand dollars a year and a giant paying seven or eight figures used the same software. David notes that venture investors are usually terrified of that mix of customer sizes. Scott says it's what let them engineer their way up the market while competitors underestimated them. There was pull-through, too. Small businesses become big businesses, and marketers move from small companies to large ones. One of their biggest customers ended up being Groupon, which came in through the small business inside sales team when it was barely getting started.

Money followed. In two thousand four, about ten and a half million dollars from insiders. In two thousand six, another seven million, with revenue around forty million dollars. Scott points out that each round mixed new money with secondary shares, so founders and early employees could take a little off the table. He says that mattered. It let them sleep at night, and it made them hungry to go the distance instead of selling too early.

Then comes the part that David calls high drama. In December of two thousand seven, ExactTarget filed to go public. It had forty-eight million dollars in revenue, it was profitable, and it had raised only six million dollars in primary capital. Then the markets collapsed. And because this was before the JOBS Act, the company's financials sat in a public filing for more than a year, where every competitor could read them, while ExactTarget had to report every quarter as if it were already public. Scott's line: all of the burden and cost and pressure of being public, with none of the benefits.

In early two thousand nine, they pulled the IPO. And here's the best decision of the story. Instead of going public, they raised one hundred forty-five million dollars, led by Battery and Scale, with TCV coming in later, much of it secondary. Internally, they called it better than IPO. Scott says staying private let them keep investing, because the public markets want margin expansion, and ExactTarget wanted to move beyond email into a full digital marketing platform.

Now watch the timing. Sequoia had just sent its famous deck telling portfolio companies to cut headcount by about twenty percent. As competitors pulled back, ExactTarget hit the accelerator, with a plan they called Accelerate twenty thirteen. It invested in engineering and built a sales organization three or four times larger than its nearest competitor. Growth went from about thirty percent in the two thousand eight and two thousand nine period, to the forties, and then the mid-fifties.

They also went international in a clever, low-risk way. They found reseller partners first, then bought them. Three Augusts in a row. First the reseller in the United Kingdom, then Australia, then Sao Paulo in Brazil. Across its history ExactTarget did six acquisitions, three for products and three for geography. The product deals included iGoDigital, in predictive analytics, and Pardot, a business-to-business marketing automation company in Atlanta that it bought for about one hundred million dollars.

ExactTarget finally went public on the New York Stock Exchange in March of twenty twelve. The IPO was priced at nineteen dollars a share, came out at about twenty-three, and the company was valued at north of a billion dollars. Scott says the IPO was heavily oversubscribed. And for five quarters, life was good. They met or beat the plan every quarter, and they were growing north of forty percent.

Then the phone rang. Marketing was becoming the hot battleground for the biggest enterprise software companies. Salesforce had a close relationship with ExactTarget, as a partner and as a customer, and Scott says they'd integrated with Salesforce before its AppExchange even existed. Salesforce had bought Radian6 and Buddy Media and had a social-first strategy, but its customers wanted more. They wanted multi-channel, they wanted data, and they wanted something that served consumer marketing, not just business to business. Salesforce knocked on the door.

Scott says that being a public company made it an entirely different level of process. A board subcommittee ran it with experienced advisors. David points out that a public company must disclose the play-by-play to the SEC, and that filing reads like Shakespearean drama, with other bidders named only as Party A, Party B and Party C. Salesforce's first offer was twenty-six dollars a share. The final price, in June of twenty thirteen, was thirty-three dollars and seventy-five cents a share, about two and a half billion dollars, roughly a fifty percent premium over where the stock had been trading, in the low twenties. Scott's take is that you maximize value from a position of strength, when you don't need to sell and several parties want you. Or, as David puts it, great acquisitions happen when companies are bought, not sold.

How did it work out? Scott says ExactTarget brought about three hundred million dollars of recurring revenue, headed toward four hundred, plus a consumer-oriented data architecture and a broad platform across email, mobile, social and web analytics, and it fit beautifully with Pardot as well, which gave Salesforce a way to compete with Eloqua and Marketo. And a product called Journey Builder, which stitches the channels together, has been embraced by Salesforce. David says it's a business line acquisition, and Salesforce still reports it as the marketing cloud line.

There's an Indianapolis ending too. Salesforce had about fourteen hundred employees downtown, planned to add eight hundred more, and the city's tallest building was going to be renamed Salesforce Tower. And Scott says the company finally got a nonstop United flight from Indianapolis to San Francisco right around the deal.

My favorite anecdote involves Microsoft. ExactTarget was built on Microsoft technology, and Microsoft became a customer, replacing an internal email system that employees didn't like. The two teams set a joint goal for the cutover, and when they hit it, they threw a party on the Microsoft campus, under a huge tent, with food, beer and wine, and they retired the old servers with sledgehammers. Everybody strapped on goggles and beat them to pieces.

Why sell at all, when things were going so well? Ben asks exactly that, and it's the question every founder gets. Scott can't say much about the board's private modeling, but he describes the shape of it. The directors did a lot of financial thinking about what life as an independent company would look like. A fifty percent premium is a big number to turn down if you don't expect to reach that market value for many years. Scott says the answer was yes to both of David's questions. ExactTarget could have fit with other big software companies, and being part of Salesforce was the right call. He says they're very happy with how it played out.

David raises the category question too. Look at the marketing technology players of that era. Responsys, Eloqua, Omniture and ExactTarget all got swallowed by bigger enterprise companies. Oracle bought Eloqua. Adobe got Omniture early. Could a few of them have stacked together into something bigger? Scott's answer is that the logical connection point, email plus digital channels plus analytics, got pulled into Adobe early, and that ExactTarget kept moving deeper into data and the web, trying to build one robust platform that made all the channels work together. That's the thinking behind Journey Builder.

And there's the email question. Scott laughs that if he had a nickel for every time someone told him social media would cannibalize email, he'd be a rich man. Even in the middle of the social media boom, he says, email is the most powerful tool a marketer has, and as e-commerce explodes, email gets more relevant, not less. The catch was that people kept thinking of ExactTarget as just an email company, and it didn't always get credit for being a broad omnichannel platform. Also, Scott admits, growth gets tougher with big numbers. At some point you're near the top of an S-curve.

Then David gets to his tech theme, and it's a good one for an operations manager. For decades in enterprise software, buying was tightly controlled by the chief information officer and the IT department. Then the cloud opened it up to business units. Now, he says, every employee is a buyer. You can start on a free tier, put it on a credit card, and spin up a Slack channel without asking anyone. David's worry, and the investor's trope, is that big incumbents like Salesforce win because of their relationships with the CEO and the CIO. Scott agrees that's real. It's one big reason the Salesforce deal worked so well, because they came with all those trusted relationships. But he says there's still plenty of room for new entrants, because the innovator's dilemma is alive and well. Cloud platforms let you go from idea to prototype quickly, and if you charge less than a thousand dollars, nobody needs the chief information officer's signature.

He even points to a company he's excited about at High Alpha that manages software spending, a software-as-a-service platform for managing your software-as-a-service applications, aimed at the chaos of overlapping subscriptions. High Alpha has two sides. A studio that plans to start eight to ten new companies over three or four years, and a venture capital fund.

Look at the numbers with hindsight. In twenty twelve, its last full year as an independent company, ExactTarget had about two hundred ninety-four million dollars in revenue. Three years later, the business sits inside Salesforce as a marketing cloud line that the company talks about constantly. David says he's on the fence about the grade partly because it's hard to separate what ExactTarget did from what Salesforce bolted on afterward. Scott's answer is that integration is remarkably difficult, and that most acquisitions fail at it, which makes this one stand out.

So what grades did the hosts give? David and Ben both landed on an A minus. Ben compared it to the true top-tier outcomes, like Instagram and Pixar, which are the bar. And Scott, who admits he's biased, gave it a full A, on both the Salesforce side and the ExactTarget side. He called the odds inconceivable: starting a software company in Indianapolis with humble expectations, then selling it for two and a half billion dollars, with every employee holding equity. And he said every person who ever invested, from friends and family to venture capital, did well at thirty-three seventy-five a share.

Scott's own next chapter is High Alpha, a startup studio in Indianapolis. And as a postscript, David noted that week that Instagram had passed five hundred million registered users, with three hundred million using it every day. Three out of every five, every day.

The operator's lessons are worth stealing. Listen to customers, because as Scott says, they'll reveal your product roadmap. Build the channel early. Stay capital efficient. And when the market says run, sometimes the better move is to hit the accelerator.
