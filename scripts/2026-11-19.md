## AI Deep Dive: Larry Lawton, Lex Fridman Podcast number five oh five

Today's Lex Fridman episode is a little different. Lex talks with Larry Lawton, one of the most prolific jewelry thieves in US history. The FBI caught him after a six-year hunt, and he spent more than a decade in prison, much of it in the hole. Now he runs a program that keeps at-risk kids out of prison, and he hosts a YouTube channel breaking down real crimes and how realistic the heists in movies and games are.

The first part is about Grand Theft Auto six, set in Miami, his old stomping ground. Larry's big idea: heists aren't about technology. They're about people. People are habit driven. He cased stores for weeks, watching delivery times, neighbors, even the angle of the sun on the windows. In his words, quote, "You're not robbing technology, you're robbing people." Unquote. He says the trailer's security looked unrealistic, and he's dying to see whether Rockstar shows the casing.

The second idea comes from solitary confinement. Larry says the torture wasn't the box, it was not knowing when it would end. With a date, you can survive. Without one, he says, you think you're going to die. He watched people crack, and he credits fighting back, even suing the Bureau of Prisons, with saving his life.

The third idea is how people read a room. Larry says the first two years in prison, you're in shock, looking over your shoulder, working out who's friend and who's foe. After a while, he says, he could feel tension before anything happened. And he says the hard times taught him what he can handle. He even notes that you can go from being a millionaire to eating raw ramen noodles, with water to make them expand in your stomach, so it's a swing that tests you.

Lex also shows off a bit of dry humor. When Larry tells him about a Russian inmate who beat four chess players without looking at the boards, Lex just asks, "But could he chess?"

Why does it matter? For anyone who runs operations, it's a reminder that systems fail where habits are predictable. Fair warning, the conversation is raw and uncensored.

## Acquired: LinkedIn

Now for Acquired. This is episode fourteen, from June sixteenth, twenty sixteen, and it's a hot take. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal recorded it just two days after Microsoft announced it was buying LinkedIn. They had no idea how it would turn out. It's speculation, with some very good reasoning. And as a bonus, we know how it ended.

Before the deal, there's a bit of housekeeping, and a bit of follow-up on earlier episodes. Ben tells listeners that the show may change its flavor. He jokes it could become a show about tech acquisitions in general, the ones that went well, the ones that didn't, and anything with a good story. David says they've never had so many requests on Slack and email as they did for this one. The Slack group, by the way, had just passed one hundred people.

On follow-up, Ben Thompson tweeted that the LinkedIn deal and Apple's developer conference were the second and third most important announcements of the week, behind Snapchat launching its advertising API. David says time will tell. And Ben describes tapping what looked like a Facebook Instant Article that expanded into a sleek native ad, with his email and phone number already filled in, so he could join a waiting list. His read is that ad products don't get much airtime, but there's real innovation there.

Okay. Let's start at the beginning. The story starts with the PayPal Mafia. PayPal was acquired by eBay in July of two thousand two. Less than six months later, on December fourteenth, a group of former PayPal people, led by Reid Hoffman, formed a new company and called it LinkedIn. David thinks it was the very first progeny of the PayPal Mafia.

They moved fast. They launched a minimum viable product in May of two thousand three. Social networks were hot. Friendster had raised money from Benchmark, and Reid Hoffman and Mark Pincus had both put money into it. MySpace was growing quickly. Facebook hadn't even started.

Here's the problem every network faces. How do you start from cold? LinkedIn's answer was the infamous trick of scraping your address book and emailing everyone in it. It was sneaky, and it later cost them a lawsuit, reportedly for about one hundred million dollars. Ben and David point out that this is a recurring theme. Airbnb used Craigslist to bootstrap its supply. Ben compared it to Microsoft, which once wrote per-processor licensing into its contracts and crushed competitors before the Justice Department made them stop. When you face a chicken and egg problem, you need an unfair advantage. It doesn't always have to be illegal, but often it turns out that it was.

By late two thousand three, LinkedIn raised a Series A from Sequoia, four point seven million dollars, a lot of money right after the dot-com bubble burst. Mark Kvamme was the Sequoia partner. Michael Moritz later took over the board seat. Then in two thousand four came the Series B from Greylock. Years later, Reid open-sourced that pitch deck, with candid commentary. He admitted he was nervous because the company had no revenue. The pitch was that LinkedIn would be the unbiased source of truth about professionals. Old directories were full of bad incentives, with people making themselves look good. A network could fix that.

Now, here's the twist about how LinkedIn makes money. It's not mainly ads. David walked through three business lines. Talent solutions, for recruiters, was about sixty percent of revenue. The full recruiter product ran about nine hundred dollars a month per seat. Marketing solutions, mostly ads. And premium subscriptions, about twenty percent of revenue, for people like venture capitalists, salespeople, and business developers. Total revenue was about three billion dollars a year, with roughly one hundred six million active users and about four hundred million registered users.

Ben pointed to a post by Josh Elman, who worked at LinkedIn and later joined Greylock. His point is that you can't judge LinkedIn the way you judge Facebook or Twitter. Are LinkedIn's users really that much more valuable? They're not very engaged. But they're monetized differently, because LinkedIn sells an extremely high-value product, which is access to those people. Ben noted that the cheapest premium plan was around a hundred dollars a month, so think about that every time a recruiter sends you a message with that little yellow badge.

That's why people who say they never visit the site miss the point. LinkedIn's own IPO prospectus warned that a substantial majority of members don't visit monthly. But LinkedIn doesn't make money when you visit. It makes money from your professional data being up to date and findable. And recruiters need it. Ben said that if a company doesn't buy LinkedIn Recruiter, its recruiters are hamstrung from day one. It's table stakes.

In two thousand seven, Reid stepped aside as CEO. Dan Nye, from Intuit, took over, and didn't last two years. David also found a funny detail in the Series C. One of the investors was the European Founders Fund, which turned out to be the Samwer brothers, the people who run Rocket Internet and copy American businesses. They even copied Founders Fund's name. In December of two thousand eight, Jeff Weiner became CEO, and he still was, at the time of this recording.

LinkedIn filed for its IPO in January of two thousand eleven and went public in May. It priced at forty-five dollars a share and closed the first day at ninety-four twenty-five. It was a watershed. LinkedIn was the first of the new wave of big internet companies to list, before Facebook, Pandora, and Twitter. The stock climbed past two hundred dollars.

Then came February fifth, twenty sixteen. LinkedIn and Tableau both reported fourth quarter results. LinkedIn beat expectations, but its guidance for the year came in lower than Wall Street wanted. The stock fell forty-three point six percent in a single day. About ten billion dollars of market value vanished. Sales Navigator, the product meant to open a new growth channel, was slower than expected. And it hit the whole software sector that Friday. New Relic and Zendesk dropped twenty-three percent. HubSpot twenty percent. Workday sixteen. Salesforce itself, thirteen. David called it carnage.

Then, on Monday, in what David called one of the best-kept secrets in major deal history, Microsoft announced it would buy LinkedIn for one hundred ninety-six dollars a share. That's twenty-six point two billion dollars. About half of what LinkedIn had been worth a year earlier.

There's important background. About a year earlier, rumors swirled that Microsoft had offered between fifty and fifty-five billion dollars for Salesforce. Salesforce wanted seventy. Microsoft walked away. Then LinkedIn sank, and the pieces came together. The letter of intent was reportedly signed about a month before the announcement, and it all started after that February drop. Kurt DelBene, a friend of the show who'd appeared in their Acompli and Wunderlist episode, was leading the integration. Ben thought the leadership team named in the press release, spanning Azure, Dynamics, Office, and Bing, hinted at where LinkedIn would go inside Microsoft.

Closing was expected within the year, which Ben guessed meant late December. Both hosts said they couldn't wait for the regulatory filings, because those would reveal how the deal really came together, including who else was at the table.

The hosts categorized it as a product acquisition. Ben said you don't buy this to cash flow it. It's an integration play. David agreed, but said it could transform an entire business line, and he saw it as a data acquisition too.

Ben gave four reasons Microsoft pulled the trigger. Reason one, identity. Microsoft's lock-in comes from Active Directory, which manages who you are inside a company. But people now change jobs every eighteen to thirty months. Your identity is not your employer's anymore. You lend your skills to a company. Ben called it the bring-your-own-device wave, but for people. LinkedIn holds the whole career history.

Reason two, sales. If you know a person's complete work history, you can sell to them better. That feeds Dynamics, Microsoft's customer relationship product. Ben quoted Ben Thompson of Stratechery, who argued that if Sales Navigator sold into a hundred percent of the Dynamics user base, a good portion of the deal would be paid for. David added that Sales Navigator struggled because sales runs on the CRM. Salesforce had become the operating system of business-to-business companies, and plugging in from the outside is hard. Microsoft could plug it in directly.

Reason three, scarcity. There's only one LinkedIn. You can't buy the other one or build one. David figured there must have been other bidders, and probably Salesforce. LinkedIn was advised by Frank Quattrone's Qatalyst. Microsoft by Morgan Stanley. At that point, they said, you think about the cost of letting someone else have it. Twitter buying Instagram would have been awful for Facebook.

They also noted how Microsoft paid. It was all cash to shareholders, but Microsoft borrowed a large part of it, because most of its cash was parked overseas, and bringing it home would mean roughly forty percent tax.

Reason four, the cloud. Windows is no longer the whole story. Microsoft is becoming a cloud services company, with Azure, Office three sixty-five, and now business tools for recruiting and sales. Ben noted that Microsoft's reputation for fumbling big deals was the common reaction from current and former employees. They weren't optimistic.

What would have happened otherwise? Probably a sale to someone else, likely Salesforce. David also raised a hidden problem. LinkedIn's stock-based compensation had exploded, from thirteen million dollars a quarter in twenty twelve to two hundred twenty-two million dollars in the first quarter of twenty sixteen. That diluted shareholders. Microsoft's ownership solved it.

Before the grades, there's a lesson in all this for operators, and it comes straight from the hosts. First, when a company is on sale, it isn't only the stock that's cheap. The whole company is cheap to a buyer who can do more with it. Ben said he understood this within a week of February's crash. Second, in a world of network effects, the question for a buyer isn't whether the price is high. It's what happens if a competitor owns it instead. And third, the hard part of any acquisition is never the signing. It's integration, culture, and execution afterward. Keep those three in mind as we get to the themes.

Now the tech themes. David said he'd long been a fan, and bought stock after the IPO and again after February. His case: very few real network effects exist, and LinkedIn's is among the strongest. The product, he admitted, looks bad. Ben joked about ten-second page loads. But nobody will beat it, because everyone you need to reach is already on it. Ben said the only way to attack it is vertical, like GitHub for developers, AngelList for startups, or Behance for designers. Building a better horizontal LinkedIn is a fool's errand, like building a horizontal video site.

David then raised a hope. LinkedIn's API was famously locked down, because the company feared people copying the network. Microsoft is a developer company with different motives. Could a real LinkedIn API be coming? Ben cautioned that venture capitalists are a niche market. And he asked a pointed question about platform versus product. Microsoft once held back Office for iPad. Would it integrate LinkedIn with Google and Salesforce too? David said he'd be really disappointed if Microsoft took the old-school approach. Ben: shots fired.

Now the grades. David gave it an A minus. His reasoning: a year earlier LinkedIn was worth twice as much, it's a uniquely defensible asset, but execution is everything. Ben went with a straight A. He said that in November twenty fifteen the stock was at about two fifty-five, while Microsoft paid around one ninety. The price is about eight times revenue, and comparable software companies traded at ten to fifteen times a year earlier. The risk is integration, and a twenty-six billion dollar write-down would be a gut punch. But Microsoft needed a product like this, and it couldn't build its own. Ben said he had faith in Satya Nadella's Microsoft, much more than in the Steve Ballmer era.

There's one more thread worth pulling. Ben and David both stress how closely the timing tracks the market. LinkedIn's stock hit its low point in February, and the sector-wide selloff meant the whole software industry looked cheaper. For a patient buyer with debt financing and a clear strategic reason, that's an opening. David added that LinkedIn's management had months to digest the deal, according to Jeff Weiner's memo to employees, which means the secret was kept both inside LinkedIn and inside Microsoft, where, as Ben noted, only a small circle of board members and executives, led by Satya, could have known. Think about how rare it is to keep a deal this size quiet for weeks while the whole industry watches.

And a quick note on why Ben thought a rival bidder was inevitable. When there's exactly one asset of its kind, bankers run a process, bidders show up, and the price rises. That a price this big could still land at about half the prior year's value tells you how hard the February reset hit.

One scale check from David. This was the biggest deal the show had ever covered, nearly the combined value of Pixar, Instagram, Twitch, Bungie, Siri, Lucasfilm, YouTube, and Acompli. Ben said don't look at it that way. Look at the cost of not doing it. David closed with a culture worry. LinkedIn is in Silicon Valley, and Microsoft hasn't historically done well with Valley campuses, but Kurt DelBene had told them Microsoft has a new mindset about where acquired teams sit.

The hosts also flagged why cultures matter here. LinkedIn has offices around the world, though it's centered in Silicon Valley. Microsoft, Ben said, has not typically done well with its Silicon Valley campuses. David's reply was a good dry one: fortunately, Alaska had just bought Virgin, so the flights between the two would be easy. Ben's answer was, that's a great place to leave that. And they did, and moved to the Carve Outs.

For the Carve Outs, both picked talks from the Code Conference. Ben chose Elon Musk, including his explanation of why the first-stage rocket lands on a drone ship, and his comment about the odds we're living in a simulation. David chose Jeff Bezos, who talked about asking what about your customers won't change in the next decade. David said it reminded him of LinkedIn. He was sure he'd still be using it twenty years from now.

So how did it turn out? Well, you now know the rest. Microsoft kept LinkedIn running as its own thing, with Jeff Weiner staying on. The hosts' grades were A minus and A. As Ben said, it all comes down to execution, and Acquired has always been about how stories like this actually play out.
