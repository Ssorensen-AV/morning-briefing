## AI News

First, spending is blowing past budgets. For context, most companies plan their software spending a year ahead. So when a line item grows this fast, a lot of finance teams are doing some very quick math. Five times more is the kind of growth that makes a CFO check the spreadsheet twice, and then a third time, and then sit down.

Second, the report looks at how Anthropic and OpenAI are capturing the market. The notes don't give the split, so I won't guess at it. But the direction is clear. A big share of that new money is flowing to the model makers themselves.

Third, a shift from seats to tokens. Here's the idea. For decades, enterprise software was sold per seat. One person, one license, one monthly fee. Tokens are different. For context, a token is a small chunk of text that a model reads or writes, and you pay for how much work gets done, not for how many humans log in. Think of it as moving from a gym membership to paying per mile. And when agents do the work, usage can climb much faster than headcount. That would go a long way toward explaining why budgets are getting blown.

## AI Deep Dive: Three Mathematicians on AI's Crisis in Math

Our deep dive comes from the freshest Lex Fridman episode, number five hundred and three, released October twenty-sixth. Lex calls it an emergency episode. Over a few weeks, AI systems reportedly claimed progress on some of the hardest problems in mathematics. So he gathered three experts. Steven Brunton, a fluid dynamics expert at the University of Washington. Ankur Moitra, a theoretical computer scientist at MIT. And Alex Kontorovich, a number theorist at Rutgers. Everything that follows is their account, and Lex himself jokes that forty to eighty percent of what's said today will be wrong in five years.

Idea one. The speed. Alex says that on a Wednesday he gave a talk arguing that AI could only build a layer or two on top of the big Lean library of formal math. Lean is software that checks every logical step of a proof, so you don't have to trust anyone. Two days later, he says, a roughly thirteen-million-line Lean proof of Fermat's Last Theorem dropped, from Anthropic's systems, with two or three humans mostly typing, quote, keep going. His reaction? Everything I said in that talk was wrong.

Idea two. The Riemann hypothesis. Alex explains it as signal processing for prime numbers. The zeros of one special function act like the frequencies of an orchestra, and the hypothesis says every instrument plays at exactly the same volume. What the AI reportedly proved is a quasi version. It rules out zeros in a wide strip, with real part between seven-eighths and one. Getting from there to the full hypothesis would need a new idea. Still, Alex says he never expected to see even that in his lifetime. He was at a gala in New York, whiskey in hand, refreshing his phone when it dropped.

Idea three. Navier-Stokes. These equations describe how fluids move. Steven says the proof builds a very special vortex that stretches and thins, like a figure skater pulling in their arms, until the flow blows up. Here's the twist. He says day-to-day engineering is almost unchanged. His line: we solved the million-dollar math problem, but there's a trillion-dollar engineering problem still waiting.

Idea four, and the heart of the episode. What's left for humans? Ankur offers the doorman fallacy. A hotel thinks a doorman just opens doors, so it installs a sliding door. It misses everything else the doorman does. Mathematicians, he says, don't just prove theorems. They build ideas and connections. Alex puts it more sharply. Math is not the fact, it's the why. He tells a story about a play where John von Neumann asks God whether the Riemann hypothesis is true. God says yes. Von Neumann asks how God knows. It doesn't end well for Johnny.

Now, is any of this genuinely new thinking? Here the guests disagree, politely. Alex describes a balloon. Inside it is everything humans already know, plus every combination of those ideas. A skeptic says the AI only moved around inside the balloon. Alex prefers a different picture. Think of tiles on a board, where each tile you place unlocks the next. The moves are known, but the places they can reach are enormous, and he calls the chain of moves in the Riemann result, quote, shocking. Ankur adds that it hardly matters. Once a result like that exists, he says, it changes which problems anyone bothers to work on. Steven likes the balloon for a different reason. Even if it only fills in the fog between known ideas, that's like giving mathematicians radar.

Then there's the computer science side. Ankur says one of the headline results was the Unique Games Conjecture, a famous question about how well hard problems can be approximated. He tells a great story. Hours before the results dropped, he asked the man who invented that conjecture whether he'd read a proof if one appeared. The answer was, quote, no. Ankur understands. Sometimes, he says, it feels like someone has stolen your thunder.

And one more, because it's fun. Ankur describes an experiment called subliminal learning. Tell one language model it loves owls. Have it generate nothing but random numbers. Then train a second model on those numbers. The second model starts bringing up owls. The idea is that tiny, hidden patterns can add up, the way coordinated taps can steer a Ouija board. Lex takes this to a comic extreme, an owl-obsessed swarm of agents. But the serious point stands. When these systems act at scale, we need to understand what they quietly pick up.

Steven offers a useful rule of thumb for that. Ask two questions. How easily can we verify the answer? And how bad is it if it's wrong? A wrong proof just gets thrown out. A wrong medical or safety decision doesn't. So he wants a firewall, meaning humans stay in charge wherever the stakes are high.

There's real worry too. Lex reads a lament from Terence Tao about patient, slow research being casually disrupted. Ankur says he feels excited, but intellectually lonely. Young researchers can't tell what to work on. Ankur also notes the labs reportedly tried thousands of problems and published only the wins, around a ten percent success rate.

The advice at the end is lovely. Ankur's version: pretend you've already won, then do what you would have done anyway. Steven adds that, for humans, family and community are the point, and everything else is a cherry on top.

Why does this matter to you? Because it's a preview. Machine-checked answers are cheap. Knowing which questions to ask, and what to trust, is the scarce part.

## Acquired: Pixar

A quick note first. This early episode has no published transcript, so I'm going to tell you the story of the deal itself, from well-established public facts. And since this is the very first Acquired episode, it's a fitting place to start. The question the hosts ask is simple. Disney bought Pixar in two thousand six. Was it a success? And if so, what made it work?

Let's go back to the beginning. Pixar didn't start as a movie studio. It started as a computer lab. In the late nineteen seventies, George Lucas set up a computer graphics group inside Lucasfilm. Ed Catmull ran it. Their goal was to push computers toward making real films.

By nineteen eighty-six, Lucas needed cash, partly because of a costly divorce. Enter Steve Jobs, who had just been pushed out of Apple. Jobs bought the group for five million dollars and put another five million into the company. He named it Pixar.

For years, Pixar was not a movie company at all. It sold expensive graphics computers, and the business struggled. Jobs kept funding it. The bright spot was a small team of animators led by John Lasseter. They made short films, including Luxo Junior, the one with the hopping desk lamp. It earned an Academy Award nomination, and a later short, Tin Toy, won the Oscar.

That lab also built the software that still matters today. Its rendering software, called RenderMan, became an industry standard for film effects. So even while the computers lost money, the technology kept earning respect. Jobs, to his credit, kept believing. He reportedly kept putting in his own money, year after year, long after most investors would have walked away.

Meanwhile, the creative side was learning something else. Lasseter showed that a computer-animated character could have personality, timing, even feelings. A lamp could look worried. That was the spark. The technology was the paintbrush. The story was the point.

That caught Disney's attention. In nineteen ninety-one, Pixar and Disney signed a deal for a feature film. That film became Toy Story, released in November of nineteen ninety-five. It was the first fully computer-animated feature. It cost roughly thirty million dollars to make and grossed about three hundred seventy million worldwide.

Here's the part that made Jobs a billionaire on paper. Pixar went public the week Toy Story opened. The shares priced at twenty-two dollars and jumped to about thirty-nine on day one. Jobs's stake was suddenly worth well over a billion dollars. Not bad for a company that had been losing money.

Next came the second deal, and this is where it gets interesting. In nineteen ninety-seven, Pixar and Disney agreed to a five-film partnership. Pixar made the movies and paid the costs. Disney handled distribution and marketing. Profits were split evenly. Pixar had the creative power. Disney owned the machine that got the films into the world.

Then Pixar kept winning. A Bug's Life. Toy Story Two. Monsters, Inc. Finding Nemo. The Incredibles. All of them were hits. Meanwhile, Disney's own animation division, which had soared in the early nineties with The Lion King, was fading. Its hand-drawn films were coming up short at the box office. Nobody could miss the contrast. Pixar was the hot studio, and Disney was the old giant that needed it.

There was also a quiet imbalance in the old deal. Pixar made the films, but under the original agreement Disney held the sequel rights to Pixar's characters. Pixar could see that its creations were helping build Disney's brand at least as much as its own.

Which brings us to the fight. Disney's boss at the time was Michael Eisner. He and Jobs did not get along. And there was a sticking point. Under the old agreement, Disney held rights to sequels for Pixar's characters. Jobs saw a risk that Disney could make cheap sequels without Pixar. In early two thousand four, Jobs announced that Pixar would look for another distributor once the current contract ended. Disney, in effect, was about to lose its best partner.

Here's the twist. Disney's board and shareholders grew restless with Eisner. In two thousand five, Bob Iger took over as CEO. And Iger did something Eisner never would. He made peace with Jobs, because he understood the real prize was not the distribution deal. It was Pixar itself.

In January of two thousand six, Disney announced it would buy Pixar. The price was about seven point four billion dollars, paid entirely in Disney stock. It closed that May. Jobs received roughly seven percent of Disney's shares, which made him the company's largest individual shareholder, and he joined the board.

The deal was about more than money. Ed Catmull became president of both Pixar and Disney Animation. John Lasseter became chief creative officer for both. Disney promised to keep Pixar's culture, campus, and independence intact. In effect, Disney bought a creative engine, then handed the keys to the people who built it.

So was it a success? Look at what followed. Pixar kept making hits, including Wall-E, Up, and Toy Story Three, which crossed a billion dollars at the box office. And Disney Animation came back to life under Catmull and Lasseter, with films like Tangled and later Frozen. Iger also used the same playbook again, buying Marvel in two thousand nine and Lucasfilm in twenty twelve. Pixar was the proof of concept.

It's worth asking how you'd judge a deal like this. There are really three tests. The financial test: did the purchase pay back the price? With the blockbusters that followed, the answer looks like a clear yes. The strategic test: did it fix Disney's weakness? Again yes, because Disney's own animation studio revived. And the cultural test: did the thing that made Pixar special survive being bought? That's the hardest one, and it's the one most acquisitions fail.

What are the lessons? First, culture is the asset. Disney did not try to absorb Pixar. It protected what made Pixar work. Second, the right leader matters. Iger saw what Eisner couldn't. Third, an acquisition can be about people, not products. And fourth, when you buy a creative company, the hardest part is leaving the creative company alone.

For a deal valued at seven point four billion dollars, that may be one of the best-aged acquisitions in Hollywood history. It's also a nice way to begin a catalog about great deals.
