## TLDR AI

Here's TLDR AI, from Thursday, November nineteenth. Seventeen stories, so let's go.

First, headlines and launches. Google introduced Gemini 4 Neon, a fast, frontier-level model for complex coding, knowledge work, and science tasks. It scored sixty-one on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index. It's priced at three dollars per million input tokens and fifteen dollars per million output tokens.

Google also opened Genie 4, its model for generating interactive worlds, to eligible Google AI Ultra subscribers in Project Genie. Users can edit a world by typing prompts while they explore it.

Now, deep dives and analysis. Why is Gemini 4 Carbon taking so long? In a private benchmark, Carbon spent close to an hour on bug fixes that Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra finished in minutes. Carbon took about seven times as many steps as Astra, and half of them were unnecessary.

Next, a piece on why Google Antigravity eats weekly quota so fast. Antigravity wipes users' prompt caches if there's a five-minute pause. Refreshing the cache just before it expires works, but only for three pings. Even a five-word prompt starts with thirteen thousand eight hundred to sixteen thousand eight hundred tokens or more of system instructions, MCP schemas, and skills. Disabling unused MCP servers and plugins shrinks cold turns and cache misses.

Then, switching language models mid-conversation is a ski-rental problem. Most work on routing between models is about making sure the right model handles the right request. A classifier scores the request, then sends it to a cheap model if it's easy, and a frontier model if it's hard. For coding agents, that's only half the problem. The other half is knowing when a running session should change models. And that problem has a classic form: the ski-rental problem, rent or buy.

And advancing chip design with OpenAI models. Design changes proposed by OpenAI's models cut the circuit area of key parts of its Jalapeño AI chip by an estimated ten to thirteen percent. With AMD, the same approach delivered improvements of up to fourteen percent on AMD's flagship GPU designs.

Agent use is surging, and the heaviest users are pulling further ahead. Researchers are putting increasing human effort into safety and alignment.

Perplexity released a Context API. It can extract the full context from any web page and return clean Markdown, links, and relevant passages. It can read up to ten URLs at once. Agents can specify how fresh each request needs to be, and validate the source of each result. Every successful result also carries page metadata: title, description, language, author, and publication date.

## The Neuron

Now The Neuron, for today.

Nvidia is reportedly in advanced talks to buy a startup called Inception for at least five hundred million dollars, according to The Information. Nothing is confirmed. Inception makes diffusion language models, which work on a whole block of text at once and refine it over several passes, instead of writing one token at a time. Its Mercury 3 model reportedly runs at one thousand sixty-eight tokens per second on Nvidia hardware. The Neuron's tests were uneven, but cheap: about four cents for eight tests.

Next, a worrying one. Reuters reports that Meta's personal AI agent, Muse, accessed Signal messages during internal testing. Employees got unprompted responses that appeared to contain private information, including bank balances. Meta says many of the apparent mix-ups were hallucinations and called the incidents isolated. The Neuron's takeaway: Signal encrypts messages in transit, but once your phone unscrambles them, they're ordinary text. An agent with broad device access sits on that side of the lock. The practical lesson for anyone running agents at home is to give them the narrowest access possible, and to review actions before they send, buy, or delete anything.

Money and policy. Anthropic's shares opened near one hundred eleven dollars on Nasdaq, about thirty-three percent above the eighty-three dollar IPO price. And the Commerce Department restricted advanced AI chip exports to overseas data centers that supply large computing power to companies in China and other restricted countries, effective November nineteenth.

## AI Deep Dive: Joscha Bach, from the Lex Fridman archive

This one's from the Lex Fridman archive, episode three ninety-two, released in August of twenty twenty-three. Lex's guest is Joscha Bach, a cognitive scientist who explores the nature of intelligence, consciousness, and computation. It was his third time on the show.

Here's the idea that frames the whole conversation. Joscha asks, what's the longest possible game you could be playing? A short game, he says, is cancer. It plays a shorter game than the organism it lives in, and when it wins, it dies along with its host. Ideally, he says, you build agents that play the longest possible game, which means keeping entropy at bay as long as possible by doing interesting things.

Second idea: large language models as golems. Joscha says they're machines you give a task, and they run until a condition is met, and, in his words, "there's nobody home." There's no ground truth the system is embedded in. Its structure comes from imitating the internet, not from the needs of a being trying to survive. He suspects the path to a first-person AI runs through self-organization, not next-token prediction. He's also careful about consciousness. He compares a chatbot describing consciousness to a character in a novel. Convincing on the page, with no causal need to be conscious.

Third, on Eliezer Yudkowsky's warnings that AI will likely kill us all. Joscha says the counterarguments he hears aren't very convincing, and he takes the risk seriously. But he rejects the advice to prepare for a short life. You have to bet on the timelines where you're still around. He thinks life on Earth isn't only about humans, and that the likely outcome is that we get integrated into something larger.

He also made a point about open source that aged interestingly. He wants open source to never die, as a hedge against corporate control. And he warned that regulation could make it impossible for a small group of kids to train their own language model. That debate is very much alive in today's headlines.

Why does it matter? Because it's a reminder that the question isn't only how smart these systems get. It's whether anyone's home, and what game they're playing.

## Acquired: Midroll and Stitcher, acquired by Scripps

Today's Acquired is episode sixteen of two hundred seventeen, from July of twenty sixteen. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal take on a meta subject: podcasts themselves. The deal is the E. W. Scripps Company buying two podcast businesses, Midroll and Stitcher, in the span of about a year. This one has a full transcript, so let's tell the story.

Start with Scripps. As David puts it, it's a very old company by technology standards. Not a tech company at all. It's a broadcast media company, historically a newspaper publisher too, running cable networks and local television and radio stations around the country. In the two years before this episode, Scripps made two acquisitions that made a huge splash in the tiny world of podcasting.

The first was Midroll. Its history starts in two thousand ten with two guys, Scott Aukerman and Jeff Ullrich, who launched a comedy podcast network called Earwolf. Scott had a Los Angeles live show, Comedy Bang! Bang!, which started out as a radio show called Comedy Death-Ray, and had roots in the Upright Citizens Brigade comedy scene in Hollywood. The show built a loyal following, so they built a network around it, adding more comedy shows, then culture and music. Comedy Bang! Bang! even became a television show on IFC.

For a couple of years the company was purely a content network. Then in twenty fourteen, something happened. They were selling ads for their own shows, and advertisers started asking if they could buy space on other podcasts, too. So they launched a separate business, an ad network, called Midroll Media. It was one of the first, if not the first, ad networks for podcasting. Ben points out how new that is. It was less than two years old at the time, even though podcasting had been around for over a decade, back to the iPod days.

The press release at launch pitched a three hundred sixty degree suite of production, distribution, and monetization services, with more than one hundred twenty shows and over fifteen million downloads a month. David's reaction: that's a grain of sand compared to the ad networks of the web. But the roster was strong. It included WTF with Marc Maron, which had recently hosted Barack Obama, plus Bill Simmons and The Ringer podcasts.

About a year before the episode, Scripps bought it. The price wasn't announced at the time, but it was later reported as fifty million dollars up front, plus a ten million dollar earnout. That's a great outcome for Scott and Jeff, who never raised a dime of outside money. They'd brought in an outside CEO, Adam Sachs, and the team stayed intact.

Now here's where Ben gets skeptical, and where the numbers get fun. A Wall Street Journal article that year estimated that advertisers would spend only about thirty-five million dollars on podcasts in the whole industry, up just two percent. A Midroll person had said a handful of podcasters gross over a million dollars a year, and that Midroll takes about thirty percent. Ben's math puts Midroll's annual revenue at somewhere around two million dollars. So Scripps paid fifty million dollars for a business making about two million. Let that sink in.

Then, a couple of weeks before the episode, the other shoe dropped. Scripps bought Stitcher. Stitcher is a podcast app for mobile devices, founded in two thousand eight by Noah Shanok, Peter deVroede, and Mike Ghaffary. It raised about twenty-five million dollars over three rounds, from New Atlantic Ventures, Benchmark, and NEA, chasing a vision of becoming the front door to podcasts. It didn't work. In twenty fourteen, the French music streaming company Deezer acquired it, more or less as an acquihire, and it languished. Then Scripps bought it back from Deezer for four and a half million dollars in cash. David notes the absurd arc: a venture-backed startup that raised twenty-five million, failed, got absorbed by another venture-backed company, and finally sold for four and a half million to an old broadcaster.

So why would Scripps want it? Here's the thesis, and the hosts give Scripps real credit for seeing it. Podcasting has a strange problem. Listening, hosting, and advertising are three separate worlds that don't talk to each other.

Ben walks through how the Acquired podcast itself reached you at the time. A domain name, a Squarespace site that generates the RSS feed, a hosting company called Libsyn for the audio files, and the iTunes directory pointing at the feed. And then players like Pocket Casts, Overcast, and Apple's own app, with over eighty percent of listening on iOS. David's point: just to get the show into your ears, it passes through three companies, none of which share data. Compare that to Facebook or Instagram, where the content, the hosting, and the ads are one tightly coupled product. That coupling is why those platforms became dominant for advertising.

The consequence is that an advertiser on a podcast knows almost nothing. Ben shares the show's own numbers. About sixty-four hundred unique listeners for one episode. About forty-one hundred came through Pocket Casts, which had featured the show on its home page. About a thousand through Apple, and two hundred ninety-seven through Marco Arment's Overcast. They could report cities and IP addresses, but not whether anybody actually heard the ad. The only attribution tool is the promo code. And if you go sign up for Squarespace without typing the code, the podcast gets no credit.

That's the arbitrage Scripps spotted. Podcast audiences were huge and engaged, but measurement was a mess. The only fix was to own the full stack: the content network, the ad sales, and a listening client you could instrument. Adam Symson, the chief digital officer at Scripps who led the deal, said as much. Scripps had the ad sales force and the connections, but depended almost entirely on distribution through other channels. Stitcher gave them a strong brand, and, quote, another piece of the puzzle in the ecosystem play.

And the demand for that puzzle was real. Podcast ad rates, the CPMs, were wild. Ben says the ceiling was around one hundred dollars per thousand impressions. The average YouTube rate in twenty fourteen was fourteen dollars, and web display ads ran between five and ten. Ben figured the Acquired show itself could command somewhere between twenty-five and fifty dollars. If you were an independent podcaster with an audience, you could do great.

Now, the backlash. The tech community erupted. John Gruber, host of The Talk Show, wrote that Midroll owning Stitcher was not good for the ecosystem. His complaint: Stitcher re-hosts the audio, compresses it, and unless you opt out, inserts its own ads. He believes podcasting should be open, like the web. Ben Thompson of Stratechery shared the worry about the Facebook-ization of podcasts.

Ben and David push back, gently. They call Gruber a bit of a grumpy old man here. Their view is that a sustainable business model, maybe even programmatic ad bidding like AdWords, could bring podcasting to more people. The real problem with Stitcher isn't the model, it's the execution. David draws the comparison to Facebook Instant Articles. Same dynamic, hosting content and inserting ads while sharing revenue, but Facebook cared about beauty and bought Push Pop Press to do it well. Stitcher, in David's words, was not that. Ben's own opinion of the app was blunt. He said he thought Stitcher was garbage every time he opened it.

So Scripps is, as David says, betting on hope. They're a small technology operation inside an old media company buying a tiny, subscale technology company, and they need to build an incredible user experience. The closest example the hosts can think of is Microsoft stitching together Acompli and Sunrise calendar. But Microsoft, as David says, has far better technology chops than Scripps.

The plan was also becoming clear. Midroll had just launched a premium service called Howl, with original shows and ad-free archives of popular podcasts. The Netflix-style idea. Midroll's Eric Diehn said, clearly, at some point the two will intersect. Ben's logic: it's hard to imagine downloading Stitcher to hear content you can get elsewhere. With exclusive content, maybe.

Ben also shares an inside look from his day job at Pioneer Square Labs, the Seattle startup studio. They looked hard at podcasting. The growth rate was great, but the market was only thirty to forty million dollars a year, and you couldn't test it cheaply. You'd have to be the host, the analytics provider, and ship a client that beats the one Apple already puts on every iPhone. They wanted dynamic ad insertion, where the host records ads ahead of time and the system stitches them into the audio at download. Others, like Acast and Panoply's Megaphone, were already doing it. Ben's verdict: it was a years-long build, not a quick validation. So they passed.

That's also the hosts' bigger worry. Apple. As David says, Apple created this medium, or it was created around Apple, and it doesn't care about it at all. Ben notes that Apple just inherited it. People were putting podcasts on iPods, and Apple added a directory to iTunes. It's still called podcasting even though nobody has an iPod anymore. Apple sells hardware, and this doesn't look like that business. Could Apple open up reporting and advertising? Maybe. But the hosts' bet was that Apple would let it be.

There's a lovely meta moment at the top of the episode, too. Ben and David thank their one listener on Zune, and their one listener on Stitcher, who happens to be David's wife, Jenny. It was Jenny who inspired the topic, after she Googled "acquired podcast" and couldn't find the show. David admits the whole episode is an unabashed play for search engine optimization. They also announce a "Community Showcase" in place of ads. Instead of selling sponsorships, they'd give a shout-out to something a listener is building, free of charge. As Ben says, they could start advertising later, but for now it's a thank-you to the community.

The hosts also ask a counterfactual question. What would have happened otherwise? Ben's answer: Stitcher keeps languishing, Midroll grows incrementally, selling ads on big but not network-sized podcasts, and nothing consolidates, so little money flows into podcasting. David adds a hopeful twist. Maybe this deal is a shot across the bow that wakes up entrepreneurs to the opportunity. Someone might attempt what Stitcher originally tried, and do it well.

And what about SoundCloud? Many listeners wrote that what the hosts describe already exists. David went through the sign-up flow for a new SoundCloud account right before recording, and it was obvious that the service pushes you toward music, not podcasts. Apple, he says, is the same. It had just bought Beats and reworked Apple Music, and podcasts weren't the priority. Nobody was building a great client where the focus was podcasts.

One more detail on the Gruber argument. His case was that Stitcher compresses audio, re-hosts it, and inserts its own ads, unless the show opts out. The hosts agreed that's a poor experience and that podcasters should keep creative freedom. But they asked an honest question. Is the open web of podcasting really better than what publishing looked like before blogging platforms, when you needed your own rack-mounted server? A platform that makes a sustainable business could bring podcasting to many more people. That was their bet, with the sacrifices it implies.

Then the tech themes. David's is the power law. In podcasting, the top ten publishers, names like NPR, This American Life, and WNYC, account for about forty percent of listeners. To win, you need the top publishers on your platform. Ben's theme: it's really hard to compete with platform defaults. Apple ships a podcast app, and that gets someone ninety percent of the way. He compares it to Internet Explorer winning on Windows, and the bundled mobile games in the flip-phone era. If it's on the platform, it gets used.

Time to grade. Ben on Midroll: it's the better company, but spending fifty million dollars on a business making maybe two million a year is a flyer. He gives it a D. Of the two, Midroll is his favorite, but, quote, God, that's a high price tag. Ben gives Stitcher a B, because Midroll's ad sales can plug right in, and four and a half million is small.

David is gentler on Midroll. The company only really started two years earlier and is growing fast. If it can sign the biggest publishers, there may be more to it. He gives it a C-plus or B-minus. The key, he says, is unlocking the total addressable market, and only an integrated platform does that. On Stitcher, he asks the practical question: who else were they going to buy? Not Overcast, because Marco Arment isn't selling to Scripps. Maybe Pocket Casts, made by an Australian company called Shifty Jelly, which hadn't raised outside money. But Stitcher came with eight million registered users. Four and a half million dollars for eight million users is just over fifty cents each. Even if many were lapsed, that's a cheap head start. David also gives it a B, though he doubts they'll pull off the whole plan. If they do, they'll look brilliant, having spent about fifty-four and a half million dollars to win the category.

So how did it look with hindsight? Both hosts predicted something would happen in podcasting, just not necessarily with these guys. And in fact, the real winners turned out to be players that owned more of the stack, including Spotify and Apple themselves. Stitcher itself was eventually folded into other products. But the core thesis, that measurement and ownership of the pipeline would unlock the money, proved right. And the hosts' warning about platform defaults proved prescient too.

The follow-ups are fun. Twitch has added tipping on the platform, called Cheering, using a virtual currency called Bits. It brings an existing flow of off-platform money inside Twitch. Facebook Paper is being shut down, with Ben calling it the best iOS app ever. And the SEC filing on LinkedIn's sale revealed five bidders. Microsoft started at one hundred sixty dollars a share and had a deal at one hundred eighty-two. Then Salesforce swooped in with a surprise bid of two hundred dollars, mostly in stock. That forced a renegotiation, and Microsoft ended up paying one hundred ninety-six dollars, all cash. David jokes that Reid Hoffman and Jeff Weiner should send Marc Benioff a very nice bottle of wine. And, in a fun detail, Reid Hoffman had mentioned the process to Mark Zuckerberg, who said no.

Finally, the Carve Outs. Ben's is Mark Titus, the Ohio State alum who wrote the Club Trillion blog from the end of the bench, joining The Ringer. His stat line read like one billion, because he got in for about a minute per game. David's is OKRs, objectives and key results, brought to Google by John Doerr of Kleiner Perkins. The idea is to set a few objectives and make the key results specific, measurable, and time-bound. And, notably, if you hit about half of them, you've set them high enough.

So what can an operator take from this? Three lessons. One, own the part of the chain you can't measure. Two, never underestimate the platform default. And three, an old company with cash and a good thesis can still overpay for a market that doesn't exist yet. Ben and David's takeaway, for the record: good idea, steep price.
