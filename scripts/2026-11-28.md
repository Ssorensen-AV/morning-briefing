Now, Deep Dives and Analysis.

The first piece is called Measuring RSI, recursive self-improvement. The argument is that neither compute allocation nor human intervention tells you whether an AI training algorithm actually improved. Neither one links human intervention to AI improvement, and neither can spot strong recursion before a more capable model has already been trained. The author proposes two fixes. Sample complexity detects the mechanism of strong recursion without building a more capable AI. Counterfactual measurement asks how much AI capability changes when it's advised by AI tools.

Second, Your Model Is a Dependency. The large language model you pick is a dependency, just like any other piece of software. Dependencies bloat, break, and quietly turn into liabilities. LLMs are the strangest dependencies developers have adopted so far, but the software supply chain playbook applies almost line by line. The advice is to start using those habits on your models as soon as possible.

Third, From Model Compression to Looped Transformers. Not every layer of a model is equally sensitive to compression. The deeper layers can often be squeezed harder than the early ones. That suggests models aren't using their architectures as well as researchers had hoped. Looped transformers offer a way to add computational depth without duplicating the main backbone parameters. The open question is how to make that extra computation actually matter.

Now, Engineering and Research.

TLDR is launching a curated community for AI builders, to talk best practices and news. The best links and comments will be featured in the newsletter. They're taking applications for founding members.

Next, LayerStream, from Nebius. It's a training engine for faster fine-tuning of mixture-of-experts models on fewer or cheaper GPUs. It keeps optimizer state and saved activations in host memory, and streams each layer's working state through the GPU only when that layer runs. On sixteen H200 GPUs, it finished a pass over a twenty-five point eight million token dataset in seven point one minutes, instead of twenty-two point four. That's three point one five times the throughput of a tuned Megatron-LM trainer, and sixty-eight percent lower GPU cost per token.

Then Plato's Key. The Platonic Representation Hypothesis says neural networks converge toward a shared picture of reality. Separately trained models can learn geometrically similar representations, but similar doesn't mean interchangeable. Here's the twist. In experiments, the map between them is surprisingly simple: a rotation and one global scale, fit in closed form, lets one LLM use another's representations in place of its own, no gradient descent needed.

## AI Deep Dive: Elon Musk

Now a trip into the Lex Fridman archive. This is episode four hundred, from November of twenty twenty-three, with Elon Musk, his fourth time on the show. It's a wide-ranging one, so here are three ideas that stuck.

First, the power problem. Musk said the limit for AI keeps moving. Back then, he said the bottleneck was silicon chips. In about a year, he predicted, it would be voltage step-down transformers, because power arrives at three hundred thousand volts and has to come down to around point seven volts at the chip. He joked, quote, "transformers for transformers." After that, he expected plain electricity shortages in about two years. Why does that matter to you? Because anyone planning compute, or even a home lab, ends up caring about useful compute per watt. And that has aged well. Power is now one of the main stories in AI.

Second, hallucination and drift. Musk said language models hallucinate most when you most want them accurate. His fix was grounding answers in physics and mathematical logic. His line was, quote, "Physics is the law, everything else is a recommendation." He also described how predicting one token after another can drift, like summing vectors, where small errors pile up. Lex suggested that revising, like an author rewriting a draft, might be what reasoning actually is. Musk agreed: draft first, then iterate until it adds up.

Third, Tesla's approach. Musk said Autopilot learned what cars, people, and cyclists are just from video, quote, "photons in, controls out." He argued Tesla's approach is far more compute efficient because it had to be, running on about a hundred watts. He compared that to the human brain, under ten watts of higher thinking, writing a better novel than a ten megawatt GPU cluster. For Optimus, his humanoid robot, he said they had to design every motor and actuator from scratch, because nothing off the shelf would do. His goal was threading a needle just by looking, and he guessed it was about a year away. Notably, he admits he's optimistic on time.

There's lighter stuff too. He and Lex talked about the absurd rocket permitting rules, including a seal strapped to a board wearing headphones to hear sonic booms. And Musk, who claims to cruise through Diablo four's hardest dungeons, said his biggest hardship was failing to beat the final boss.

It's worth remembering this was recorded before the models we're talking about today. Some predictions landed, and some, like the robot timeline, are still being tested.

## Acquired: Skype

And now our main story, Acquired, episode twenty-four of two hundred seventeen: Skype. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal call it a story with more drama than something with a lot of drama. They're right.

It starts in nineteen ninety-nine. Two men, Niklas Zennström in Sweden and Janus Friis in Denmark, work at the Swedish telecom company Tele2. Tele2 wants a web portal, because in those days everybody did. But nobody at the company can build good web software. So they look to Estonia, a country barely ten years out from Soviet rule. They run a newspaper ad offering five thousand Estonian kroons a day, about three hundred and thirty dollars, which was more than the average Estonian earned in a month.

They hire three developers: Jaan Tallinn, Ahti Heinla, and Priit Kasesalu. The hosts apologize for butchering the names. There's one problem. The developers don't know PHP, the language of the web at the time. No problem. They learn it over a weekend and build the portal.

Soon Zennström and Friis leave Tele2 to become entrepreneurs. It's the heyday of Napster, so they decide to build a competitor. It would share any file type: music, movies, software. They call it Kazaa. They call the Estonians again, and Kazaa is built. It quickly becomes the most popular application on the internet. Ben remembers fighting his friends about whether LimeWire or Kazaa was better.

With popularity come massive legal problems, just like Napster. So the founders sell Kazaa on paper to some Australian businessmen. The details are, in David's words, lost in the sands of time. But here's the key detail. They keep the technology. That will matter later.

Now the clever leap. The peer-to-peer technology that moves files can also move voice. Instead of one route between two computers, voice packets hop across a network of users. With enough density, you get clear, high quality calls, for free. They even build their own protocol. It mixes direct peer-to-peer connections with always-on super nodes, and it lasts until about twenty fourteen.

The first name was Sky peer-to-peer, shortened to Skyper. Skyper dot com was taken, so they dropped the R. Skype was born. Ben jokes it's a one-syllable, five-letter word, and today you could never find that domain.

What did they get right? Simplicity. An early employee, Lauri Tepandi, said they set out to write a program simple enough for a soccer mom with no knowledge of firewalls or IP addresses. And it was free, in a world where calling abroad cost a fortune. Telecoms were heavily regulated, with inflated prices. Skype went right around them.

It launches in August twenty oh three. On day one, ten thousand downloads. By the end of the first month, one million users. By the end of the first quarter, four point one million. By the end of the first year, nineteen point eight million. And remember, there were no smartphones yet. Many Americans didn't even have broadband. Because it was peer-to-peer, they could support all those users without insane infrastructure costs.

Funding came early. Legendary investor Bill Draper and Howard Hartenbaum put in money before launch, basically because these were the Kazaa guys. After launch, eighteen million dollars came from Index, Bessemer, and DFJ.

Now, the culture. Most of the company was in Estonia, and the hosts love the stories. Every new employee supposedly did a shot of tequila, sambuca, and Tabasco sauce. Ben did it himself. And the office boardroom had a pool. Not a pool table. A pool. A kiddie pool, they think, but still a ton or two of water, which required negotiating with building management about the floor.

In June of two thousand five, Skype adds video calling. David points out that this came two years after launch. And Ben notes that text chat, now dominant, was the third feature of Skype.

Then, in September of two thousand five, eBay buys Skype for two point six billion dollars. Why? One rationale was that buyers and sellers could call each other to verify transactions. Both hosts struggle to see it. Ben calls it eBay trying to become a conglomerate. David says it was, regardless, a dumb acquisition, because of one key mistake. Once again, the founders kept the technology. It sat in a separate company called Joltid, owned by Zennström and Friis, and licensed to eBay and Skype.

Culture clash was immediate. In twenty oh six, eBay management flies to Estonia for roadmap meetings. The Skype team throws a huge party at the hotel. Zennström gets behind the bar and pours shots, then climbs on it and yells, quote, "What happens in Estonia stays in Estonia." Everyone jumps in the pool, fully clothed. Local news crews catch it. eBay pays for the damage. David says that was not the corporate culture eBay signed up for.

Meanwhile, Skype keeps growing. By two thousand nine, it has over half a billion users and is the fastest growing division at eBay. In the third quarter of that year alone, Skype does one hundred eighty-five million dollars in revenue. But it doesn't fit inside eBay, and Wall Street wants a spinout. So eBay works on a deal with a group of private equity firms, including Silver Lake, plus Andreessen Horowitz, which had just been founded, to invest directly and spin Skype out at roughly the price eBay paid. Ben notes that eBay had written down the value of Skype internally back in October of two thousand seven.

Then the bomb. Skype doesn't own its own core technology. Zennström and Friis sue eBay and the investor group to stop them from using it. And this plays out in public, because eBay is a public company. David says one very real path was that Skype could simply die. The options were: build an alternative technology, which is hard, because peer-to-peer is the heart of the product; or settle. They settle.

Here's the settlement. Joltid gets ten percent of Skype. Skype gets the technology. The founders get an eighty-five million dollar cash payment, and then invest eighty million dollars into the spinout, which gets them about four percent more. So they end with roughly fourteen percent. eBay keeps thirty percent. The investor group gets fifty-six percent. And there are strange side terms. Skype invests in the second fund of Atomico, Niklas's venture firm, and in Rdio, the music streaming service, also started by the Skype founders. They also agree to promote Joost, the founders' peer-to-peer television venture, on Skype. David says they had a third act and a fourth act.

Now the private equity chapter, which Ben calls a classic and well executed play. Skype hires Tony Bates, a star at Cisco, as CEO in October of twenty ten. Before that, in June of twenty ten, Mark Gillett of Silver Lake becomes chief development officer. They restructure the company, finally integrate the technology it now owns, and push hard into mobile, with iOS and Android apps. They file to go public, an S one, aiming to raise one hundred million dollars. David notes this is classic dual track, one track an IPO, one track a sale. Filing publicly draws buyers out of the woodwork.

And one shows up. In May of twenty eleven, Microsoft announces it will buy Skype for eight point five billion dollars, then its largest acquisition ever. Skype had seven hundred million users, eight hundred sixty million dollars in revenue for twenty ten, and two hundred sixty-four million in operating profit. Ben points out that's about thirty-two times operating profit. The September twenty oh nine private equity price was two point seven five billion dollars. So, in under two years, almost six billion dollars of value created. eBay did fine too. It sold sixty-five percent at the spinout, kept thirty percent, and got back roughly what it paid in two thousand five.

The hosts stress that Skype was a really good business through all the chaos. Rumors said Google and Facebook were circling, at roughly half the price. Facebook had even used Skype to power video calling in Messenger. Microsoft, though, led through the Office organization, under Kurt DelBene, and was willing to pay more.

Why would Microsoft pay so much? Ben offers one angle. Skype is a Luxembourg corporation. Microsoft had a lot of cash overseas, and bringing it home meant huge taxes. Buying a foreign company is one way to put that cash to work. David adds, it's certainly better than buying treasury bills.

What happened after? Tony Bates became president of Skype, reporting to Steve Ballmer, and was even talked about as a possible Microsoft CEO. He later went to GoPro. Microsoft had three overlapping tools: MSN Messenger, Office Communicator, which became Lync, and now Skype. It took three or four years to narrow things down. MSN Messenger was killed, Lync became Skype for Business, and Skype got built into Windows and Outlook dot com. A year later Microsoft bought Yammer. David says it's confusing: Skype for this, Skype for that, while Slack is simply Slack, one front door.

Now the hosts' analysis. Category: Ben calls it a product acquisition, because Microsoft paid too high a multiple to simply cash flow the business. David agrees, noting eBay's deal was more of a business line acquisition.

Tech themes. First, the dual track game, where an IPO filing creates leverage for a sale. Second, the rise of the mega acquisition. Eight and a half billion was shocking then. Microsoft employees joked about counting how many Skypes something was. Yet, David points out, it's less than half of what WhatsApp later cost, twenty-two billion dollars. And WhatsApp offers the same value proposition, cheap communication across borders, just for text instead of voice.

Third, David's theme, organic growth. He cites Bill Gurley's blog post, All Revenue Is Not Created Equal. It features a slide from an early Skype pitch deck. Vonage, the competitor, advertised on national television and spent about four hundred dollars to acquire each user. Skype spent one tenth of one cent. No marketing, and no server costs per call. That's pure product-market fit.

Grades. Ben gives Microsoft's acquisition a B minus. He says he would have given a D in twenty twelve, but over time it has improved. Consolidating the confusing products was the right move, and it was a good use of that overseas cash. David also lands on a B minus. He says it would have been a bad acquisition, but for finding use for foreign cash, and because Skype was a good business. But relative to its potential, it was never fully realized. One reason, in his view: no real product champion stayed. Bates was a turnaround guy, not a product guy. The heroes of Skype are gone. The ones people still talk about are the original founders, quote, "the dudes from the newspaper ad."

Let's step back and look at how this aged, because the episode was recorded in twenty sixteen. The hosts hoped Skype's consumer roots might leave Microsoft well placed for the workplace communication race that Slack was pioneering. That isn't how it played out. Microsoft built its own workplace chat product, Teams, in twenty seventeen, and over the following years Teams took the place that Skype for Business had been meant to fill. Skype for Business was retired. And in May of twenty twenty-five, Microsoft shut down consumer Skype too, pointing users toward Teams. David's worry that nobody inside Microsoft was championing Skype looks, in hindsight, pretty perceptive. A product with no champion rarely survives a big company's reorganizations.

It's also worth pausing on the timing of the pandemic. Video calling exploded in twenty twenty, and the winner was Zoom, which David had already said, in this very episode, tended to work best. Skype had the name, the users, and a decade of head start, and still lost that moment. Why? The hosts' hints are all here: three overlapping Microsoft products, a confusing set of Skype for this and Skype for that, and no single front door. Zoom was just Zoom.

And a quick note on the Estonians, because the story doesn't end with the sale. The Estonian developers who built Kazaa and Skype became something of a founding generation for the country's startup scene. Many Skype alumni went on to found or fund companies, and Estonia built a reputation as one of the most digital countries in the world, the very thing Ben was admiring when he visited Tallinn. Jaan Tallinn, the first developer on that newspaper ad, went on to become a prominent investor and an early voice on the long-term risks of artificial intelligence. So the ad that paid three programmers more than a month's wages for a single day of work echoes into the AI conversation we started this briefing with.

One more detail on the legal drama, because it's the part operators should remember. Kazaa itself ran into the music industry's lawyers for years, and the owners eventually settled. The Skype founders spent those years learning how to separate technology from the company that uses it. That wasn't an accident. It was a playbook. Sell the company that carries the legal risk, keep the engine, and license it back. It worked beautifully for Zennström and Friis, and it was a nightmare for everyone who bought from them. For you, as someone who has signed a lot of integration and vendor contracts, the lesson is simple. Always ask who owns the thing underneath the thing you're buying.

In follow-ups, they talk about Google's new Pixel phone, which they call the Google iPhone. The big insight, borrowed from Ben Thompson and John Gruber, is that Google Assistant seemed to live on the Pixel, not in Android itself. Why does that matter? An assistant gives answers, not options, and Google's ad business depends on options. So a high margin phone might become a new business model. Back then, at least, it was sharp analysis.

In their hot takes, they discuss the AT&T and Time Warner deal, and worry about net neutrality. And they cheer the New York Times buying Wirecutter.

For carve outs, Ben recommends the New Yorker piece, Sam Altman's Manifest Destiny, which he says left him thinking he'd been thinking too small. David's pick is SOMA the Musical, starring his friend Jake Saper, a venture capitalist, as the entrepreneur.

So what can an operator take from Skype? One, own your core technology. The founders kept the keys twice, and both times it nearly sank the deal. Two, simple products grow without marketing. Three, big companies buy things they can't integrate; the product matters less than the people who will champion it. And four, a good business can survive a lot of bad owners.
