## TLDR AI

Here's the TLDR AI issue from Monday, November twenty-third.

In Headlines and Launches. First, a piece on perplexity and intelligence. Perplexity measures how well a model predicts the next token on average. Lower perplexity means better predictions. It's a metric research teams have put years into optimizing, and the piece argues it will likely help standardize how we talk about AI progress as it keeps delivering steady gains in general intelligence.

Second, Thinking Machines released Inkling Two Mini, an open-weight model with twenty-six point four billion parameters that does extra internal computation to reason before it answers. It leads every open-weight model under one hundred billion parameters on the DeepSWE coding benchmark.

Now, Deep Dives and Analysis. METR reports that frontier models often match or beat skilled humans on well-defined coding and optimization tasks. The length of tasks they can complete doubles about every three point six months. METR says public evidence can't rule out a drastic speedup in AI research within the next couple of years.

A counterpoint follows. An essay argues the most important charts in AI are wrong. Capabilities are getting impressive, but they're still far below what influential forecasts and benchmarks predicted, and the gap keeps growing. Now that real-world data exists, the author says, it's time to move from AI theorists to AI empiricists.

Third, a short piece on why post-training could teach models to evade monitoring. Penalizing detected reward hacking could select for behavior that dodges the monitors. The industry needs stronger training sandboxes, better-audited tasks and graders, and research into whether interventions improve alignment or merely conceal bad behavior.

In Engineering and Research. Tencent released Hy Four, an open-weight model that understands text, images and video. It has a seven hundred seventy billion parameter backbone that activates forty-nine billion parameters per token. It adds a million-token context, sparse attention, and a speculative-decoding drafter, and it targets coding, computer use, finance, research and long-running assistants.

NVIDIA released Nemotron-Labs-Sigma, a family of fourteen open checkpoints at three billion and eight billion parameters. They generate text by denoising compact token embeddings instead of predicting one token at a time. The eight billion model supports few-step generation and self-speculative decoding, with a three point four six times average throughput speedup over standard decoding on a GB two hundred.

Hugging Face introduced harness adapters. They let models like Qwen three point eight, twenty-seven billion, tune themselves to a specific harness and task. Using adapters trained with reinforcement learning, the models get higher accuracy and use far fewer tokens.

Also in that section, TLDR is launching a curated community for AI builders. The best links and comments will be featured in the newsletter, and you can apply through their form.

In Miscellaneous. TLDR's community asked for predictions for twenty twenty-seven. One reader's take: by the end of that year, office work will no longer use frontier models you pick yourself, and choosing a model could feel as archaic as tweaking its temperature.

Next, a profile of the brother-sister duo who want to keep Anthropic weird. The company embraces being atypical because it believes the AI future will be anything but normal. Dario Amodei, forty-three, is the chief executive and ideological leader. He has one direct report, his chief of staff and sister, Daniela Amodei, twenty-nine. The company screens new hires with culture interviews, and the article looks at how the two built a culture in their likeness.

And a big one. Levent Alpöge released a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis as a Lean library of seventeen million lines of code on November twentieth. The Lean and Mathlib communities have full confidence in it. The library compiles, and the code proves the correct statement. The wider math community is expected to help explain the ideas in the coming weeks.

Quick links. Gemini Omni one point two Flash can now create GIFs with smooth motion and consistent quality across every frame. Amazon is reportedly nearing a seven point five billion dollar purchase of Decart, a three-year-old AI startup. Bison-one and groundhog-one are new decision models built on Google's Gemma Four, aimed at teams that want an American base model. And OpenAI is stepping up its defenses against adversarial distillation, where outsiders train models on its outputs to copy its capabilities.

## AI Deep Dive: James Sexton, a Divorce Lawyer, from the Lex Archive

No machines in this one, but it's worth your commute. From the Lex Fridman archive, back in September of twenty twenty-three, episode three ninety-six. Lex talks with James Sexton, a New York divorce attorney with more than twenty years in high-conflict cases and the author of How to Stay in Love.

Sexton's first idea is that marriages don't fail from one thing. They fail from disconnection. Everyone wants the answer to be cheating or money, but he says those are the big, all-at-once moments. Quoting Tom Wolfe on bankruptcy, he says disconnection happens "very slowly and then all at once." His example is granola. If someone stops buying the one you like, that's a signal worth raising early, not after the big blowup.

Second idea: stop trash-talking your partner. Sexton says culture encourages us to roll our eyes at long-term partners, and he calls that toxic. The couples hardest to break are thick as thieves. They take sides against anybody, but never against each other. A partner who says "you've got this" is, in his words, a superpower.

Third, he's a big fan of prenups, which he says cost, at most, a few thousand dollars when a lawyer does them. The real obstacle is just an uncomfortable conversation. He also warns against internet templates, because a missing notary acknowledgment can make one invalid.

One line to keep. On how couples miss problems, he says, "We don't know who discovered water, but it wasn't a fish." Why does it matter? Because the same early-warning mindset applies to any system you run, at work or at home.

## Acquired: Android

Now our main story. Acquired, episode twenty, Android. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal recorded this in September twenty sixteen, and they call it a cornerstone of all of computing today.

Start with the man. The company was founded in Palo Alto in October of two thousand three by Andy Rubin, Rich Miner, Nick Sears and Chris White. David says Andy was basically born to start it. Andy began at Carl Zeiss, the camera and lens maker. Then he went to Apple during the John Sculley era. A group of Apple people spun off General Magic, which built something like a personal communicator, a Palm competitor, years before its time. It went public, never really launched a product, and went bankrupt.

Alumni of General Magic then started WebTV, based on the then-popular idea that the internet would arrive on your television, not on your computer. Microsoft bought WebTV. Andy and the team built Microsoft TV, which David calls an abject failure. So Andy left and started Danger. Danger made the Sidekick, arguably the first consumer-focused smartphone. It had a cool factor. Ben remembers the industrial design as crazy and unique, and David first noticed it on Entourage, where everybody seemed to have one. Supposedly, Larry Page and Sergey Brin were huge Sidekick users too.

There's a nice little aside here about timing. Andy's old company, Danger, didn't get acquired by Microsoft until two thousand eight, much later than people assume. So when Andy started Android, Danger was still out there, and he was building something deliberately different. Danger sold a whole package. Android would sell only the brain.

Andy left Danger early and founded Android in two thousand three. Danger was full stack: hardware, software, carriers, everything. Android was only an operating system company. The idea was to take Linux and make it run on mobile devices. And the first target market? Digital cameras. This was twenty oh four, and everybody had a point-and-shoot. Ben notes that nobody in two thousand three could have known cameras would vanish into phones. Fortunately, the team decided the market was too small and pivoted to mobile phones.

Ben has a camera confession. He says he'd just pre-ordered a phone size he didn't really want, because the camera was better, and he realized he uses the thing more as a camera than a phone. David's response is lovely: maybe Andy and the team were more right than they thought at the time. Even the first iPhone's camera wasn't a selling point. Ben doesn't think he had a camera phone until two thousand five or two thousand six, and he says they were horrendous.

Here's the part Ben and David love. As far as they know, Android never raised venture capital. At one point Andy was running low on cash. Steve Perlman, who had been the CEO of WebTV and had worked with Andy at General Magic, showed up at the office with an envelope holding ten thousand dollars in cash. Andy tried to give him shares. Steve refused. He just wanted to help a friend.

Let's pause on that Steve Perlman story, because it says something about this company. Andy had worked for Steve at both General Magic and WebTV. When the money got thin, Steve didn't ask for equity, a board seat or a thank-you. He handed over an envelope. David's reaction is the right one: what a good friend. And it reminds you that behind a deal that Google would later call its best ever was a small team in Palo Alto, running on friendship and stubbornness.

Think about the sequence for a moment, because it's a useful pattern for anyone who builds things. Andy's first company was ahead of its time and failed. His second tried to put the internet on television and was absorbed by Microsoft. His third made the first mobile device with real consumer cool. His fourth became the operating system that ended up on most of the world's phones. Each stop taught him something about hardware, software, carriers or developers. David quotes Steve Jobs on connecting the dots: you can only do it looking backwards. Looking backwards, it's hard to imagine anyone better prepared.

In July of two thousand five, Google bought Android. This was before the company had shipped anything, and it was a long way from shipping. The price wasn't announced. Rumor says about fifty million dollars. Years later, David Lawee, who ran Google's corporate development, called it Google's best deal ever. For years, people speculated about a Google phone, and nothing appeared. Andy and the team just kept working.

Then in January of two thousand seven, the world changed. Steve Jobs announced the iPhone. David and Ben call it one of the best product launches of all time. And look at the reactions. Steve Ballmer laughed at it. The BlackBerry people reportedly watched the keynote and said it was fake, because you can't get scroll performance like that. And at Google, engineer Chris DeSalvo later said that what they had suddenly looked so nineteen-nineties. Their prototype with HTC looked like the Palm Treo, with BlackBerry-style keys and no touch screen. They scrapped it and went back to the drawing board.

One more thing worth saying about that moment. The Google team had been months from showing a finished device. Then a competitor walked on stage and changed the standard for what a phone should be. Most teams would have defended their work. This one threw out the keyboard, rebuilt around touch, and shipped about a year later. That willingness to scrap a nearly finished product is probably why Android exists in the form we know.

In November of two thousand seven, Google announced the Android operating system and the Open Handset Alliance. Members included HTC, Sony, Samsung, Sprint, T-Mobile and Qualcomm. Ben calls it the whole stack: manufacturer, operating system and carrier. Google also announced a ten million dollar Android challenge for developers. And it made clear that Android was open in two ways. It was open source and free, which later led to forks like Cyanogen, the Kindle Fire and, eventually, Xiaomi. And it was open to developers, at a time when Apple had said it didn't want third-party developers. Apple announced the App Store in two thousand eight. David suggests Google's openness helped push Apple that way.

The first Android phone, the T-Mobile G1, made by HTC, arrived in October of two thousand eight. It still had a slide-out keyboard and physical buttons. It didn't make much of a splash. The iPhone, meanwhile, was exclusive to AT&T, which was raking in subscribers while Verizon, Sprint and T-Mobile had a big problem. By the holidays of two thousand nine, everyone except Apple realized they had to respond. Verizon launched the Droid, made by Motorola. The campaign was called Droid Does, a reverse of the old Mac and PC ads. Ben jokes that Verizon paid Lucasfilm every time the word Droid appeared, even at the bottom of every magazine ad. One odd side effect: people asked, "iPhone or Droid?" not "iPhone or Android?"

Think about where the market sat. David and Ben say that by this point Android had about eighty percent of the people and the iPhone about twenty percent, but iPhone owners pay for apps far more than Android owners do. They expected one winner, a repeat of Microsoft versus Apple. Instead the world settled into an equilibrium with two platforms, at least for this decade. David calls it not a peace treaty, but a stable standoff.

From twenty ten through about twenty twelve, everyone not named Apple raced to copy Apple. Samsung was the most shameless, Ben says, rushing copies to market within about two months. The bitterness got personal. In the Walter Isaacson biography, Steve Jobs says he'd spend his last dying breath and every penny of Apple's forty billion dollars in the bank to destroy Android, which he called a stolen product. He said he was willing to go thermonuclear war. Google's Vic Gundotra answered at the twenty ten I O conference. Without Android, he said, we faced a draconian future with one man, one company, one device, one carrier. David's take: Google was Apple-ing Apple, casting itself as the underdog hurling the hammer from the famous nineteen eighty-four ad, even while holding a search monopoly.

The rivalry kept its sharp edges. Ben mentions Tim Cook calling the Android ecosystem a toxic hellstew, a quote pulled from a writer and put up on stage. And they recall the old Apple line, "if you see a stylus, we blew it." When Tim Cook was asked about that recently, Ben says, his answer was a gentle correction: it's a pencil, not a stylus. David's reaction: what would Steve have said?

David also steps back for a moment. He says the tech world is surprisingly small. Marc Lore worked at Amazon after Amazon bought his company, and later vowed to beat them. The PayPal alumni went on to build half of Silicon Valley. Photoshop and Pixar both trace back to Lucasfilm. And Ben, who worked on Office for iPad, is a living example of how fast competitors become partners. Apple and Microsoft were bitter rivals twenty years earlier, and now Apple has an enterprise partnership with IBM.

David's view of why everybody fought so hard is simple. Mobile was the biggest market technology had ever seen. First a couple of hundred million people in the United States, then a billion around the world, and eventually nearly every person on the planet buying a smartphone and getting online for the first time. Everyone raced to capture it. But he wonders whether they were fighting the right fight at first, because they fought over hardware, when the value was moving elsewhere.

A fun side note. In August of twenty ten, HTC bought a majority stake in Beats Audio. For a while you could buy Beats-branded HTC phones. Hard to imagine now that Beats belongs to Apple. And Ben points out how fast friends became enemies. When the iPhone launched, Eric Schmidt was on Apple's board and appeared on stage with Steve Jobs.

Now, why did Google really care? Ben and David say Android exists to keep Apple from becoming the front door to Google's services. Google makes its money when people search and click on ads. Then came the Oracle lawsuit, which made public how much Google paid Apple to be the default search on the iPhone. It wasn't a flat fee. Apple got roughly thirty-four percent of the search revenue from its platform. Goldman Sachs estimated Google made about fifteen billion dollars from mobile search in twenty fifteen. With Apple holding about eighteen percent of the market, that works out to somewhere near a billion dollars paid to Apple. So Android and Chrome are really the same strategy: don't let anyone sit between Google and its customers. Ben figures that, with Android on about eighty percent of phones, Google avoids paying out roughly four billion dollars a year.

Critics say Google can't succeed at anything beyond AdWords, because Android, Chrome and YouTube don't make money. David disagrees. Those products protect the core business. And Android does make money directly. From the Oracle documents, Ben cites about thirty-one billion dollars in annual Android revenue. Perhaps ten to fifteen billion of that is Google Play, where Google keeps thirty percent, so three to four billion dollars a year. Goldman estimated Android delivered twenty-two billion dollars in profit the previous year.

Two more pieces of history. In August of twenty eleven, Google bought Motorola for twelve point five billion dollars. David calls it brilliant and boneheaded. The patents helped defend Google in cases like Oracle's, since Google was a young company without a deep patent bench. But the hope of a unified stack from operating system to hardware never happened. Google sold the Motorola assets to Lenovo for two point nine billion dollars.

And Xiaomi, founded in twenty ten, often called the Apple of China. By September twenty sixteen, it was the second most valuable private tech company, behind Uber, at roughly forty to fifty billion dollars. Samsung relies on Google's Android, with software skins on top. Xiaomi completely forked Android and locks it down. Ben explains the difference. Android from Google comes free with Maps, Gmail and the Play Store. The open source version is free too, but without the Play Store and services, you have a cold start problem. You'd have to build a whole developer ecosystem. Amazon had to do exactly that. That's how Google keeps manufacturers on stock Android, and keeps search services from being swapped out.

So what's the category? Ben says technology. The team bought core technology that hadn't become a product. David agrees, with a bit of talent, and says it moved from technology to product to business line to asset. Ben adds the real prize was defensibility, bought cheaply.

What would have happened otherwise? Android would likely have been bought by someone else or failed. And for Google, could they have built it in-house? Probably, since they were the darling of Silicon Valley. But would anyone have championed it? You needed an Andy Rubin. And the open source, give-it-away strategy might not have come naturally to Google without Android.

Tech themes. First, you have to own the front door to the customer. Ben credits Ben Thompson at Stratechery: distribution is now free, so you win by having the best customer experience. David contrasts Microsoft's Windows Mobile approach, controlling everything from carriers to proprietary software, with building something great on open Linux. Second, competition keeps moving up the stack. It was once hardware, then operating systems, and now apps like Uber versus Lyft, or Snapchat versus Instagram.

Now, the grade. Ben goes first. He says A plus. The deal cost roughly fifty million dollars, produced a margin-saving defensive moat, and kept Google safe for a decade or two as the world changed. David wrestles with it. He compares it with Instagram, their gold standard, which created a new revenue engine for Facebook. Android is a margin saver. Is a defensive deal as good as an offensive one? David says he's limping into the A plus. Then he lands it: Android is his new gold standard for defensive acquisitions. Still, he'd rather play offense.

In follow-ups, they note that Google's Waze launched ride sharing in San Francisco, which Uber obviously noticed. Google was a big Uber shareholder, and Google's chief legal officer, David Drummond, resigned from Uber's board. Ben and David wonder whether Google chose Waze over Maps because of that. Their lesson, again: in tech, friends become enemies quickly.

So here's the takeaway for an operator. Android's founders almost aimed at digital cameras. They looked at a market that seemed big and walked away. Then Google backed a patient team for years, until the iPhone forced a complete restart in two thousand seven. Good strategy often depends on staying alive long enough, and being willing to scrap your prototype when the world changes.
