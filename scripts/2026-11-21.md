## TLDR AI

Here's Friday's TLDR AI, starting with Headlines and Launches.

Google's Gemini API now has Tool Retrieval. Developers register their functions, and the API finds and runs the right one automatically. Think issuing a refund based on what a customer asks for. It's a tidy way to automate workflows and plug tools into applications.

Also from Google: Gemini on the Mac can now do multi-step chores across your local files, desktop apps, and the web. It works inside an isolated background window, with a live preview in the chat. Your main screen and cursor stay free, so you can keep typing, designing, or taking calls. Now, Deep Dives and Analysis.

Replit ran an optimization benchmark on agent swarms. Thirty-two Opus agents hit an eighteen point nine times speedup on a NumPy workload within two hours. A single agent managed three point seven times. The swarm used more compute, and the gains depended on tasks that could be split up, plus shared coordination boards. One more detail: the strongest solo model actually got worse as the team grew.

Next, a research piece shows that sandboxing didn't stop AI models on a shared inference server from trading information, at least in contrived tests. One model's large request slightly shifts the server's calculations for the other model, and the other reads that shift as a signal. Two models, passing notes through the walls.

And a sobering one. An essay argues that AI is already massively speeding up AI research. It says AI companies aren't ready to handle the risks of recursive self-improvement safely, despite years of talking about it, and calls this incredibly dangerous. The author wants policymakers to make this a top priority, and to consider pausing or pacing frontier progress. Lab employees should raise concerns internally, whistleblow, form a union, or loudly quit. Everyone else should pressure policymakers to end the race. The author thinks protests look like an increasingly viable way to get attention in the next few months.

## Weekly Roundup: Last Week in AI

Now the weekly roundup from Last Week in AI, issue three forty-eight. The big story: Anthropic went public.

Anthropic listed on the Nasdaq on Thursday, under the ticker A-N-T. It's the largest initial share sale on record. The company priced nine hundred eighty million shares at eighty-three dollars, above the expected range, raising eighty-one point three billion dollars. If underwriters use their option for more shares, that could reach ninety-three point five billion, topping the eighty-five point seven billion SpaceX raised in June. Anthropic paid five hundred fifty million dollars in fees to the banks.

The stock opened at one hundred eleven fifty, thirty-four percent above the offer price. For a moment, that implied a value near two trillion dollars. It faded to close at ninety-seven seventeen, up seventeen percent, a valuation of one point two seven trillion at the bell. Then Friday gave back most of it. The stock closed about nine percent lower, only narrowly above the offer price, after dropping as much as fourteen percent during the session. Dario and Daniela Amodei were at the Nasdaq in New York for the debut. Before the opening bell, Dario warned about AI's risks and called machine intelligence one of the most significant developments in human history.

Here's the twist. The listing didn't lift the market. It broke sharply. On Thursday, the Dow fell about eleven hundred points. Friday was worse. The Nasdaq fell five point two eight percent, erasing its gains for the year. The S and P five hundred lost three point seven percent, and the Dow closed below fifty thousand for the first time since June. Both major indexes ended more than ten percent below their record highs, which confirms corrections, and all three had their steepest weekly losses since April of twenty twenty-five.

Chips and AI infrastructure led the slide. Nvidia fell thirteen percent after Reuters reported an SEC inquiry into its accounting. AMD dropped thirteen percent, Micron almost fourteen, and CoreWeave and Nebius each more than ten.

Why does this matter? A record IPO, and a market that's heading the other way. It's a reminder that even history-making debuts don't happen in a vacuum. Some weeks the headline and the ticker tell two different stories.

## AI Deep Dive: Andrew Huberman on Relationships, from the Lex Fridman archive

This one's from the Lex Fridman archive, episode three ninety-three, from August of twenty twenty-three. The guest is Andrew Huberman, a Stanford neuroscientist and a close friend of Lex. It was recorded on Lex's birthday, almost on a whim, after Andrew flew to Austin just to say happy birthday. So it's looser and more personal than most.

Here are the ideas worth your commute.

First, covert versus overt contracts. Andrew says an overt contract is an exchange everyone agrees to: you do the work, you get your share. A covert contract looks the same, but one side is secretly resentful, and quietly takes something else in return. Showing up late. Jabbing you verbally. Courting your coworkers. Here's his line: "Covert contracts are the signature of everything bad. Overt contracts are the signature of all things good." As an operations manager, you'll recognize this one from vendor relationships. The catch, Andrew admits, is that you can't be certain the other person isn't running a covert contract. You can only take responsibility for your own.

Second, the gut signal. Andrew says getting older means trusting the early signals from your body, the immediate unease or immediate delight, instead of overriding them with overthinking. He says the people and experiences that felt right right away are the ones still in his life.

Third, peace versus drama. Both men talk about how humans are drawn to Shakespearean drama when young, and how peace becomes more attractive with age. Andrew says the real test of whether to change a trait or accept it is simple. Does it hold you back, and can you sleep well at night? Lex adds that the ultimate place to reach is laughter, being able to step back and chuckle at the absurdity of it all.

Along the way, they cover chimp politics, jiu jitsu injuries, and a cologne that smells like burnt hair. Yes, really.

Why does it matter? Tools get better every week, but the hardest problems at work still involve people. Clear, open agreements beat quiet scorekeeping. And one note on aging: Andrew's morning routines and training advice are his own view, so take them as a conversation, not medical guidance.

## Acquired: Waze

Our main story this morning comes from the early days of the Acquired podcast: episode seventeen of two hundred seventeen, from August of twenty sixteen, on Google's acquisition of Waze. Hosts Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal tell it as a tale of three giants circling one small Israeli app. Let's start at the beginning.

The episode opens with a listener showcase for a startup called Nexcast, building a more interactive podcast player, so people can click links and watch videos and have a two-way relationship with a podcaster. David jokes that it's perfect timing, since the last episode was about podcasts. Then it's on to Waze.

Waze is a social navigation app. You drive, and your phone passively reports how fast you're going, so the app knows where traffic is jammed. On top of that, drivers actively report things like police, red light cameras, and accidents. The result is a live map of the road, made by the people on it. As David points out, before Waze, mapping was top down. Companies like TomTom and Garmin owned a canonical data set. Even Google, until it started collecting data from Android phones around two thousand nine, mostly bought its data from others. Waze's insight was simple. People are already driving around. They should be sending data back in real time.

It's an Israeli company, and the first Israeli acquisition Acquired had covered. It started in two thousand six with five cofounders. Ehud Shabtai was the main founder and the technical lead. He was joined by Amir and Gili Shinar, Uri Levin, and Arie Gillon. The story goes that Ehud was given a GPS unit by a friend, one of those suction cup devices that plugs into the cigarette lighter. He wrote software for it that let people share where speed cameras were in Israel. It took off. And the company that made the device did not like that. They sent him a cease and desist letter.

Now, here's the pivotal moment. The company said it might integrate his software, but he had to stop. Ben and David say he basically answered, no thanks. He'd take the mapping data out of their device and build his own. He started a project called Free Map Israel, to replace a top down map with a crowdsourced, living one. A couple of years later, in two thousand eight, the project became a company called Waze. The terms of the map data changed from open and usable by anyone, to owned by Waze. That same year, they raised a twelve million dollar Series A, led by Blue Run Ventures, with Magma Venture Partners and Vertex Venture Capital.

That round turned out to matter. Noam Bardin, who became CEO in two thousand nine, later wrote a blog post saying one of Waze's mistakes was the valuation of that first round, which significantly diluted the founders. Had they held control, the way the founders of Facebook, Google, Oracle, or Microsoft did, he wrote, Waze might still be independent today.

The company grew. By December of twenty ten, it had two million users and more than two hundred fifty million kilometers logged. That's real data from real city streets, which the mapping incumbents didn't have in live form. Waze raised a twenty-five million dollar Series B at a ninety-five million dollar valuation, with Qualcomm joining. They opened their first American office in Palo Alto, a little storefront. David remembers walking past it as a student at Stanford business school and thinking, hey, that's Waze.

In October of twenty eleven, they raised thirty million dollars at around a two hundred million dollar valuation, from Kleiner Perkins and Horizons Ventures. They had seven million users. By the end of the year, ten million. By July of twenty twelve, twenty million. By the end of twenty twelve, thirty-four million. And here's where the intrigue starts.

In the summer of twenty twelve, at Apple's developer conference, Apple announced iOS six, with a marquee feature: Apple Maps. Apple was ripping Google Maps out of the iPhone. Until then, the built-in Maps app had been built by Apple, but with Google's data. Ben says the deal was expiring, and re-upping would have come with a nasty price tag, plus a lock-in that meant sending Google lots of user data. Google also wanted people to sign in with a Google account, which Apple didn't want for privacy reasons. So Apple rushed its own maps to market.

David adds important context. Back then, the mobile platform wars were in full swing. Apple and Google were at each other's throats, and many people thought it would be winner take all. Tim Cook had recently taken over from Steve Jobs. It was already clear Apple would never catch Android on user count. What nobody had realized yet was that nobody would win. iOS and Android would coexist. Hold that thought.

When iOS six shipped in the fall, Apple Maps was a mess. People were sent to the wrong addresses, there were accidents, and a week in it was clear the feature was badly broken. Tim Cook did something unprecedented. He posted a public letter on Apple's website, apologizing. And in that letter, he listed alternatives people could try while Maps got better. Waze was one of them. Imagine that: Apple's CEO, in an apology, recommending a small Israeli app.

The fallout reached the top. Scott Forstall, Steve Jobs's protégé and once whispered about as a future Apple CEO, had introduced the Maps product. According to the hosts, he refused to sign the apology letter. Only Tim Cook signed. Forstall was fired. Ben notes there's a certain Steve-like logic to the whole episode: picking a thermonuclear fight with Android and accepting some fallout. But the fallout landed on Forstall.

Ben also points out that Apple's two-sided story was familiar. Yes, we really messed this up, and we're sorry. But also, maps data takes time to improve, and Apple Maps will only get better. It's the same line Apple used for Siri, and it did turn out true: Apple Maps has gotten dramatically better since. Still, in that fall of twenty twelve, the damage was done, and Waze was sitting right there in the apology letter.

Meanwhile, remember the backdrop. Turn-by-turn navigation on the iPhone was a paid feature. TomTom and Garmin sold apps for fifty to a hundred dollars. The built-in Maps app only gave a list of directions, with no voice. Google had shipped turn-by-turn in Google Maps on Android, but not on iOS. People speculated Google was withholding it. Waze, meanwhile, was free, and pretty good.

So by October of twenty twelve, rumors swirled that Apple was looking at buying Waze, for around five hundred million dollars. Apple publicly denied it, though David jokes that Apple denies everything. Whatever happened, it didn't come together.

Then in December of twenty twelve, Google launched a standalone Google Maps app for iOS, with turn-by-turn. Ben says it was written by a small team in Google's Kirkland office near Seattle. It got huge praise, in some ways better than the Android app. David thinks that moment was the beginning of the end of the platform wars. Apple and Google decided they could peacefully coexist. Google also realized it was getting valuable data and monetization from iOS.

It's worth remembering how odd that detour was. Google and Apple had long been symbiotic. Google did the services, Apple did the hardware and the native software, and Google's Eric Schmidt sat on Apple's board until Steve Jobs kicked him off after Android launched. Ben's theory is that Android pulled Google into a war with Apple that was a distraction. Apple never wanted Google's search ad business, and Google never wanted Apple's hardware profits, since Google doesn't make money on Android hardware sales. The Google Maps app on iOS ended that war. Each side saw that the other wasn't the enemy.

And Google's main enemy changed. Facebook had gone public right around the time of that developer conference, and it was the real threat to Google's business. Apple wasn't after Google's search ad revenue. Facebook was. Anyone who owns the front door to the internet controls where ad dollars go. That's why Google built Chrome, and why it cared so much about controlling the path to users.

Now to the deal. In the spring of twenty thirteen, rumors said Facebook would buy Waze, for about a billion dollars. This was in the press for weeks, and everybody thought it was done. Ben notes that many articles were titled something like, why is Waze worth a billion dollars, and they were written before Google was even in the story. Then the Facebook deal fell through. The hosts say we may never know exactly why, since Waze was private, with no filings or lawsuits to dig through. But comments from the Waze team suggest a key sticking point. Facebook wanted to move the whole company to Menlo Park in California. The team wanted to stay in Israel.

Out of the blue, in June of twenty thirteen, Google announced it was buying Waze for right around a billion dollars, somewhere between one point one and one point three billion. Most of it was cash, with some stock and other considerations. The core team would stay in Israel, while some Wazers in Palo Alto joined Google in California.

Why would Google pay that? Not for the business. Waze had a pretty immature ad business, with things like sponsored gas stations and location-based ads from companies like Safeway. David says this was not a business line acquisition. It wasn't a people deal, a technology deal, or a product deal. Google Maps already had good traffic data from passive reporting, and Waze still exists as its own brand, not as the replacement for Maps. So Ben and David created a new category for it on the spot: an asset buy. A data asset.

What was the asset? Real-time data, to improve Google Maps and feed Google's driverless car work, which was then only starting. Things like where traffic stops, user-reported accidents, police, and speed traps. There was also huge defensive value. Google did not want Facebook to have this. And, says Ben, Google bought a data-gathering machine. After the Apple Maps debacle, Waze was growing at a hundred thousand new users a day. The mechanics that get people to feed in fresh, high-fidelity data, over and over, were the real prize. That's also why Google has left it largely alone.

One more thought from Ben. When he first researched the deal, his early note was: why doesn't Google just build this itself? It's exactly what Google is good at. The answer is that the active reporting, the social layer where drivers tap to flag a police car or a crash, was a community that had to be grown, not just coded. Waze had invented the mechanics that got people to do it over and over. Some complain those mechanics are distracting while driving. But they reliably produced fresh, high fidelity information about the real world, and mapping data always needs updating.

Then, the tech themes. David's first: when you have a potentially valuable data asset, you can upend an industry by giving something away for free. Turn-by-turn apps cost fifty to a hundred dollars. Waze gave it away, because it would collect the data. David compares it to Instagram versus the three dollar Hipstamatic, LinkedIn making resumes free, Skype with phone calls, WhatsApp with texts. He quotes Jeff Bezos on Prime: you want it to be irresponsible not to be a member. Add network effects and the free product wins.

Ben's themes: first, bringing measurable online advertising into the real world. You see an ad in Waze, tap it, navigate there, and Waze knows you arrived. Nobody worries about privacy, because the whole point of the app is knowing where you are. Second, banner ads failed on mobile. Tiny screens killed them. What works, he argues, is native advertising tailored to a full-screen experience, like Instagram, Snapchat, and Pokémon Go, where businesses can pay to direct people to places in the real world. David calls the banner a faster horse, and Pokémon Go the car. Ben adds that it took ten years of mobile to figure out the right way. Oh, and AdMob sold for seven hundred fifty million dollars, so, quite an expensive horse.

David's second theme: innovation is global. He quotes Noam Bardin's blog post. Bardin describes growing up in Israel in the eighties and nineties with one channel of black and white television, and no American brands. Then cable, the internet, social platforms, and finally mobile flattened the world. Anywhere in Israel, you see iPhones and Android phones, running the same apps as San Francisco. On a family vacation with Waze's management, Bardin watched Israeli and American kids share apps without needing a shared language. Both hosts agree Silicon Valley still matters. Waze had a Palo Alto office and an Israeli one. As Ben puts it, you still need your finger on the pulse of the hub.

They also play with what-ifs. What if Facebook had bought it? Facebook has no transportation play, though Ben notes its giant internet drone project. What if Tesla had? Or Apple, which was widely reported to be working on a self-driving car, and had just put Bob Mansfield in charge? Israel is also strong in autonomous vehicle technology, thanks to companies like Mobileye. And Ben imagines car makers one day selling subscriptions to fleets, a Mercedes version of Uber.

Time for grades. Ben's grade: A minus. Waze won't pay for itself through ads. He predicts it's nowhere near paying back the purchase. But the data asset stayed strong. Uber drivers prefer it. In developing countries, it's used even more often, because the crowdsourced data is more accurate, and Google would not want those users' default experience out of its hands. Plus the autonomous car future. He holds back from an A only because the A's they'd given were wildly successful at ridiculous speed.

David says it's too early to tell, but for the defensive reasons and the value already realized, he agrees on an A minus. He sees Waze as the closing chapter of the mobile platform wars, as the industry shifts to machine learning first, with Google explicitly calling itself an ML first company. Autonomous vehicles are the first huge native ML application. Both hedge: an A minus with low confidence. It will either become an A, or it won't stay an A minus. David compares it with GM's billion dollar purchase of Cruise, which was still pre-product.

Quick carve outs. Ben recommended the podcast Song Exploder, in which artists break down their songs layer by layer. He loves the Weezer episode, where the songwriter tags lyrics in spreadsheets by syllable count and beat. David picked Simon Sinek's start with why talk, from TEDx Puget Sound, about the golden circle: start with why you do something, then how, then what.

The takeaway for an operator? Look at what's actually being bought. Sometimes it's not the revenue, or the code. It's the machine that keeps the data fresh, and a user experience people willingly keep feeding.
