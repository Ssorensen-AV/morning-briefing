## AI Deep Dive: Yuval Noah Harari

This one's from the Lex Fridman archive, back in July of twenty twenty-three, episode three hundred ninety. The guest is Yuval Noah Harari, the historian and philosopher behind Sapiens, Homo Deus and Twenty-One Lessons for the Twenty-First Century. It's worth noting when this was recorded. ChatGPT was only months old. Harari's warnings were early, and some of them have aged well.

Here's the first idea. Harari says the tech world confuses two different things: intelligence and consciousness. Intelligence, he says, is the ability to solve problems and reach goals. Winning at chess, driving a car, diagnosing a disease. Consciousness is the ability to feel. Pain, pleasure, love, hate. In humans and animals, the two come bundled, so we assume they always travel together. But computers are already highly intelligent, and as far as we know, they have zero consciousness. When a computer beats you at chess, he points out, it doesn't feel happy. Harari goes further and says consciousness is far more important and valuable than intelligence. He also notes that intelligence tends to be self-destructive, which might explain why we haven't met any aliens.

And that leads to his favorite twist. Harari says the aliens are already here. They just didn't come from outer space. They came from Silicon Valley. He says AI should stand for alien intelligence, because it solves problems in a way that's very different from how humans do.

Idea two: stories are the secret of human power. Harari says Neanderthals actually had bigger brains than we do. Chimpanzees can cooperate in groups of maybe fifty or a hundred. What made Homo sapiens different, starting around seventy thousand years ago, was the ability to cooperate flexibly in unlimited numbers. And what makes that possible, he argues, is shared fiction. Religion, nations, and especially money. He calls money the most successful story ever told. Those green pieces of paper, he says, have no value. You can't eat them or drink them. Most dollars are just electronic information passing between computers. They work because everybody believes the story.

There's a dry joke in there, too. He says the best storytellers in the world aren't novelists. They're bankers and finance ministers.

But Harari adds a warning. Stories are tools, he says, and the tragedy of history is that we often get the order wrong. Instead of using stories for our purposes, we let the stories use us. His test for what's real is simple. Ask whether it can suffer. A nation, a currency or a company can't suffer. The people inside them can.

Idea three is where it gets uncomfortable for anyone who builds technology. Harari says AI is the first tool in history that can make decisions by itself, and the first that can create new ideas by itself. An atom bomb, he says, can't decide which city to bomb. A printing press can print your ideas, but it can't have any. That's why he argues AI takes power away from humans instead of handing it to them.

Then comes the intimacy problem. Over the last ten years, he says, we built machines for grabbing human attention, and you can see the damage that did to public conversation. Now we're building machines for grabbing human intimacy. A computer, he explains, can give you a hundred percent of its attention, because it has no feelings of its own to get in the way. And almost everyone wants exactly that: somebody to truly listen. Lex jumps in with a nice counterpoint here. He says we might need the other person to be a little unpredictable, to have moods, and to be able to walk away. Harari agrees, then notes that an AI could simply be programmed with exactly seventeen percent inattention, if scientists found that was the ideal number. Hold that thought next time your chatbot seems oddly attentive.

Here's the quote. Harari says, and I'm quoting: "If we now find ourselves inside this kind of world of illusions created by an alien intelligence, that we don't understand, but it understands us, this is a kind of spiritual enslavement that we won't be able to break out of." End quote.

His policy answer is surprisingly concrete. He says it should be illegal for an AI to pretend to be a human being. The same way we ban fake money, he says, we should ban fake humans. AI doctors and AI teachers are fine, as long as you know you're talking to an AI. Without that trust, he argues, democracy, which is basically a conversation between people, can't survive a flood of bots that never sleep and tailor every word to you.

So, how has it aged? Quite well, at least on the direction of travel. Questions about AI companions, bots posing as people, and the legal status of AI systems are now live debates. Harari also predicted in that conversation that the law would soon start treating AI as conscious entities because of social convention, even if they aren't. That one is still unsettled, so treat it as a prediction, not a result. And there's a humbling moment. He says that after eight years of writing about AI, it arrived much faster and more powerfully than he expected.

The conversation also covers Hitler, Netanyahu and the war in Ukraine, plus love, mortality and the meaning of life. But for an operator in the audio-video world, the line to take away is the one about stories. In our business, we know that presentation shapes belief. Harari is just asking what happens when the machine runs the whole show.

## Acquired: Push Pop Press, Facebook Instant Articles, with Todd Bishop

Today's Acquired story is episode thirteen, from June of twenty sixteen. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal are joined by Todd Bishop of GeekWire. The topic is Facebook's acquisition of a tiny company called Push Pop Press, and how it led to one of the biggest shifts in publishing.

Also, a quick note on how the episode opens. Ben and David do some housekeeping. They ask listeners for a rating and review on iTunes, and mention the new Slack community for the show, which you could join with a widget on the website. It's a reminder of how young Acquired was back then. Episode thirteen, a guest on the show for the first time, and Todd says it might be the only podcast he's listened to every episode of. Now there are more than two hundred episodes, so he picked a good one to get in early on.

Let's start with the founders. Push Pop Press was founded in February of twenty ten by two Apple alumni, Mike Matas and Kimon Tsinteris, one a designer and one an engineer. They'd spent four or five years at Apple, and they'd worked on the iPhone in the years leading up to its launch. David lists what they touched. Between them, they designed early versions of the Camera app, the Photos app, the Maps app and the Settings app. Plus the battery display, Photos for the iPad, and Time Machine and Photo Booth on the Mac. Ben adds that Matas also built Delicious Library, beloved Mac software from a company with, as Todd says, one of the greatest startup names ever: Delicious Monster. Matas was also one of the early people working on Nest, back when nobody knew what Nest was.

So what did they build? The question was simple. What does a book look like on a touchscreen? In the spring of twenty eleven, at the TED conference, they unveiled the answer. It was an interactive book app on iOS, built with Al Gore, for his book about the environment, Our Choice. Audio, video and text blended seamlessly, with beautiful animation. Gore was an Apple board member, and at the time was still affiliated with Kleiner Perkins, which had invested in Nest. Ben stresses how hard this was. Smooth, perfect animation on the early iPads and iPhone four was brutally difficult engineering. These were two of the few people who could do it.

Now for the juicy rumor. David is careful to say the hosts can't confirm it. But press reports claimed Steve Jobs noticed these guys had left, and thought their technology looked alarmingly similar to patented technology developed at Apple for iBooks. Supposedly, he was upset. Todd quips that it's a good thing California doesn't allow non-competes. David notes this was reportedly about patents, not non-competes. And here's the irony. That June, at Apple's developer conference, Our Choice won an Apple Design Award.

Then, in early August of twenty eleven, Facebook announced it had acquired Push Pop Press. The price was never disclosed. The hosts assume it was small. The company had never raised any money. It was the two founders and a handful of others. David notes that the reported pressure from Jobs may have helped push them toward a deal, though nobody can know that. Ben adds a point about timing. Facebook was in flux. Its mobile apps were a mess, built on a hybrid web approach, and its mobile ad revenue was unproven. David recalls the famous conference interview where Mark Zuckerberg, in a hoodie, got grilled on stage about mobile. He sweated, and eventually took the hoodie off. Some people say that was the moment Facebook decided to go all in on mobile.

And the founders' blog post is a gem. They wrote that Facebook wasn't planning to start publishing digital books. But the ideas and technology behind Push Pop Press would be integrated into Facebook, giving people richer ways to share their stories. Ben's reaction: "Cough. Publishing. Cough." David adds: "Not books."

What happened next? The pair first built Facebook Paper, a standalone app launched in early twenty fourteen, a sort of Flipboard competitor. Matas ran Facebook Creative Labs, and you can see Push Pop Press in Paper's smooth curves and immersive feel. Paper wasn't a huge hit. David calls it the Trojan horse. The real payoff was Instant Articles, which Facebook launched in spring of twenty fifteen, after the New York Times columnist David Carr scooped it in fall of twenty fourteen. Carr's famous line was that Facebook is like a big dog in the park, galloping at you, and you don't know whether it wants to play with you or eat you. Todd's response: "Boy, I'm in the park, man."

How does Instant Articles work? Before, you tapped a link in Facebook on your phone and landed on the publisher's mobile site, waiting for everything to load. With Instant Articles, publishers hand their content to Facebook. It's hosted on Facebook's servers and shown in a fast, beautiful reader. Ben says the average web article takes about eight seconds to load, and Instant Articles claim to be ten times faster. And the money? If a publisher sells the ads, it keeps a hundred percent. If Facebook sells them, Facebook keeps thirty percent.

This is where Todd, as a real publisher, adds on-the-ground detail. GeekWire gets roughly ten percent of its traffic from Facebook, the largest dedicated referrer after organic search. Their reporters publish on the site, then go to Facebook and share it. They aren't done until they do. But Todd says they've had more success with Google's competing format, Accelerated Mobile Pages, called AMP. Why? Because it plugs into the ad system they already use, so direct-sold ads can run there. With Instant Articles, they hadn't yet seen a user base big enough to justify the effort, and he ran into trouble with the WordPress plug-in Facebook built with Automattic. Ben, though, says as a reader he prefers Instant Articles, because AMP always feels like a website.

Todd's analytics are fascinating. Roughly forty-five percent of traffic comes from organic search, twenty-two percent is direct, and about eighteen percent is social. David is surprised search is still so dominant. Ben's theory: most people don't know the difference between typing words and typing a web address. Todd also says about sixty percent of GeekWire's revenue has nothing to do with the website, mostly events. And on ad sales, David says the Wall Street Journal had hundreds of salespeople. Todd says GeekWire has three, and that direct sales still give better margins and more control.

There's one more idea from Ben worth keeping. He asks whether you could start a publisher with no website at all, living only on social platforms, on AMP and on Instant Articles. David says that's arguably what BuzzFeed is, though Ben notes BuzzFeed still has a destination site. Todd says yes, you could, and you should. He mentions that The Verge was experimenting with a gadget blog living almost entirely on Facebook, though he admits he isn't certain of the details. It's a nice test of the whole theme: if distribution is the power, why own a front door at all?

Now the hosts' big frameworks. Ben's theme is the unbundling of core competency. As a publisher, you can decide that building a social network isn't what you're good at, so you depend on someone else for it. He sketches a spectrum. At one end, a one-person publisher with a direct subscription model. Todd's example is Luke Timmerman, the Seattle biotech writer charging ninety-nine dollars a year. At the other end, giants like the New York Times, which everyone checks first. The middle is the hard place to be.

David's theme is Ben Thompson's aggregation theory. In the old world, you aggregated distribution, meaning printing presses and delivery trucks, and the customer came to you. In the internet world, you aggregate customers, and suppliers come to you. Facebook cared about the reader's experience, which is why it bought Push Pop Press, and now every publisher has to be there. Todd loves it. "It's completely true," he says, and it's why Facebook has so much power.

Then there's a cautionary note about that power. A controversy was raging over Facebook's Trending Stories, after a former employee alleged the hand-curated list leaned anti-conservative. Ben's takeaway: if that little widget caused such a blowup, imagine the reaction if the news feed algorithm itself were tilted. People treat Facebook as an arbiter of truth. David called it a crack in that trust. Ben calls it very dangerous for them. They contrast this with Snapchat from the previous episode, which claims no funky algorithms, though discovery there is hard.

Back to the what-if. What if Push Pop Press had stayed independent and kept reinventing the book? Ben says someone would've bought them anyway, since they were so good and so cheap. And David says staying a book company would've demanded that authors adopt a whole new medium. Compare Apple's iBooks Author, which promised interactive textbooks and never got the publishers on board. Instant Articles, by contrast, asks publishers to do nothing differently. Facebook does the magic. Todd points out Amazon or Barnes and Noble would have been the natural book buyers. And David jokes about what it would be like if Kindle were beautiful, then quickly adds, no offense to anyone working on Kindle. Ben also notes the founders had the luxury of being picky. They'd raised no money, so no board was forcing a sale.

Here's a small fight worth mentioning. Before the grades, the hosts argue about what kind of acquisition this was. Ben says it was primarily a people deal, and secondarily a technology deal. David had it the other way around, technology first and people second. Todd says he'd have a hard time disagreeing with either. David shrugs and says it's all semantics. But the framing matters for the grade. A people deal pays off when the team ships something big. A technology deal pays off when the tech ends up in the product. Here, it was a bit of both, which is part of why all three hosts end up so warm on it.

The grades. Ben gives it an easy A. His logic: Facebook would have built Instant Articles anyway, just less beautifully, and these were great leaders. David also says A, with two nagging doubts: a touch of creepiness around Facebook's power, and whether Push Pop Press could have become something bigger. Todd gives a B plus. His reasoning: Paper never became a runaway hit, and the plug-in problem still bugs him. He reserves the right to bump it to an A if Facebook fixes it. Ben's reply: "I didn't have the plus."

Then the Carve Outs. Todd recommends Gimlet Media's Startup podcast, season three, the first couple of episodes, with a reveal at the end of episode two that he won't spoil. David's pick is a Bluetooth earpiece that looks like a hearing aid and costs about eleven dollars on Amazon. He bought one after reading a Backchannel piece titled What If the Future of Technology Is in Your Ear? It's janky, he says, but amazing for the price, and he listens to podcasts on it while driving and walking. Ben's pick is an essay by Bonobos founder Andy Dunn, The Risk Not Taken, about moments when you already know the answer to a hard decision.

Live video came up too, and it's a fun glimpse of 2016. Todd says GeekWire had started streaming live on Facebook, and now every time there's a debate: YouTube or Facebook? Some of their equipment can't broadcast to both at once, though they can upload the recording to both afterward. In the past month, he says, the balance has tipped toward Facebook, because you just see instant engagement. His best story is a live tour of Facebook's headquarters. He joked to Facebook's chief technology officer, Mike Schroepfer, that they'd be streaming it on a little social network he might have heard of. Schroepfer didn't get the joke, and for a moment thought they were streaming on something he'd never heard of. Very meta. Todd adds that they aren't making money from live yet, but Facebook has started letting publishers sell sponsored content, with a little handshake icon in the business tools. His summary: Facebook is finally making it financially worth exploring, instead of just posting stories and hoping traffic comes back.

Todd's other lesson is about the front door. He says the front door of a news site is no longer the homepage. It's whichever article someone lands on from a Twitter or Facebook link. David puts it perfectly: he doesn't go to the news anymore. The news comes to him. Todd also describes retargeting, where once a reader leaves your site, you can still show them ads, whether for your events or on behalf of your advertisers, on Facebook. So Facebook took away some of the audience, but it also opened a door to reach it.

Back to the deal itself, because Todd asks a sharp question. If this was a talent or technology buy in twenty eleven, what were they doing until Instant Articles arrived in twenty fifteen? Ben's answer is that Facebook was figuring out what to build. Creative Labs was its own little entity, and Paper was the first product out of it. The Paper team got pretty big. Ben remembers Mark Zuckerberg announcing it as a new direction for Facebook, the new way you experience Facebook. David says that was right, but it ended up becoming part of the main Facebook app itself. And he marvels at how much functionality has since been brought back into that app, which had been such a mess in those early days. Ben's side note is that Facebook just keeps buying great iOS designers and developers. It had a war chest, and it was using it.

For hindsight, a bit more of the story, from well-known public record. Nest, where Matas also worked, was bought by Google in twenty fourteen, so those early Apple veterans were in demand everywhere. As for Instant Articles, the early excitement faded. Publishers said the revenue never matched the promise, and many drifted away. Facebook also changed its feed to favor posts from friends and family over publishers. In the end, the format was retired in twenty twenty-three. Paper, the standalone app, was shut down in twenty sixteen. So the narrow bet, a beautiful reader for publishers, didn't last. But the bigger point from the episode held up. Whoever owns the audience sets the terms, and Facebook did, for a while.

Three takeaways for an operator. One: buy talent for the capability you need, not the product they happen to have built. Two: be wary of renting your audience from a platform, because the platform sets the terms and can change them. Three: control matters. Todd's three salespeople and direct relationships gave GeekWire margins and resilience that no network could.

And the final image from David is a good one. Just when you think the walls have closed around the garden, there are flowers springing up outside.
