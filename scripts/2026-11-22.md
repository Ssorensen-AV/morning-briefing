## AI Deep Dive: Neri Oxman on Designing with Nature

This one's from the Lex archive, episode three ninety-four, back in September of twenty twenty-three. The guest is Neri Oxman. She's an architect, designer, scientist and artist. For years she led the Mediated Matter group at MIT, building things at the meeting point of computation, fabrication, materials science and synthetic biology. Now she's running a company called Oxman, which wants to change how we design and make products, working with nature rather than against it.

Here's the first big idea. She starts with a word you may not have heard. Anthropomass. It's everything humans have made, as opposed to biomass, everything that's alive. She credits a professor named Ron Milo, at the Weizmann Institute, for the definition. And she says twenty twenty was the crossover year. That's when the stuff we built, the plastics, the asphalt, the concrete, the cities, outweighed all the living things on the planet. She calls that a moment that made her course correct.

So her question is a bold one. What if we grew things instead of building them? Her slogan is, grow everything. She asks it almost like a test. If you could grow an iPhone, or grow a car, what would that world look like? Could a building actually heal nature, instead of just taking up space? Her goal is a world where you can't tell grown from made, and it doesn't matter.

Idea number two is how she gets there. Her lab calls it computational templating. Think of it as setting the stage so that nature and technology can, in her words, duet. Her most famous example is the Silk Pavilion, which ended up at the Museum of Modern Art in New York. It stood about six meters tall, and it was spun by silkworms. Not woven by machines, spun by worms. Robots shaped the heat and light around them, and the worms responded. Lex loved the term she uses for the living system doing the work. A hero organism. It might be a silkworm, a bee, or a bacterium engineered to make pigment.

Now the bacteria part is where it gets futuristic. Her new lab uses what she calls growth chambers, little capsules that work like ecology in a box. You control light, humidity and temperature from the top down, while tuning genes from the bottom up. In one capsule you might grow transparent wood for buildings. In another, a shoe. She even floated a time machine for flavor. Set the dials to Ohio, December thirty-first, nineteen eighty-one, and ask what tomatoes tasted like. Then dial forward and ask what food might taste like in the future. Dry humor note. That is the most ambitious use of a thermostat I've ever heard.

Idea number three is softer, and it's the part that stuck with me. Late in the conversation they talk about love and flaws. Lex reads a famous letter attributed to Einstein, written to his daughter, in which he says love is the universal force that governs all others. Neri's take is more down to earth. She says a flaw is like added surface area. Think of a brick with a rough face. The roughness gives the mortar something to grab, so you can build a home. Vulnerability, she says, works the same way. It's the glue that lets people connect. Lex called it a good line, and he's right.

There's one short quote worth keeping, from her advice for young people. She says, quote, gratitude and presence, appreciation are really the most important things in life. She talks about her grandmother's tiny garden, which felt enormous to her as a child, and about paying attention to a full moon. Her point is that most of us will see only a few dozen full moons in a lifetime, so we should actually look.

Why does it matter? Because this conversation is from before the current wave of generative AI took over every headline, and she was already talking about nature getting access to the cloud, and about large language models for biology. Some of it has aged well. The shift toward biology as a design material is very real. But treat the grand claims about growing a car with caution. In twenty twenty-three, she herself said the lab's big reveal was still ahead. Inspiring vision, still early evidence.

## Acquired: An Acquirer's View into M&A with Taylor Barada of Adobe

This is episode eighteen of the Acquired archive, from August of twenty sixteen. It's a special. Instead of dissecting one deal, hosts Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal use a guest as their vehicle. Taylor Barada was the head of corporate development, corporate strategy and strategic partnerships at Adobe. Before that he was at Zynga, and before that he ran corporate development at Yahoo. He's a Northwestern law and business grad who spent a couple of years at Bain. And here's the twist. He's the first guest on the show who used to be a professional athlete. He played pro soccer in England, and he describes himself back then as an independent, solo soccer entrepreneur, because there was no real American scene to join. So he's been negotiating his own deals since long before the day job.

The question the hosts open with is a good one. How do these acquisitions even begin? Taylor says it's far less dramatic than people think. It's not a big moment where someone announces the company is for sale. Most of the time, about eighty percent, it's relationships and strategy. Someone introduces you and says, you folks are in the same space, you should know each other. The other twenty percent are the calls where a company is shopping itself around, maybe because a rival expressed interest, or because it just wants the highest bidder. He takes those calls, but he says the bar is high.

He quotes a blog post from the investor Mark Suster, with a line David loves. Invest in lines, not dots. A single point in time is hard to judge. A string of conversations over a couple of years lets you see people say what they'll do and then do it. David points out how much this mirrors raising venture capital. You're always raising, even when you're not closing.

Taylor's advice to entrepreneurs is almost funny in its simplicity. Don't make the first meeting a pitch to be sold. He tells a story about a venture investor who tried to introduce a founder to Adobe, and the founder said, why would I take that meeting? I'm not trying to sell. Taylor says that misses the point. Use it like the old saying. When you want money, ask for advice. When you want advice, ask for money. Just get to know each other. And be realistic about partnerships. Every small company thinks a big company holds the keys to the kingdom. In his experience, partnerships take a long time to sign, a long time to mature, and the truly game-changing ones are few and far between.

Now, culture. This is Taylor's main theme, and he says it's uniquely important at Adobe. He says they've walked away from extremely large deals, north of a billion dollars, because the culture fit wasn't there. Part of it is Adobe's history. The company morphed from PostScript and printing tools into desktop publishing, then creative tools, and then into marketing. That last leap came through the Omniture acquisition. Taylor says Adobe doesn't care about, quote, winning the press release. Value comes from what you actually do together in the market afterward.

His favorite sign that a deal will work is subtle. First, the entrepreneur sees that Adobe can accelerate their vision. But the real green light is when the founder starts to embrace Adobe's bigger vision and says, I can expand that, I want in. He's seen it at Yahoo, where two founders he helped bring in, Mike Kerns of Citizen Sports and Adam Cahan of IntoNow, rose to become senior vice presidents of product under Marissa Mayer. Kerns founded Citizen Sports with Jeff Ma, who you may know from the Bringing Down the House blackjack story out of MIT. David adds that Kurt DelBene of Microsoft, a past guest, told a similar tale about Acompli. Its founder, Javier Soltero, ended up running all of Outlook.

Let's talk about the process, because this is the part operators like you will appreciate. Taylor says every deal is a snowflake, but repeat buyers like Adobe, Microsoft, Oracle and Facebook all have a playbook. It goes like this. First, an early meeting with the business unit that cares about the product. A demo, a first look at the numbers. Sometimes, if a founder is nervous, Taylor will take one call without an NDA, just to hear the story. Then comes the big milestone, the letter of intent, or term sheet. It almost always includes a no-shop clause, usually forty-five to sixty days, sometimes thirty. That's your cue to bring in a lawyer, who can tell you what's, quote, market.

After that the circle widens. The buyer comes in for at least a day, often two or three, of deep dives into product, go-to-market, financials, operations and technology. A data room gets built. Taylor says early-stage startups sometimes have messy houses. Maybe they used outside agencies and never had them sign agreements. It's rarely a deal killer, but it's something you work around. Then comes the definitive agreement, share purchase or asset purchase. Then the announcement. Some deals sign and close the same day. Others sign, announce, and close about thirty days later after conditions are met.

Now here's a section I think is the intellectual core of the episode. The hosts ask whether Adobe sorts deals into buckets, like product acquisitions, business acquisitions or acquihires. Taylor says they use that lingo, but they don't get hung up on it. His real framework comes from his Bain days, and a book called Profit from the Core. The authors analyzed growth initiatives at about two thousand companies. The idea is that your core business has five dimensions: the customer, the channel, the geography, the business model and the product. Change any one of them and you're one step adjacent to the core. Change more and the risk climbs fast. One step out, he says, is roughly a thirty to forty percent chance of success. Three or four steps out, you're down around ten percent.

That doesn't mean you never go far. Sometimes the biggest opportunities are several steps out. You just have to make sure they're worth the risk, or you drift into undisciplined diversification, where you do no better than a private equity firm or a holding company, and probably worse, because that's not how your business is set up.

Taylor's example is Omniture, and he's frank about it. Looking back, he calls it about a four-step adjacency. It was software as a service, a completely new product, sold through an enterprise sales motion Adobe didn't yet have in mature form, with a recurring-revenue model that the creative side hadn't adopted. It was a very risky, very big bet. But Omniture was the market leader with real momentum. And afterward, Adobe moved from the Creative Suite to the Creative Cloud. He says that business is now a two billion dollar plus operation, on the road to being much larger. So the lesson is not that you avoid risk, it's that you take it for the right reasons.

In practice, he says, tech and talent deals tend to sit in the core. A little core expansion, one step out, might mean buying something proven in the market but not yet at scale, and then scaling it through Adobe's channel. After Omniture, Adobe did a number of add-on deals to broaden the portfolio and sell through the same channel. And he makes an operator's point here that I like. It's extraordinarily hard, and underappreciated, to build a large enterprise sales force. It takes roughly a decade and a huge investment. So once you have one, adding products to each salesperson's bag is a big part of the strategy.

Then comes the what-would-have-happened-otherwise segment, and Ben asks what percentage of deals Adobe looks at actually happen. Taylor says it's very low, probably under ten percent. Adobe has historically done roughly four to ten or more deals a year. He thinks the strategic umbrella is big enough to do more, but they've intentionally focused on making the ones they do work. Bankers have actually come in asking how Adobe's deals keep working out, and his answer is the willingness to say no. That starts at the top, with CEO Shantanu Narayen, whom he describes as having founder-level passion after nineteen years at Adobe and nine as CEO. Adobe gets around a thousand inbound pitches a year, maybe two to five emails a day, asking if they'd like to check someone out. Mostly the answer is a quick, polite no, because they don't window shop. If they take a meeting, it's because they think it could be interesting.

Then a nice line about the whole business. Taylor jokes that if he ever wrote a memoir, it would echo the Lance Armstrong book. It's not about the deal. The deal itself is mechanical. He has a lot of respect for the bankers who love that world, and says their jobs are really hard, since they only get paid if things get done. But for him, M&A is a tool, and the real question is always what your strategy is.

Ben asks about the role of the business owner versus corp dev. Taylor says there's an executive sponsor and a business owner, and the second one is critical. Founders with venture connections often try to go straight to the CEO. Sometimes that's fine. Often it's counterproductive, because if you get in front of the big boss before the business owner and corp dev have framed why this matters, you might get a polite no and then an uphill battle. David calls the venture version of this partner shopping, and says nothing kills a deal faster. Taylor's rule is simple. Play it straight and open. Treat corp dev and the business owner as partners, not people to manage. This isn't enterprise software, where you pitch a widget and the IT group says yes. It's a subtle, collaborative dance. You're, in his words, selling your baby, and you want to know if the buyer will be a good steward.

He also reminds sellers that no one can, quote, make you do anything you don't want to do. And he notes that almost no misstep is unrecoverable if the deal truly makes business sense. He's never not done a deal, though some were harder to get over the line because someone reached the CEO too early.

One more detail on the people side. Taylor says the happiest outcomes are when founders are, as he puts it, unleashed. They may be product people at heart, and raising money was never the part they loved. Inside a bigger company they can pour all their energy into the product. That's why he asks, early, whether a founder is truly excited about the long haul. If not, that's okay, but be honest, because passion is very hard to fake over years.

Then he compares how company culture shapes the process. At Yahoo, the team spent time getting strategic alignment, so that a rumor like Google is looking at buying the target didn't drive the agenda. His response at the time, he says, was, so? Doing what your competitors are doing is not a strategy. Yahoo also had a reputation for being slow, so his team was transparent with entrepreneurs about every hoop. At Zynga, it was the opposite. He tells a great story about the founder, an incredibly aggressive and dynamic entrepreneur, hearing that some of the creative people had walked out the door of another company. He got off the phone, and it was like a movie. Find those guys. Two hours later Taylor had them on the phone. He asked, can you come to San Francisco tomorrow? They said no, are you crazy? He said, great, we'll be there at one o'clock. Not exactly a standard process, but it fit a company growing at that speed.

At Adobe, the strategy is built around big trends. Taylor names the big ones. First, mobile, and getting it to the point where it's a tailwind rather than a headwind. He points to Mary Meeker's yearly internet report, where the shift to mobile outpaces the trend line every single year. Second, unlocking network effects in business software by treating data and content as strategic assets, rather than just selling tools. Consumer platforms have done that for a decade, and he sees a hybrid emerging in business software, where customers keep their own data but blend it with network-level data from the provider.

Now, the part where Ben and David grade the deal. Since there's no single acquisition, they ask Taylor how Adobe judges success. His answer is that integration lives inside his team. He says that's critical, because otherwise people behave like they're just responsible for banging out the deal and tossing it over the wall. He sees the job as a growth job, where a deal is just one part. That shows up in small ways, like retention packages. Sometimes the founder says a certain person is critical, but once inside a bigger company, three other functions can fill that role, and a different person's importance goes up. So you reward the first at closing, and put more retention on the second.

And they measure. Adobe reports to the CEO, CFO and board every quarter for two years after each deal, against financial, product and employee retention metrics. They try to be honest, not all green. If something fails, they do a formal post-mortem. Taylor's analogy is a great one. Acquisitions aren't like NBA free-throw shooting, where you hit seventy, eighty, ninety percent. They're more like hall of fame baseball hitting. Around thirty percent is not bad. Adobe, he says, is dramatically higher, and he says they haven't had the hundreds of millions of dollars in complete write-downs that other companies have taken.

His final point is about people. Entrepreneurs who sell and watch their work wither inside a big company can be, quote, bitter. But when a deal works, the founder's legacy is enormous. David says the happiest day is closing the round, and the real work starts after. Taylor agrees. It's day one, not the end. And you can't fake passion.

In the follow-up, David and Ben note Instagram had just launched Stories, a nod to their earlier Instagram and Snapchat episodes. Ben thinks Facebook should absolutely be afraid of Snapchat's engagement, but notes Snapchat lacks Facebook's global reach. David thinks it could deserve its own episode.

Then the Carve Outs, which are my favorite part. Taylor's three book picks. Mindset by Carol Dweck, about growth versus fixed mindset. Ben admits he's routine-driven at home and flexible at work, so he's both. Taylor also likes the Tim Ferriss podcast. Shoe Dog by Phil Knight, the Nike founder's memoir, which Taylor says will make venture investors feel like they're doing God's work, since Knight spent almost ten years on a shoestring chasing bank loans. And Originals by Adam Grant, on creativity. David's pick is Simone Biles, who had just won the individual gold at the Olympics by an enormous margin. He says she makes Michael Jordan look like he's in the D League. Ben's is a ten-minute Vox video on the history of the Concorde, for the airplane nerds.

So what's the takeaway for an operator like you? Three things. One, deals are relationships first, and the process is the easy part. Two, the buyer who plans for integration before the deal closes tends to win. And three, success rates for acquisitions are lower than people think, so culture is the cheapest insurance you can buy.
