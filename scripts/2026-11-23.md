## The Neuron

Here's what The Neuron is flagging today.

First, OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, proposed that frontier labs agree on common limits. These are capability thresholds nobody crosses until monitoring and understanding milestones are met. OpenAI is publishing its own internal measurements first, using a score called perplexity, where lower means smarter. He says below zero point eight is where major cybersecurity and math abilities begin. It's only a proposal, but it's a leading lab asking for the brakes.

Second, Palo Alto Networks agreed to buy Applied Compute for four point five billion dollars in stock. Applied Compute helps companies customize and run open-weight models.

Third, OpenRouter removed three inference providers and pulled zero data retention eligibility from a hundred and twenty-five endpoints, after researchers found providers weren't honoring data policies. OpenRouter says its own systems weren't breached. If you route models through it, worth a look.

Fourth, a practical tip. A new test called SWE-CoWork found coding agents often say done when they aren't. Thirty-one percent of requirements they reported finished failed their tests. The fix is to put outcomes and acceptance checks in one place, make the agent list requirements before coding, and show how it verified each one.

And one more. Lean and Mathlib maintainers say they have full confidence in the Riemann hypothesis proof Anthropic's Levent Alpöge built with Claude, though one last check was still running.

## AI Deep Dive: Walter Isaacson

This one's from the Lex Fridman archive, back in September of twenty twenty-three. The guest is Walter Isaacson, biographer of Einstein, Steve Jobs, Leonardo da Vinci, Ben Franklin and Elon Musk.

The first idea is the misfit. Isaacson points out that Einstein was rejected by the Zurich Polytechnic, couldn't get a teaching job even after trying about fourteen high schools, and in nineteen oh five was a third-class patent examiner. That same year he wrote three papers that transformed physics. Isaacson thinks the patent office helped. Einstein wasn't an acolyte of some professor. He was a visual thinker, picturing what it would look like to ride alongside a beam of light, while working on devices to synchronize clocks.

The second idea is the price of intensity. Lex asks whether Steve Jobs had to be so cruel. Isaacson recalls asking Steve Wozniak that very question. Woz said he'd have been nicer to everyone, then paused and added that if he'd run Apple, they probably wouldn't have made the Macintosh or the iPhone. Isaacson notes that Jobs, Musk, Bezos and Gates all used the word stupid freely, and that Musk's father did too. Isaacson doesn't say it's required. He says there are kinder successful leaders, but that sometimes brutal honesty is necessary.

The third idea is Musk's algorithm: question every requirement, delete, and if you don't end up adding back about twenty percent of what you deleted, you didn't delete enough. Isaacson thinks Musk probably overdid the Twitter layoffs, which cut around eighty-five percent of staff, by about that twenty percent.

One short quote, from Isaacson on what we can learn: "I can be more curious. I can question every rule and regulation."

Why does it matter? Because it's a reminder that genius and bruising edges often travel together, and that the useful part to copy is the curiosity, not the cruelty. Three years on, the Twitter story has aged mixed. The service did keep running, as he predicted. Whether it thrived is another matter.

## Acquired: Jet

Today's Acquired episode is number nineteen of two hundred seventeen, from August of twenty sixteen. It's titled Jet, and it covers Walmart buying Jet dot com. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal recorded it just days after the deal landed, and it's a good one for anyone who's ever watched a big incumbent try to buy its way out of a corner.

Let's start with the deal. On August eighth, twenty sixteen, Walmart announced it would acquire Jet dot com for three point three billion dollars. That was three billion in cash, plus three hundred million in stock retention incentives for employees. Jet was only two years old and had been public for barely a year. And Jet's founder, Marc Lore, would keep running Jet as a standalone brand, and would also take over Walmart dot com.

But to understand the deal, you have to go back to Marc Lore's first act. David tells it with relish. Before Jet, Lore started a company called Quidsi in two thousand five. You may not know the name, but you probably know its main business: Diapers dot com. Great domain, says Ben. Killer, agrees David.

Here's the delicious bit. Lore got the idea while living in Seattle, where his daughter attended the same private school as Jeff Bezos's children. The two reportedly met at a school picnic and chatted about e-commerce. Neither knew how tangled their paths would become.

Diapers dot com grew, and Amazon noticed. Per the book The Everything Store, Amazon tried to clone the business and undercut it on price. Meanwhile, Walmart had also made a bid for Quidsi. According to the book, Bezos told his corporate development team that, under no circumstances, was Walmart to be allowed to buy it. In two thousand ten, Amazon bought Quidsi for five hundred forty-five million dollars.

Lore stayed on for a couple of years, then left in twenty thirteen. And he left with a chip on his shoulder. In an interview with The New York Times, he said that at Amazon, quote, it didn't matter how you treated people, end quote. You just paid enough that you didn't care if you burned them out, and then you got new people. David notes the irony. The hosts talk about Amazon's long-term thinking all the time, and Lore called it short-term.

So in the summer of twenty fourteen, Lore starts Jet, and the vision is to beat Amazon at its own game, on price. His thesis was that Amazon's core customer was upper-middle-class, and there was a huge middle class who'd trade convenience and selection for low prices. The model was Costco. A membership site, fifty dollars a year, half the cost of Prime. Like Costco, Jet would barely make money on products. The membership fees would be the profit. Lore said, quote, we're basically not making a dime on any of the transactions, end quote.

And Lore didn't start small. In July twenty fourteen, a full year before launch, he raised eighty million dollars from investors including NEA, Bain and others. In February twenty fifteen, still with no website and no sales, he raised another hundred forty million. That's two hundred twenty million dollars before selling a single item. Ben remembers the reaction. Everybody burned in the late nineties said, oh my God, the bubble is back. David's version: Pets dot com all over again.

Jet launched on July twenty-first, twenty fifteen, and it came with a bang. Big spending on advertising, subway ads all over New York, billboards across the country. And about three hundred fifty thousand people signed up for an early insider program that offered six months of free membership. Not bad for a site that hadn't launched.

The mechanics were clever. The goal was for everything on Jet to be ten to fifteen percent cheaper than anywhere else online, meaning Amazon. To get there, Jet built technology to change shopper behavior. The default on Amazon, says David, is to order one thing right now. Jet wanted bigger baskets. Hit a minimum for free shipping. And as you added items that sat in the same fulfillment center, so they could ship in one box, Jet dropped your prices. Use a debit card instead of a credit card and you got a discount, because Jet passed back half of the interchange fee. Waive your right to return an item and you got an extra discount. Ben's reaction: all of this requires a lot of technology and a ton of financial modeling.

So, did it work? Here's the twist. Just three months after launch, in October twenty fifteen, Jet dropped the membership fee. It made shopping free for everyone. They had killed their only profit engine. David says Jet had become a charity run for the benefit of the American consumer, echoing what analysts used to say about Amazon. Lore's explanation was that on some items, the data showed Jet only needed to discount four to five percent, not ten to fifteen. But as David points out, Amazon already runs on razor-thin margins. Knocking off another four or five percent and doing the fulfillment yourself looks suspect.

And yet the very next month, November twenty fifteen, Jet raised another three hundred fifty million dollars in a round led by Fidelity, at a one point four billion dollar valuation after the money went in. Why would an investor do that, after hearing the profit model was gone? Because the growth was incredible. In December twenty fifteen, just six months after launch, Jet said it had two million active customers and thirty-three million dollars of revenue in that month alone. By May twenty sixteen, monthly revenue had nearly tripled to ninety million dollars.

David and Ben think the investors' logic had another layer. This was a get-to-scale play. If Jet could reach scale, maybe it'd become a sustainable business. And if not, there were plenty of companies frightened of Amazon that would love to buy one of the only at-scale e-commerce businesses in the country.

Then came the tell. In July twenty sixteen, Lore told Fortune that e-commerce had never been a winner-take-all market. There would be a really large number two, three and four, he said, and Jet could be one of them. David's translation: he basically gave up on being number one.

Back to the deal terms for a moment, because there's a detail worth savoring. Lore doesn't just walk away rich. It's been reported that a huge portion of his payout, both the three hundred million dollars in stock incentives and a big part of the cash he'd earn up front, is tied to him staying at Walmart for five years. David says that's very long compared to the usual lockup in a tech acquisition. And Lore still owned a big stake in Jet, maybe a quarter or even a third, even after all that fundraising. Ben's read: you don't pay three point three billion dollars for a person, but they clearly wanted Marc Lore. The announcement itself makes a point of saying he'll run Walmart dot com.

That brings up the big question. Is there going to be a really large number two, three and four in American e-commerce, as Lore claimed? David finds it hard to imagine. Ben tried to research who is number two to Amazon, and the honest answer is that it depends on the category. A friend who used to work at Amazon told him that in electronics, Best Buy is still strong online, but still far behind. There's no big horizontal platform that's a clear number two.

Now to the hosts' framework. First, the question of what would have happened otherwise. Not a sale to Amazon. Jeff Bezos would never sell, and Lore would never work for him again. Maybe Target or a big retailer. Maybe Google, which had tinkered with Google Shopping Express. And Amazon was famously one of Google's biggest advertisers. David and Ben also mention that Amazon's smile program may have been partly about getting people to bookmark Amazon directly, so it wouldn't have to pay Google for traffic. But realistically, Jet was burning capital, and it couldn't count on public markets the way Amazon did in its early years. Someone was going to buy it.

Now the hosts' acquisition categories. Each deal gets sorted as people, technology, product, business line or asset. Ben's flowchart goes like this. He doesn't believe Walmart will run Jet dot com as its own thing forever. He expects them to run it standalone for a few years, take the learnings, and maybe even rebrand the whole Jet model as Walmart dot com. So he doesn't call it a business line buy. He calls it people and technology. Walmart has WalmartLabs, which built the mobile app, but no deep technology DNA. Buying a large group of people running a fast-growing business is a chance to tip the scales, and to make Walmart a place technologists actually want to go, the way they want to go to Amazon. David agrees, and adds that Jet is partly an asset buy too. The Walmart press release's second bullet touts Jet's growing base of urban and millennial customers. Who doesn't shop at Walmart? Urban and millennial customers. So there's an asset story, but the two biggest reasons are the people and the technology.

Second, why was Walmart a good home? David lists three reasons. One, money. Walmart has far more than Jet could ever raise. Two, the Walmart CEO, Doug McMillon, who by David's account had a, quote, man crush, end quote, on Lore, promised him carte blanche and all the resources he'd need. Three, the customer base. Jet's original idea was to serve price-conscious middle Americans, which is exactly Walmart's customer.

Ben adds that Jet and Walmart are not so natural a fit on the other end. The press release highlighted Jet's urban and millennial customers, which is exactly who does not shop at Walmart. Some commentators were already calling Walmart this generation's Sears.

There's also a lovely tangent about how big this market still is. David brings up a recent interview with Tim Cook, who was asked what comes after mobile, since iPhone growth had just slipped. Cook's answer was that mobile is the greatest market technology has ever seen, and it's still early. Smartphones will touch more corners of life, the way they became your taxi through Uber. David applies the same lens to Amazon. At the time, e-commerce was only about five percent of American retail. Their phrase for it is day one. Ben points out that Apple skipped a generation and lost the previous war before it nailed mobile, so he wonders who's sitting out this war and will arrive hungry enough to unseat Amazon.

And what about the price tag? David's view is that for Walmart, three point three billion dollars isn't a lot of money. Even if they both think Jet will ultimately lose to Amazon, it's a better shot than Walmart had on its own. Ben says you have to do an expected value calculation, and that it's binary. It's either company-saving, which both consider unlikely, or it doesn't work. And the far more likely outcome if Walmart had skipped Jet, he says, is that Walmart simply could not have competed with Amazon.

One more thread from the hosts. Ben recalls guest Taylor Barada from the week before, who argued the best acquisitions are the ones where the founder is excited to get their hands on the bigger company's assets. So what would Lore be excited about at Walmart? Money, first. Walmart can pour in far more than any private investor. Whether it will keep pouring is a tall order to hold them to, Ben admits, since nobody knows what promises were made. But if Walmart is committed, it's a great place to land.

Third, the tech theme. Ben sees a classic innovator's dilemma. For twenty years Walmart has watched Amazon grow into a behemoth. But to compete, Walmart would have to cannibalize its own business and build a super low margin one. How do you tell shareholders the next ten years will be rough because we're undercutting ourselves?

David leans on Ben Thompson's Aggregation Theory, the idea that on the internet, the best customer experience wins. In his view, Jet's logic was flawed. Lore bet that some customers care only about price. But Amazon's holy trinity is price, convenience and selection, and nobody wants less of any of them. David also calls Jet a faster horse. What will actually disrupt Amazon, he guesses, is drones, three-D printing or virtual reality, not another retailer. And Ben notes Amazon plays a different game altogether. It earns money on third-party sellers, fulfillment, and running websites on AWS. Jet was playing the plain retail model.

They also do a bit of expected value math. If Jet had even a one percent chance of becoming a trillion dollar business, that's worth ten billion, so three point three isn't crazy. David calls this the art of investing versus the science. And there's a people argument. Three point three billion dollars is an expensive acquihire, but who else could Walmart recruit to build Amazon inside Walmart? Nobody who's actually capable would take that job. Hiring Lore brings a world-class team too, even if, as Ben jokes, you're rearranging the deck chairs on the Titanic.

One last point on Lore's mindset. When you set the quotes side by side, you can watch his ambition shrink. In twenty fourteen, he talks about winning the American middle class away from Amazon. By the summer of twenty sixteen, he's saying there's room for a big number two. Weeks later, he's a Walmart executive. David sees that arc as the story's real heart: a founder who sized up the fight, and chose the best available exit.

Then the grades. Ben goes with a D. It's not an F, he says, because Walmart had to do something. David says C, because this is the best they could do. Then Ben teases that sounds as good as YouTube, and David relents to a C minus. Ben says their rating system is all slidey. Fair enough.

The episode wraps with a few extras. They discuss the new Star Wars trailer and the strong female lead. They also try a new section called Hot Takes, with thirty-second reactions to recent deals. Verizon buying Yahoo. Lyft reportedly turning down an offer from General Motors, which Ben says should have been a really low bid. Microsoft buying Seattle startup Beam, a Twitch-like streaming service. And Randstad buying Monster dot com for a little over four hundred million dollars, less than one-fiftieth of what LinkedIn fetched. The takeaway for David and Ben: network effects are a thing.

So what's the operator's lesson? First, price alone is a weak moat, especially against someone with a better cost structure. Second, a growth story can fund a lot of sins, until the day the story needs a buyer. And third, sometimes an acquisition isn't about the asset at all. It's about the people who know how to do what you can't.

How did it turn out? With hindsight, Walmart kept pushing e-commerce for years, and Lore's team ran Walmart dot com for some time. But the hosts' skepticism that Jet itself could beat Amazon was well placed. The Jet brand eventually faded from the picture. The bet was really on Lore.

That's the Jet story: a founder with a grudge, a lot of other people's money, and a giant retailer that needed a new playbook.
