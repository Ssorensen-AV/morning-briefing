## Acquired: NeXT, Live at the GeekWire Summit

Today's Acquired is episode twenty-three, from October of twenty sixteen. It's their first live show, recorded at the GeekWire Summit, and the subject is Apple's acquisition of NeXT. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal call it the hero story of Steve Jobs. It also happens to be the best acquisition they ever graded. Let's get into it.

We start in nineteen eighty-four. The personal computer has arrived, and Apple has the Macintosh. But computing has moved on to a new wave: the workstation. David admits he didn't know what that was until he looked it up. A workstation was a personal computer on steroids, defined by the three M's. One megabyte of memory, one megapixel of display, and one megaflop of computing performance. Some people added a fourth M, for megapenny, since they cost about ten thousand dollars each. Universities and big corporations used them for scientific work.

Apple had no workstation. Down the street, Sun Microsystems, born at Stanford, was the darling of tech. It went from founding to one billion dollars in revenue in six years.

Meanwhile Steve Jobs, no longer CEO, was put in charge of a new division called SuperMicro, combining the Mac and the Lisa. His secret project was codenamed the Big Mac. The goal was to take a workstation, get rid of the fourth M, and sell it for five hundred dollars. With the technology of the day, that was brutally hard.

It wasn't going well. In May of nineteen eighty-five, CEO John Sculley and board member Arthur Rock decided enough was enough. They stripped Steve of the division and made him a figurehead. His office moved to an empty building across the street. He called it Siberia.

That summer, Steve met Paul Berg, a Nobel laureate in chemistry at Stanford. Berg complained that the Sun workstations were so expensive that undergraduates couldn't get enough time on them to model DNA. That lit a fire. Steve saw the need: put powerful computers in students' hands without compromising on power. By the end of summer, he resigned from Apple to start a company to pursue it.

Here's where it gets spicy. On September thirteenth, he resigned and told the board he'd take several people from SuperMicro with him. Among them were Joanna Hoffman, Bud Tribble, George Crow, Rich Page, Susan Barnes, Susan Kare, and Dan'l Lewin. Two weeks later, Apple sued him. The charges were stealing trade secrets, and breach of fiduciary duty as chairman. That wrecked what was left of his relationship with Sculley.

Steve gave Newsweek a great line. He said it was hard to believe a two billion dollar company with forty-three hundred people couldn't compete with six people in blue jeans.

They settled in January of nineteen eighty-six. NeXT couldn't compete with Apple, and Apple got to review NeXT's products before release. That February, Steve sold all his Apple stock except one share, so he could still attend shareholder meetings. Very Steve.

He put seven million dollars of his own money into NeXT. Then he spent a hundred thousand of it right away on the famous designer Paul Rand. Rand went away, and came back with a hundred-page brochure and one idea. The name NeXT, all capitals except a lowercase e for education, and a logo tilted at exactly twenty-eight degrees. Ben points out the money was paid up front. Steve loved it.

Then the office. Not a scrappy co-working space. The most expensive real estate in Palo Alto, with a staircase designed by I. M. Pei. Later they moved to a complex in Redwood City, also designed by Pei, with Eames chairs and ten thousand dollar leather sofas.

He also tried a management experiment. NeXT wasn't a company, it was a community. Everyone could see everyone's salary. That wasn't very dramatic, because there were only two salaries. If you joined before nineteen eighty-six, you made seventy-five thousand dollars. After, fifty thousand. He was avoiding politics after his ouster. It didn't really work, and NeXT eventually changed it.

Everyone wanted to know what NeXT would build. Then Ross Perot, yes, that Ross Perot, who had founded two tech companies himself, invested twenty million dollars at a hundred twenty million dollar valuation. Remember, this was before they had a product. David jokes that they had a brand book and a very expensive logo, and nothing else but Steve Jobs.

They rented a factory in Fremont able to build a hundred fifty thousand machines a year. They announced a three thousand dollar price. Then they missed the date, and missed it again. Ben notes this is a scar modern Apple clearly learned from. Today, Apple announces an event a week before. Back then they announced products years early.

Finally, in late nineteen eighty-eight, NeXT held a gala at Symphony Hall in San Francisco. Three thousand people attended. Not a single person from Apple was let in. Ben calls that vengeful.

On stage was the NeXT computer, a one-foot cube of solid black magnesium. Of course it was black. A violinist from the symphony played a duet with the machine, to show off its digital signal processing and real audio. Ben's verdict: a very expensive tech demo with a lack of product-market fit. Everyone got a framed poster.

The specs were wild for the time. A twenty-five megahertz Motorola sixty-eight oh thirty processor. Eight to sixty-four megabytes of RAM, when the workstation benchmark was one. A seventeen inch megapixel grayscale display. And built-in Ethernet, because the machine was designed to be networked from the ground up.

But there was one design mistake. The machine had a two hundred fifty-six megabyte magneto-optical disk, and no hard drive and no floppy. So you couldn't move files off the machine, because the disk with the operating system was the thing you'd have to pull out. Steve's answer was the network, but nobody else was on one. David compares it to removing the headphone jack in nineteen eighty-eight. They quickly shipped a version with a hard drive and a floppy.

And the retail price? Ten thousand dollars. The same as every other workstation. So nobody bought them. NeXT sold only about fifty thousand units in total.

One more detail worth savoring. The pricing saga tells you a lot about NeXT's problem. Steve's original dream at Apple was a workstation for five hundred dollars. At NeXT it became three thousand dollars on paper, and ten thousand dollars at retail, exactly where the old workstations already sat. They'd built a beautiful machine for a market they were supposed to disrupt, then charged the incumbent price.

But here's the twist. The software was incredible. NeXTSTEP, the operating system, was at least five years ahead of everything else, maybe a decade. Ben explains why. At Xerox PARC in nineteen seventy-nine, Steve had been shown three things. The graphical user interface, which became the Mac. Object-oriented programming. And networking. Steve later admitted he was so blinded by the first that he didn't stick around for the other two. He joked, if he had only stayed for another twenty minutes. NeXT combined all three. Object-oriented programming, through Objective-C, let developers build far more complex programs, far faster. David says writing modern software without it would be like driving across the country in a golf cart.

And the proof is in what got built on it. Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web on a NeXT computer at CERN. John Carmack at id Software wrote Doom, Quake, and Wolfenstein three D on NeXT. David notes those were the first real three D games.

In nineteen ninety-three, NeXT cut its losses and stopped selling hardware. It became a software company. One product was WebObjects, one of the first internet application servers. Dell built its e-commerce site on it. And, David says, the iTunes Store was still running on WebObjects. Which explains a lot.

Meanwhile, Apple was getting creamed. Windows three and then Windows ninety-five were dominating. Apple had no modern operating system. It tried to build its own, two competing projects, codenamed Copland and Gershwin, and they failed. Ben compares it to Microsoft's Longhorn days. Operating systems are hard, he says, and organizations are even harder.

So Apple went shopping. There were two candidates. NeXT, and Be, the company started by Jean-Louis Gassée. Fun fact from David: Gassée had literally replaced Steve running the SuperMicro division, before leaving to start Be. Gassée wanted three hundred million dollars. Apple was willing to pay around a hundred twenty to a hundred twenty-five. They couldn't agree.

So just before Christmas of nineteen ninety-six, Apple bought NeXT for four hundred twenty-nine million dollars, which went to NeXT shareholders, including Ross Perot. And separately, one and a half million shares of Apple stock went to Steve Jobs. He came back as a consultant. The deal closed in February of nineteen ninety-seven.

How was it as an investment? David the venture capitalist says NeXT raised money from Steve, from Perot, and from Canon, another hundred to hundred thirty million. So it sold for more than it raised, but not by much. It was not a home run. Nobody got rich off NeXT. NeXT was even planning an IPO that year, and Michael Dell once said they should shut down the company and give the money back to shareholders.

It's worth saying what the near-miss looked like. If Apple had bought Be instead, or if the price talks with Gassée had gone differently, you'd be living in a different world. Ben's blunt view is that NeXT had a fighting chance on its own but probably wouldn't have done well in the long term, and that Apple likely wouldn't be in business at all. They were getting creamed in the market. Michael Dell's famous quip, that Apple should shut down and return the money to shareholders, wasn't a joke at the time. It was a reasonable forecast. And the lesson for any operator listening is that sometimes the asset you're buying isn't on the balance sheet. It's the person, the conviction, and the ability to rally an entire organization around one direction.

Now watch what happens next. At the keynotes, the contrast was striking. CEO Gil Amelio stood up and said it was important for Apple to court developers. Ben says you walk out thinking you didn't disagree with anything, but you weren't inspired. Then Steve came on for a few minutes, and the crowd went wild.

Over the July Fourth weekend of nineteen ninety-seven, Steve convinced the board that Amelio had to go. The next week they fired him. In September, Steve became interim CEO. He stayed interim for three years. And it took five years from the acquisition for Mac OS ten to ship, in twenty oh one. Amelio had said they'd ship it in nineteen ninety-seven. In the meantime, Steve cleaned house. At the Worldwide Developer Conference in July, a developer asked about OpenDoc, and Steve basically said they'd had to put a bullet in the head of many things. Focus, he said, is about saying no.

And look at what survived. NeXT had a dock, when nothing else did. Interface Builder is still how people build iPhone apps. Ben points out that when the iPhone launched, the selling point was that it runs OS ten. Blackberry people refused to believe the scroll performance. Then came iOS, the iPad, the watch, the TV. All built on the same Darwin kernel they got from NeXT. David sums it up. It's all NeXT.

Pause on that for a second, because it's the heart of the story. Steve Jobs was thrown out of his own company, spent a decade in the wilderness, built a computer that failed commercially, and still came out holding the single most valuable piece of technology Apple could have asked for. David calls it the hero story of Steve Jobs for exactly that reason. Initial success, a long exile, and then a return that makes Apple the most valuable company in the world. Ben adds that you can't script it. They've literally made three movies about it.

Now the Acquired scorecard. First, the category. Ben and David say it's a people acquisition. The CEO's own slide listing the reasons for buying NeXT ended with one slide that said Steve Jobs. It was the only thing on it. David says watching the two on stage together, Steve was a man among boys.

Their tech themes. Ben's theme is that it really is all about the people, and especially founder-leaders. When Steve told developers to bet on the new platform, people believed him. Ben also says it's really hard to win over a developer ecosystem, citing Windows Phone.

David has two themes. First, in technology you stand on the shoulders of giants. Snapchat only works because of deep, geeky building blocks that came first. Second, what made Steve Steve. Object-oriented programming and networking were all out there at Xerox PARC. But those were technology, not product. The scarce skill is taking a potential and turning it into something that just works and delights. He connects it to Instagram, which wasn't the first app to make photos look good, and to a VC's favorite question: what signal do you have that people actually want this?

Ben adds an example. Microsoft Research has huge talent, but it's famously hard to bring research into products. When Steve came back, he killed Apple's advanced technology lab and said everyone must work with the lens of the product they're shipping.

And now, the grade. Ben compares it to Google's Android acquisition, which saved about four billion dollars a year, and which they'd graded A plus. Apple, he says, does about two hundred fifty billion dollars in revenue now. Probably would have gone out of business without the deal. And the core of every Apple product is NeXT technology. David says Apple has done approximately a trillion dollars in revenue since Steve came back. A fake number, said Ben. David's football analogy: Apple was behind by thirty points in the fourth quarter, and Steve ran a play that scored a thousand-point touchdown. Gil Amelio, he says, is the George Lazenby of Apple CEOs. He completed the greatest acquisition of all time, and didn't see any of it coming. Better to be lucky than good. The grade: A plus. Ben says it's the best acquisition they've ever looked at, and probably ever will.

Quickly, the carve-outs. Ben recommends the Ezra Klein Show episode with Stewart Butterfield of Flickr and Slack. Slack grew by solving its own team's pain points, built on IRC, and with less ego because everyone was a user. David recommends The Verge's investigation of DJI, the drone maker. They run a university robotics competition in China, hire the winners, and now have over a thousand PhDs working on computer vision. The CEO, Frank Wang, says drones are only the first product. David said it felt like Steve's Xerox PARC moment.

And that's NeXT. The company that failed as a computer maker, and succeeded as the soul of Apple.
