## AI News

The AI Daily Brief's Sunday episode asks a simple question. What do AI-native companies actually do differently?

The host, NLW, draws on examples from Stripe, Shopify, Uber, Ramp, and others. He breaks it down into seven practices that turn AI agents into useful systems, rather than clever demos.

Here's the range of what those practices cover. They start with shared company context, so agents know what the business actually knows. They include work that gets triggered automatically, instead of waiting for someone to type a prompt. And they end with verification and human accountability. In other words, somebody still checks the work, and somebody still owns the result.

Then there's a twist. The episode argues that the "AI teammate" model may be giving way to something more effective. That's the idea of a fixed roster of digital coworkers, each with a name and a job title. The alternative is flexible groups of agents organized around the work itself.

For context, if you've ever staffed a live event, you already know the difference. You don't hire a permanent crew for every possible show. You assemble the right team for this load-in, then regroup for the next one. Same idea, applied to software.

That's all the show notes give us, so we'll leave the detail to the episode itself. If you're curious about the seven practices in order, it's worth a listen on a walk.

## AI Deep Dive: Jimmy Wales on Wikipedia

Now, from the Lex Fridman archive, episode three eighty-five, released in June of twenty twenty-three. The guest is Jimmy Wales, co-founder of Wikipedia.

Lex introduces Jimmy as the co-founder of one of the most impactful websites ever. And the story of how it started is a good one, because it began as a failure.

Before Wikipedia, there was Nupedia. Jimmy wanted a free encyclopedia, but he believed volunteers on the internet would be mocked unless the work was academic. So Nupedia had a seven-stage review process. Here's what happened. One of the first entries to survive that process went live, and within days the wider community spotted plagiarism. They had to pull it. So much for seven stages.

Jimmy tried to write an entry himself, a short biography of the economist Robert Merton. And he felt the dread of grad school all over again. His draft would be sent off to prestigious finance professors for critique. It wasn't fun. That's when he realized the model would never work.

Then came the wiki idea. Wikipedia launched as a side project, partly because the academics might hate it. And Jimmy says they got more done in two weeks than in almost two years of Nupedia. People could just jump in and type. He even tells a lovely story about spotting that Robert Aumann had just won the Nobel Prize in economics, and Wikipedia had no entry. Jimmy got to be the first to create it. It was a terrible article. But two days later, other people had made it better. That's the whole magic trick.

Idea one, then. Lower the barrier to starting, and let the crowd improve it.

Idea two is about how Wikipedia handles living people. Jimmy explains that a biography of a living person is a danger zone, because you can do real harm. He describes a rule of thumb about people notable for only one event, like a crime victim. They get mentioned, but they don't get a biography. He also explains undue weight. He once found a short biography of a small-town city council member that spent a whole paragraph on his son's DUI arrest. Jimmy removed it. He says a section titled controversies is poor practice, because it invites people to go find more.

And he adds something Lex clearly appreciates. Jimmy says that for him, the most important thing is community health. Will Wikipedia get things wrong sometimes? Of course. The real test is how people react when someone points it out. Defensiveness is the warning sign.

Idea three is where this archive episode gets interesting for us today. This was recorded in the early months of ChatGPT four, and Jimmy has a very practical story. He asked ChatGPT for examples of white athletes being compared to fast cars. It gave him three specific, plausible examples. He then googled every single quote, and none of them existed. His summary of the flaw was that it will make things up just to be amiable.

But he's optimistic too. He imagines an AI tool that reads an article and all its sources, and flags anything that doesn't match. It doesn't have to be perfect. If it comes back with ten suggestions and seven are decent, that's worth a volunteer's time. He also describes a weekend demo built by Wikipedia's head of machine learning. Ask a question, find relevant articles, then answer using only those articles. That grounding, he said, prevented the making stuff up.

Here's the one quote worth keeping. On the question of government pressure, Jimmy says, quote, "We've never bowed down to government pressure anywhere in the world, and we never will." End quote.

And on the business model, he explains why Wikipedia never ran ads. He thinks organizations follow the money in the long run. No ads means no clickbait, and no algorithm nudging you toward the pages that pay best. The first fundraiser, on Christmas Day in two thousand three, after servers crashed, aimed for twenty thousand dollars. It raised nearly thirty thousand within about three weeks.

So why does this matter? Because the question he raised in twenty twenty-three is the one we're living with now. If AI answers the question, where does the credit and the funding go? Jimmy's bet was that proper grounding and links would keep Wikipedia at the top. He said so himself. That's a bet worth watching.

How has it aged? His warning about confident, made-up citations has aged very well. It's still the thing to check first.

## Acquired: Writely, Google Docs

Now, on to Acquired. Today's pick is episode nine of two hundred seventeen, titled Writely, or Google Docs. It was released in March of twenty sixteen. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal are your hosts, and they open with a theme from the last episode, productivity software.

Let's set the scene, the way David does. It's the mid two thousands. Web two point oh is in full swing. A browser technology called Ajax has just arrived, and it lets websites update live, without reloading the whole page. A small group of technologists at Intuit decides to start a little crew to play with it.

They found a company called Upstartle. David loves that name, and Ben jokes that it sounds very bubble. They try a couple of projects, and then land on one they call Writely. It's collaborative document editing, in the cloud. David wonders whether they even called it the cloud back then. Ben says it seems so table stakes now. And he's right, but remember, this was a toy at the time.

The founders were Sam Schillace, Claudia Carpenter, and Steve Newman. They worked on it for a couple of years. It was in beta, with a few thousand users. And they hired just one other person, a former Intuit colleague named Jennifer Mazzon. Four people in total.

In March of two thousand six, Google announced it had acquired the company. The price was undisclosed, but rumored to be around ten million dollars. All four joined Google, and became product managers on what turned into Google Docs.

Here's a detail David points out. Writely was actually not the first. Google's first purchase in this area was a smaller New York company called 2Web Technologies, in two thousand five. They were working on a product called Excel2Web. Google Spreadsheets launched in Google Labs in June of two thousand six, just a few months after the Writely deal. Then, at the start of two thousand seven, Google Docs, built on Writely, went public.

Ben remembers how it looked at first. The page said Google Docs and Spreadsheets, one long name. It looked like someone took the spreadsheet view and dropped your documents into it, sortable by date. That was about all the integration there was.

Then Google bought two more companies in two thousand seven. Tonic Systems and Zenter. Both were building presentation software for the browser. Ben says Tonic was largely the back end, and Zenter largely the front end. Zenter was also an early Y Combinator company. By September of two thousand seven, Google launched presentations. And just like that, there was a full suite of Microsoft Office-style productivity software, in the browser.

David is impressed by the speed. Ben stresses that this all happened within about a year and a half. And the individual deals were all rumored at roughly ten million dollars each. Ben compares that to the last episode. Microsoft paid about half a billion dollars in total for Acompli, Wunderlist, and Sunrise. Google, by contrast, was inventing the market for cloud productivity on the cheap, because people weren't flocking to it yet. Nobody realized they needed real-time collaboration, where you can watch the other person's cursor move in your document. It was largely a toy.

Here's a moment Ben loves. When Google announced Docs and Spreadsheets, Eric Schmidt told a gigantic audience that Google was not competing with Microsoft, and that this was not a competitor to Office. Ben's tone says it all. David wonders aloud whether Kurt DelBene, the guest from the previous episode, was listening at the time.

Ben's read on the price tags is worth pausing on. These were super bare bones products, he says. All pretty inexpensive. The real asset was a small band of people who understood the browser better than almost anyone. That's why the numbers look so small beside Microsoft's half billion dollar shopping spree.

Now for a side note of history. In June of two thousand seven, the same year the suite came together, Dropbox was founded. David points out that for years people had whispered about a mythical Google G Drive. It was the so-called Dropbox killer. And it didn't launch until two thousand twelve. So David asks, how might history have changed if Google had bought Dropbox or Box at the same time? Ben's answer is, it'd be interesting.

Now to the categories. For acquisition category, Ben says this is technology and people. Everybody in that era was seeing what they could do with Ajax. Google Maps was a flashy demo. Companies like Etherpad were playing with collaborative editing. Ben says content editable was the hot new thing in browsers. Google was buying people who knew how to do it, and then building the real product in house.

David says he'd planned to call it a business line acquisition. But as they researched, they found five or so companies behind Docs, and none was far along. To him, that makes it a classic buy versus build decision. Google decided it wanted into productivity apps, and wanted them in the browser. It judged that buying would be faster. Ben agrees. By his math, spending about fifty million dollars shortcut the time to market, and put all that brain power in house right away.

David then adds a twist on the strategy. As the battleground evolved, productivity software went from Word, Excel, and PowerPoint on your PC, to a whole suite, including email and cloud services. Inside Google, he says, it's a mix. They bought the traditional productivity software. They bought the email piece, Gmail. And the cloud piece, they built in house.

Ben asks the big question. Should Google have gotten into productivity at all? Google, he says, is an advertising business. Search, AdWords, and clicks. It's the cash cow. And you either believe it stops being the cash cow someday, or you ask what a productivity suite adds to it.

David's view is that Google has long been looking for what's next. Ben notes that Google had just become Alphabet, with revenue reported as Google plus other bets. They're so fascinated by the next widget that they reorganized the whole company around it. David compares it to Microsoft, who spent years as the Windows company. Google has been the search company for eighteen years. In his words, Google is graduating from high school and going to college. Ben says that would have been a cute name for the episode.

David also mentions a recent deal. Google had just acquired a company called Bebop, founded by Diane Greene, the founder of VMware, who sat on Google's board. Bebop hadn't shipped anything. But Diane was taking over the entire cloud business at Google, including Google Apps. That, to David, shows how the definition of productivity was shifting.

Ben adds a theory. Maybe Docs was an early big bet, back when Google was thinking about bets at all. In two thousand six, they weren't doing self-driving cars, or balloons that deliver internet. Looking at Microsoft's productivity cash cow, Docs could have felt like a giant swing. Today, he says, he's not sure Google would go into the space if it started over.

David has a sharper version. He wonders whether Google looked at Microsoft's two legs, Windows and Office, and asked what its own version would be. Search was their operating system of the web. So what was productivity on the web? That's what Docs was.

Next, Ben makes the classic low-end disruption argument. The incumbent sells to enterprises that demand every last feature, even though any one person uses about five percent of Office, mostly the same five percent. Then Google arrives with something that looks like a toy, and gives it away for free. But people discover that real-time collaboration on the web matters more than all those old features. Ben says that three to five years ago, he'd have called this one of the most classic examples ever.

But he doesn't think it worked. He had braced for Office to fade. It didn't. David asks if Ben's own firm, Pioneer Square Labs, which started in twenty fifteen, uses Office. Ben says yes, partly because of BizSpark, a Microsoft program that gives startups software for free. And a couple of them would pay anyway, because legal documents get changed in Word and need perfect rendering. But anything he starts from scratch, a feature spec, a one pager, a welcome document, is Google Docs.

Ben then adds the numbers. About a year and a half earlier, a Bitglass report had Google Apps at sixteen percent of the market, double Microsoft's cloud offering at eight. But about six months before the episode, Office three sixty five had jumped ahead, twenty five percent to twenty three. And Ben speaks from experience. He interned at Microsoft on what was then the Word web app. It was a joke compared to Google Docs, he says. He wrote specs while studying how Google Docs did it, and asked whether they could do better. The answer was nope.

So what happened? Microsoft, Ben says, got its act together. Its native apps got excellent, through building and buying. Its cloud apps held their own. Real-time collaboration arrived. Their user experience is still below Google's, in Ben's opinion. But they could tell the story to the enterprise, and Google never won it out.

David lands the key point. Nobody would argue the cloud Word is beating Google Docs. But it doesn't matter. Ben calls it the wrong battleground. Microsoft still captures the majority of the value in productivity, and Google captures almost zero of the dollars. Meanwhile, Amazon is taking a tax on everyone.

Ben calls it a great case study in incentives. If he's ever stuck trying to understand a business, he asks what each party is incentivized to do. Microsoft is a productivity company, and needs to defend its castle. And it wasn't defending it against a productivity upstart. It was defending against an advertising company that saw this as an afterthought. Google had real advertising problems to solve, such as the move from desktop to mobile.

David has a fun way to put it. Think of Larry Page, Jeff Bezos, Steve Ballmer, and then Satya Nadella. How much of your mind does productivity take? Bezos, probably zero. Page, maybe ten percent. Ballmer and Nadella, ninety. Who has their back against the wall? That's who gives it their best shot.

They also look at the cloud picture. Ben notes that Amazon had just broken out AWS in its earnings, and it was already profitable on its own. Each of the big three has layers: infrastructure, platform, and software. But they started in different places. Amazon with infrastructure, Microsoft with software, Google with App Engine, a platform. Ben says App Engine locked you into Google's data storage and Python, while Amazon gave you just one step above running your own server. They made enormously different bets. David says Amazon won the first round, because everyone used AWS. Even Dropbox paid what he calls the Amazon tax. And he answers Ben's earlier question. No, Google doesn't need every layer. AWS never offered much email, and it's the far-and-away leader.

There's also a nice aside about Ben Thompson. David mentions a Stratechery article on the Amazon tax from the week before, and calls it fantastic. The point is that Dropbox, for years, ran on Amazon's servers and paid for it. Ben adds that Apple and Dropbox were both building their own cloud hardware that month, and that Google's cloud platform was making news too. The ground was shifting under productivity software, even as they spoke.

Then comes what David calls the what-would-have-happened-otherwise section. If Writely had stayed independent, would it have been a true low-end disruptor? Would it have gone into storage, like Box and Dropbox? They mention Steve Jobs calling Dropbox a feature, not a company. Ben jokes that Apple should buy Dropbox. David says there's history there. Ben imagines Dropbox buying Upstartle, creating its own OneDrive plus Word Online stack. David points out Dropbox did buy Mailbox and other companies. Ben says it's a company that isn't good at acquisitions. David says that's a future episode. Ben adds, all of Dropbox's bodies buried.

Finally, the grades. Ben goes first. He previously gave YouTube a C, calling it a money pit. Here, he gives Docs a C as well. Not because it loses money. He thinks the business is self-sustaining. It's because it's a distraction. In his view, it's expensive in opportunity cost, the cost of attention. Google was looking for a second huge business, like Windows and Office for Microsoft. Productivity became a decent, profitable business, but not a Google scale one. Meanwhile, the real issue was the shift to mobile.

David had planned to give it a B, because it didn't cost much, and it isn't a capital drain like YouTube. But Ben convinces him. For technology companies in a fog of war, the most important resource is focus. David says this is what they coach founders on constantly. Focus, focus, focus. He settles on a B minus. Ben says that's still more generous than him.

Then, the new segment. They call it the carve out, a thing from media, or a book, that they love. Credit to Slate's Gabfests for the inspiration, says David. Ben goes first, and picks The Ringer, Bill Simmons's new sports and culture site, which launched after ESPN shut down Grantland. He reads from the very first email, where Bill runs through the names he rejected. One sounded like a hedge fund. Another, a horse favored to win the Kentucky Derby. And, Ben says, one was too Ludacris. It's relatable writing, even if you're not a sports person.

David's carve out is the book Creativity, Inc., by Ed Catmull of Pixar. He calls it one of the best nonfiction books he's read in years. His favorite idea is that creating something great is always a struggle, and that you need the crisis to make something great. That's true of Pixar, and, he says, startups, which look up and to the right from outside, but are up and down every day on the inside.

One more thought from Ben, on why this matters for anyone running operations. He says that if you look at the whole story, nothing about the technology was the problem. Real-time collaboration really was better. Customers really did love it. What decided the outcome was who needed the win most. That's a useful lens the next time you size up a vendor, or a competitor, or even a partner.

So, here's the takeaway. Google spent about fifty million dollars and got a product used everywhere, and, in the hosts' view, a profit pool that mostly went to someone else. Ben and David's lesson is incentives and focus. Know who's fighting for their life, and where your own attention is going. Ben signs off in his usual way, with a plug for reviews on iTunes.
