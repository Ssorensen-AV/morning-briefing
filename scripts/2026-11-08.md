## AI Deep Dive: Kyle Vogt

This one's from the Lex archive, back in twenty nineteen. Lex Fridman sits down with Kyle Vogt, then the president and chief technology officer of Cruise Automation. Kyle co-founded two companies that each sold for about a billion dollars. Twitch, and Cruise. That's a short list of people.

Let's start with the kid. Kyle grew up in Kansas, and he was building robots for BattleBots at thirteen or fourteen. He taught himself by reading tens of thousands of forum posts. And he's refreshingly honest about the results. Quote, "We weren't very good at BattleBots. We lost all of our matches the first round." One robot had a polished stainless steel spike that shot out eight inches. It was, in his words, pretty ineffective, but it looked cool. The second had a titanium ax powered by liquid carbon dioxide, which had its own set of problems. A man after every engineer's heart.

Here's the moment that pointed him toward self-driving. He was a teenager on a ten-hour drive across western Kansas, heading to a competition, mind-numbingly bored. And he thought, a computer can do this. Find the yellow lane markers, steer the wheel. His first attempt ran on a Pentium machine, written in BASIC. He admits there was no chance of doing anything like modern deep learning. But the itch stuck.

At MIT, he joined a student team for the first DARPA Grand Challenge, mostly freshmen and sophomores, up against faculty-led teams. Then, on qualifying day, the one steering motor they'd bought died. The truck couldn't steer. Kyle later called that challenge one of the most effective uses of taxpayer money he'd seen, because a small prize kicked off the whole wave of autonomous vehicle work.

Then he left MIT in his junior year. A month-long trip to California, a one-way ticket, and he never went back. The company was Justin.tv, which started as a reality show about one guy, became a live video platform, and then became Twitch. There's a great story from launch night in March of two thousand seven. TechCrunch published early. The live-streaming backpack was glitchy, spraying video across three or four cellphone modems, and the audio was screeching every few minutes. Only two people wrote code. Kyle owned the video system, alone, with hours to fix it. He called it a character-defining moment, and said he looks back on it fondly.

Now, here's the idea I'd keep. In twenty thirteen, Kyle founded Cruise, and he gave himself three requirements. The success of the product had to depend on hard technology. It had to have a positive impact on society. And it had to be a big business. He also knew Twitch took about eight years to work, so he'd commit ten. That's a filter worth borrowing for any big decision.

The first plan was a retrofit kit for highway driving. It didn't survive. Kyle says they assumed three car models would cover most of San Francisco, then surveyed customers and found they'd need twenty or fifty. Retrofitting also raises liability questions if a manufacturer quietly updates the car. About a year in, they dropped it and went all-in on driverless. GM bought Cruise in twenty sixteen for about a billion dollars.

On working with General Motors, he describes a culture clash. At a car company, he says, you get rewarded for following process and hitting deadlines, because a plant shutdown burns millions. In Silicon Valley, you try crazy ideas, and ninety percent fail. Three years in, he calls learning to bridge the two one of Cruise's greatest assets.

And the technical heart of it? He says the hard part isn't any one scenario. Humans are excellent at edge cases, and computers are the opposite. So the work is, quote, "thousands of little things," a grind he calls the unsexy phase, going from prototype to production. He expected twenty nineteen to be the year they crossed into superhuman performance.

One more thread I liked: the psychology of the road. Kyle points out that Americans spend over an hour a day on social media, about thirty-six hundred seconds, yet people lay on the horn when another car costs them three seconds. And he notes that flooring the pedal at a green light barely helps. On a fifteen-minute trip, aggressive driving gets you there within about thirty seconds of a calm ride. He jokes that self-driving cars could lower the collective blood pressure of society by a couple of points. Lex adds that driving is the real-world version of being a troll, a little shielded bubble for letting off steam. Fair.

And a note on how people build things. Kyle says there's no reason autonomous cars should top out at human level. He sees no first-principles reason they couldn't be twenty times better, or more. That's the optimistic version of the story, and it's the reason a certain kind of engineer wakes up in the morning.

Why does it matter? Listen with hindsight. The ride-hailing economics he laid out, vehicle cost, vehicle lifetime, hours of operation, still define the business. His point that nothing earns money until the fleet is as good as humans proved right. But the timeline was optimistic. Cruise didn't launch a paid driverless service until twenty twenty-two, and it hit serious trouble in twenty twenty-three, which led General Motors to wind the program down. Kyle left in late twenty twenty-three. Still, the grind he described is the same grind facing every AI product today, and his closing advice holds up: real passion, great co-founders, and refusing to quit.

## Acquired: Instagram

A quick note before we start. This early Acquired episode, episode two of two hundred seventeen, has no published transcript. The show notes tell us Ben and David ask whether Facebook's twenty twelve purchase of Instagram was a success, what criteria define success in an acquisition, and what lessons carry over to future deals. So I'm going to tell you the story of the deal itself, from well-established public facts, and I won't put words in the hosts' mouths.

Let's start with a number. One billion dollars. In April of twenty twelve, Facebook agreed to pay about that much, in cash and stock, for a photo-sharing app with thirteen employees and no revenue. Not thirteen hundred. Thirteen. You could fit the whole company around one conference table, with room left for lunch.

Was it crazy? That's the question the whole deal turns on. And it's a better question today than it was then, because we now know how the story ends.

Let's go back to the beginning. The founders are Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger. Kevin grew up in Massachusetts, studied at Stanford, and worked at Google and then at a small startup called Nextstop. He loved photography, and he was a talented programmer who also had a feel for products. Mike was born in Brazil, also studied at Stanford, and focused on symbolic systems, a mix of computer science, psychology and design. He was the engineer's engineer. Together they'd become one of the great founding pairs of the era.

But they didn't start with Instagram. In early twenty ten, Kevin was building an app called Burbn. Yes, like the whiskey. It was a location check-in app, in the style of Foursquare, with a lot of features piled on. You could check in, make plans, earn points and post photos. It was cluttered. Kevin raised a small seed round, around half a million dollars, from investors including Baseline Ventures and Andreessen Horowitz, and then brought Mike on board.

Here's the twist, and it's one of the great pivots in startup history. The two of them looked at how people actually used Burbn. Almost nobody cared about check-ins or plans. People loved the photos. So they did something hard. They threw out nearly everything and kept just the photo part. They rebuilt the product around three things: take a photo, apply a filter to make it look good, and share it. That's it.

Why did that work? Think about the phones of twenty ten. The iPhone four had just launched, with a much better camera. But mobile photos were still often grainy, dull and awkward to share. Uploading meant several taps, slow networks and a mess of settings. Instagram attacked each problem. The filters made ordinary snapshots look like something from a vintage camera. The square format gave the photos a distinctive look and also kept them fast to upload. And the app was extremely fast. The founders obsessed over speed. Photos started uploading in the background while you were still typing the caption. A small trick, a big feeling of magic.

The name itself was a blend of instant camera and telegram. The idea was an instant telegram for pictures. Nice, right?

Instagram launched on the App Store on October sixth, twenty ten. Within hours the servers were straining. About twenty-five thousand people signed up on day one. In a week, a hundred thousand. In about two months, a million users. The founders were working in a tiny space, keeping the service alive with a very small team, while hosting on Amazon's cloud. Mike often gets credit for keeping the whole thing from falling over.

There were smart growth choices too. The app was tied to existing social networks, so people could post their photos to Twitter and Facebook and bring friends along. Every shared photo was a tiny advertisement for Instagram. And the founders gave early attention to celebrities and influential users. The tech world's early adopters spread it first, and then it escaped into the mainstream.

Now, money. Venture investors chased them. In early twenty eleven, Instagram raised a round of about seven million dollars, led by Benchmark, with others joining. By then the company had perhaps a couple of million users. Then in the spring of twenty twelve, it raised a much larger round, reportedly about fifty million dollars, at a valuation of around five hundred million dollars. Investors included Sequoia, Thrive Capital, Greylock and Benchmark. Remember that number, because it matters. A company with a dozen employees and no revenue was already valued at half a billion dollars, before Facebook ever got involved.

Let me add some color on the competition, because Instagram wasn't alone. In twenty ten, mobile photography was a real scrum. Hipstamatic had already made retro filters popular, though it charged for the app and its sharing was clunky. Other startups, like Path and Picplz, pursued photo sharing too. Twitter had photo features, and Facebook had its own mobile app. Why did Instagram pull away? Because it combined three things nobody else combined: great-looking results, a dead-simple flow, and a built-in social network. You didn't just make a nice photo. You instantly had somewhere to put it, and an audience to react.

That last piece, the feed, is easy to overlook. Once you follow people and see their photos in a stream, with likes and comments, you've created a habit loop. The photo gets made for the audience. The audience brings back the photographer. Every upload creates a reason for several other people to open the app. For an operator, that's a flywheel: more content brings more viewers, which brings more content.

There's one more detail from the early days worth passing along. The team stayed lean on purpose. Even after the big funding round, Instagram hired slowly. By the time the deal was announced, it had thirteen people, and most of them were engineers. That tiny size had a hidden benefit. Everyone could keep the whole product in their head. Decisions took minutes. And the infrastructure leaned heavily on cloud services and open-source tools instead of expensive custom systems. Small teams, simple stack, relentless focus. It's a recipe a lot of companies talk about and few follow.

Next, the business context. What was Facebook, in twenty twelve?

Facebook was racing toward its initial public offering. It had close to nine hundred million users. But there was a big worry in the air: mobile. People were moving from desktop computers to phones, and Facebook's mobile business was young. Its own photo product was not winning hearts. Photos were already the most popular thing on Facebook, and mobile was the future. If a new platform owned mobile photo sharing, it could become a rival for attention, for the social graph, and eventually for advertising.

Mark Zuckerberg understood that threat early. According to widely reported accounts, he'd been watching Instagram for a long time, and had talked with Kevin Systrom on and off. The conversations grew serious in early April. Facebook's board needed to hear about it. And the timing was getting tight, because Instagram had a huge stack of offers on the table. Twitter, by many reports, had shown interest. Venture investors would have been happy to fund it at a still higher valuation. Instagram had also just launched an Android app on April third, twenty twelve, and it was downloaded more than a million times in the first day. The growth rate was wild. Thirty million registered users, and climbing.

On the ninth of April, Zuckerberg and Systrom shook hands on a deal. Facebook announced it publicly that same day, and the number was startling: one billion dollars, a mix of cash and Facebook stock. Zuckerberg wrote that Instagram would stay independent, with Kevin running it, and that Facebook would work to grow it. That promise matters, and we'll come back to it.

Here's what makes the price so striking. Facebook had no proof that Instagram could ever make money. Zuckerberg was paying for users, for a tiny, brilliant team, and for a rival that would never get the chance to grow up. In the language of acquisitions, it was a defensive and an offensive play at once.

And the reaction? Mixed to harsh. Critics asked how a photo app with thirteen employees could be worth a billion dollars. Some said Facebook was throwing money around before its IPO. Others pointed to a stark comparison: Kodak, the century-old photography giant, had filed for bankruptcy in January of that year, with roughly a hundred and forty-five thousand employees at its peak. Instagram had thirteen. The new king of photography had a team the size of a startup lunch table.

Then came a twist, and any honest answer to "was it a success?" has to face it. Facebook went public in May of twenty twelve, at thirty-eight dollars per share, and the stock slid in the following weeks, falling to around twenty dollars by late summer. Because part of the Instagram price was paid in Facebook shares, the deal's value shrank. By the time it closed, in September of twenty twelve, the headline figure was closer to seven hundred fifteen million dollars. A billion-dollar headline, a smaller reality. It still looks like a bargain now, but at the time, it looked like a gamble that got more expensive and then cheaper at once.

There was also regulatory scrutiny. The deal had to clear antitrust review, including the Federal Trade Commission in the United States, and regulators approved it. Years later, that approval would come back to haunt Facebook, and I'll get to that.

Now let's talk about what happened afterward. Because this is the part where the story separates itself from nearly every other tech acquisition.

First, Facebook kept its promise. Instagram stayed independent for years, with its own brand, its own app, its own team and its own office culture. Kevin kept running it. Many acquirers kill the thing they buy by smothering it with process. Facebook, mostly, left it alone. It supplied the things a tiny team couldn't: servers, security, recruiting, sales muscle and, eventually, money.

Second, the growth was breathtaking. Around the time of the acquisition, Instagram had thirty million users. By late twenty thirteen, the number was well over a hundred million. In twenty fourteen, it passed three hundred million. By twenty sixteen, five hundred million. And in June of twenty eighteen, Instagram announced one billion monthly users. A thirty-fold increase, in six years.

Third, the product kept evolving. Video came to Instagram in twenty thirteen, fifteen seconds at first. Direct messaging followed. Advertising arrived that same year, starting small and then ramping, once Facebook's ad machinery was plugged in. And in twenty sixteen came Stories, a feature openly modeled on Snapchat. Zuckerberg and Facebook were not shy about copying. Snapchat had famously turned down a multi-billion-dollar offer from Facebook in twenty thirteen, and Stories was, in a way, Facebook's answer. It worked spectacularly. Stories surged past Snapchat's own usage. Later, Reels took on TikTok in the same fashion.

Fourth, the money. Instagram was reported to bring in a small amount of revenue in its early ad years, but by the end of the decade, analysts estimated it was generating many billions of dollars a year. By twenty twenty-one, widely reported estimates put Instagram's annual advertising revenue at tens of billions, and somewhere around a third of Meta's total business. I want to be careful here, because Meta doesn't break the number out neatly. But the scale is clear. A company bought for a billion dollars now generates that much in a matter of days.

Estimates of what Instagram would fetch if sold today range wildly, but they run in the hundreds of billions of dollars. Even the most conservative guess is a multiple of a hundred times the purchase price. For that reason, people often call it one of the best acquisitions in the history of business, right alongside the likes of Google buying YouTube, or Disney buying Pixar and Marvel.

So that's the good news. Now, the cautionary tale.

The founders left. In September of twenty eighteen, Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger resigned from Instagram. Public reporting pointed to tension with Facebook leadership over autonomy. The independence Zuckerberg had promised in twenty twelve had narrowed over time. Instagram gained more integration with Facebook. It lost its own growth team's freedom. The founders were reportedly frustrated about resources and direction. And, as often happens, the people who'd made the magic did not stay for the long haul. Kevin and Mike went on to build other things, including Artifact, a news app that later sold to Yahoo.

Then there's the antitrust angle. Remember that approval in twenty twelve? In December of twenty twenty, the Federal Trade Commission and a large group of state attorneys general sued Facebook. The core claim was that Facebook bought Instagram, and later WhatsApp, to neutralize competitors, a strategy described in the lawsuit with an unforgettable phrase: buy or bury. Internal emails from the period were quoted, in which Zuckerberg worried about Instagram as a competitive threat. The case dragged through the courts for years. In November of twenty twenty-five, a federal judge ruled in Meta's favor, finding that the government had not proved Meta held an illegal monopoly. But the fact that the question was litigated at all shows how a billion-dollar bet becomes a political issue once the winner gets large enough.

All right. Let's step back and ask the question the show asks. Was it a success? And what criteria define one?

Here are criteria any operator can use. And these are my framing, not the hosts' words.

One: did the buyer get what it paid for? Facebook wanted to neutralize a mobile rival and to secure a huge audience. It got both, and then some.

Two: was the price sensible relative to what the asset became? By any measure, yes. A purchase price of a billion dollars, which later shrank to about seven hundred fifteen million in practice, for an asset now worth a hundred times more. That's an extraordinary return.

Three: did the asset survive the transaction? Plenty of acquisitions fail here. The product gets neglected, the team leaves, the culture dies. Instagram's product flourished. Its founders eventually left, but only after six years, and only after the app had grown thirtyfold. That's a pretty good run.

Four: did the deal fit a strategy? Instagram fit Facebook's strategic need exactly: mobile, photos, younger users, and a second social network for the family of apps. It also let Facebook hedge against its own aging core product.

Five: what were the costs? The costs are real. Regulatory risk, a high-profile departure of the founders, and the ethical debates about social media's effect on teenagers that have followed Instagram in recent years. Those costs are hard to put in a spreadsheet, but they're part of the ledger.

It's also worth putting the price in context against other deals of the era. In twenty twelve, a billion dollars for a company without revenue looked unthinkable. Two years later, in twenty fourteen, Facebook paid roughly nineteen billion dollars for WhatsApp, a messaging app, which made the Instagram price look quaint. Google had paid one point six five billion dollars for YouTube back in two thousand six. Those deals share a pattern. A young platform with a huge, passionate audience and no clear business gets bought by a giant that can supply the business model. The buyer isn't purchasing profits. It's purchasing a position in the future.

That pattern raises an uncomfortable question for any founder or buyer. How do you value an asset that has users but no revenue? There's no tidy formula. Some argue you price the threat it poses. Some say you price the audience, user by user. Others say it's a call option on a new category. In Instagram's case, all three views gave a high number, and the real answer turned out to be higher still.

One more note on timing. April of twenty twelve was close to the last moment this deal could have happened at that price. A month later, Facebook was public, scrutinized by shareholders every quarter. Buying a thirteen-person, revenue-free company for a billion dollars after the IPO would have drawn very different questions. Zuckerberg, with firm control of the company and a long-term view, could act decisively before the window closed. That freedom is a lesson in itself: sometimes the right move is available only to someone who is willing to look foolish for a while.

Now for the lessons. Let me give you three that an operator could take to the bank.

Lesson one: speed beats price. Zuckerberg moved in days, not months. Instagram was growing so fast that waiting a quarter would have cost more, or the chance would have vanished entirely. When something extraordinary is growing exponentially, the price tomorrow is not the price today. In my world, and in yours, that's the difference between buying the system now and arguing over specifications until the vendor is gone.

Lesson two: buy the team and keep the product intact. Facebook resisted its instinct to fold Instagram into itself. For years, that restraint was the secret. The most valuable things in a young company are often fragile: the culture, the pace, the taste. Absorb them too fast, and you've bought an empty shell. Think of it like a well-tuned audio system. You don't rewire the whole rack the week you acquire it.

Lesson three: the real price of a deal is paid over time. Facebook's stock moved, the founders departed, regulators came calling a decade later. A deal isn't a moment. It's a relationship, with consequences that keep arriving. The sticker price is just the opening bid.

And here's a lesson from the founders, too. Kevin and Mike are proof that focus wins. Burbn had dozens of features and went nowhere. Instagram did one thing, and did it with obsessive speed and polish. They cut everything but the part users loved. How many products, how many projects, how many meetings, would survive that edit?

Let's tie this together with hindsight. In twenty twelve, the headline was that Facebook had overpaid. People joked about it. Some commentators called it a sign of a bubble. A decade and more later, the same headline reads as one of the great bargains in corporate history. Facebook paid for thirty million users and thirteen people. It got a platform that reached a billion, then more, shaped a generation's habits, reshaped marketing and made billions upon billions. At the same time, it triggered the very antitrust fight that Zuckerberg's critics had worried about. Both things are true.

So here's where I land, and I'll frame it clearly as my read, not the hosts'. By the criteria that matter to an acquirer, financial return, strategic fit, preserving the asset, this was a triumph. By the criteria that matter to society, it's more complicated, and still being argued. Great deals don't have to be simple. They have to be worth it.

If you want one image to carry through the day, it's this. A tiny team, a single clear idea, and a fast, beautiful product. A giant company that, for once, mostly had the good sense to stay out of the way. That's rare. That's why people still study it.
